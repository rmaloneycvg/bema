# Nahum — Diyn (The Other Side of the Mishpat Coin)

BEMA 53 · Session 2
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/2-53-nahum-diyn.md`
Book: Nahum (with Exodus 34:6-7 as the controlling cross-reference)
Hosts: Brent Billings (co-host) · Marty Solomon

> This episode juxtaposes Nahum against the previous episode's Jonah: *"Today we juxtapose the book of Jonah against the other Assyrian prophet to Israel, the book of Nahum. We'll take a look at the other side of the equation of how God deals with evil."* If Jonah's word is *potential* (God's unbelievable patience toward Assyria), Nahum's word is **diyn** — God's retributive discipline, the "three" in the 1000:3 ratio. Read against the Session 2 arc (the Prophets: the covenant partner's unfaithfulness and the God who refuses to quit), Nahum turns the lens outward onto the *oppressor*: Israel has fallen, is crushed under Assyrian rule, and cries out "God, do you not care?" — and Nahum answers "Yes." The same God who stayed his hand in Jonah finally acts, not to abandon the story but to protect the people he will not quit on.

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

1. **Cold open — the other side of the equation.** Brent frames the pairing with Jonah: *"We'll take a look at the other side of the equation of how God deals with evil."* Marty names Jonah's word — *potential* — and Assyria as *"a brutal oppressor. The injustice at their hands is something that history records with vigor."*
2. **The 1000:3 ratio.** Marty (who wrote the Moses chapter in *The Gospel of Being Human*): God's love and justice are *"not an equal balance in the story. It's 1000:3."* From the Name: *"I show love to a thousand generations... and I repay sin to the third generation."* The book of Nahum is about that three — *"it's not 1000:0. It's 1000:3."*
3. **Review — where we are in the prophets.** Amos and Hosea to Israel; Micah and 1 Isaiah to Judah — all *"warning God's people that they had lost the plot of the story."* Judah heeds (led by **Hezekiah**); Israel does not and falls to Assyria (**Sennacherib**). Jonah and Nahum are the Assyrian-period prophets sent to a now-defeated Israel.
4. **Mishpat vs. diyn.** Brent defines **mishpat** as *"restorative justice where you're trying to put things back together."* Marty introduces **diyn**: *"it's that retributive justice... Discipline is on its way."* Not the opposite of mishpat — *"the other side of the mishpat coin."*
5. **Reading Nahum 1 — a different tone than Jonah.** Verse 1 ("concerning Nineveh") then verse 2 ("jealous and avenging God"). Brent: *"it's a slightly different take."* Marty: *"You're starting to start to wonder why this wasn't a Veggie Tales episode."*
6. **The vantage point — written *to* Israel, *about* Nineveh.** *"This book isn't written to people like us."* To the oppressed, the alarming picture of God is *"a message of hope, of liberation, of justice."* The danger: reading *"from our place on top of the power pyramid"* and weaponizing it against enemies.
7. **The Assyrian brutality.** *"They were known in history for something called staking, where they would take their enemies and they would impale them on poles. They were known for flaying."* The cry "God, do you not care?" is answered: *"Yes, yes, God sees and God cares."*
8. **Chapter 1 → the Source A column.** Marty's running "false dichotomy" game (Source A = idolatry/morality; Source B = injustice/Empire). Chapter 1, *"especially verse 14,"* scores for idolatry.
9. **Reading Nahum 2 — "I am against you."** Marty wrestles the phrase: *"I'm the guy who always talks about: God is for all of us."* Resolution: God is against them *as oppressors*, not as people — the same thing God said about his own people. Chapter 2 leans Source B (injustice).
10. **Reading Nahum 3 — the tiebreaker.** *"a city of blood, full of lies, full of plunder... piles of dead, bodies without number."* The book defiantly resists the framework; by chapter's end it is clearly Source B (injustice).
11. **"Verocity" — hyperbole with teeth.** The hyperbolic language ("lift your skirts over your face," "pelt you with filth") is the prophet making the point *"with a verocity"* — Marty's accidental blend of veracity + ferocity (he meant **ferocity**). Heschel's **pathos**: *"God has feelings about that, and the prophet feels those."*
12. **Closing synthesis & the images review.** Marty's notes: Jonah explains God's patience but not the ongoing problem of evil; *"God is patient and patient and patient... and then God acts. This is the book of Nahum."* We hold Jonah in one hand and Nahum in the other — *"trust the story."* The images are reviewed (plumb line, garden, judge, vineyard, potential, **diyn**), closing with the Impact Campus Ministries / BEMA appeal to live well between Jonah and Nahum.

## Key Lessons

- **God is slow to anger, but not *never* to anger.** Marty: *"God is slow to anger, but he's not never to anger. It is 1000:3, it is not 1000:0. And these are things that greatly matter."* The "three" is real, and it is not a three you want to experience.
- **Diyn is discipline aimed at restoration, not raw punishment.** *"I love the word discipline because it's different than just pure wrath, just pure punishment. Discipline might have punishment involved, but the larger thing that God's doing is he's restoring."* Even retributive justice serves the bigger project of putting the world back together.
- **The mishpat coin is weighted toward grace.** Brent: *"It's a weighted coin... You flip it, it's not gonna be a fifty-fifty."* Marty: *"Mishpat's going to be driven by grace and mercy and forgiveness... But there is another side to that coin."*
- **To the oppressed, judgment on the oppressor is good news.** *"when you're under the boot of Nineveh oppression, this message is a message of hope, of liberation, of justice. You're wondering, does God care?"* The "alarming picture of God" is only alarming from the wrong seat.
- **Where you sit determines whether you read rightly.** *"we often read the Bible from our place on top of the power pyramid. What's dangerous is you then use prophecy like this against your enemies. And it's a complete misuse of a prophecy of hope."*
- **"I am against you" is covenant protection, not divine favoritism.** *"he's not against them as people, as children of God. He's against them as oppressors."* God has said the same thing to his own people when they became the anti-story.
- **The "three" shows up when a people becomes the anti-story.** *"when we become the anti-story, God's discipline, it's that three... it's that three that shows up, because we've become the anti-story."* Discipline is triggered by anti-shalom, whoever is practicing it.
- **God finally acts — "Enough is enough."** *"there comes a time where God announces their coming destruction. And God sends his message to the prophet Nahum: Enough is enough."* Nahum answers the problem Jonah leaves open.
- **We must hold Jonah and Nahum together.** *"we have to hold Jonah in one hand and Nahum in the other... God hears the cry of the hurting and promises that he's in the business of mishpat."* Neither patience nor discipline alone tells the truth about God.

## East/West Lens

*(Only the categories this episode actually engages, each cited.)*

- **Read from the vantage of the hearer, not the abstraction (community/context over systematic theology).** Marty insists on *"hearing this not in an abstract vacuum of systematic theology, hearing this as the people of Israel wanting to know if God cares about the oppression and the oppressor."* The text's meaning depends on who is listening and from where.
- **Hyperbole and juxtaposition as the native idiom (word-pictures over flat statement).** The 1000:3 ratio is *"supposed to be the hyperbole, the juxtaposition... It's not 1000:1000, it's not 500:500. It's 1000:3."* The prophet's violent language is read as rhetoric with *"teeth to it,"* not propositional data.
- **God who feels — the pathos of the prophet (relational over detached).** *"God has feelings. Remember Abraham Joshua Heschel talking about the pathos of the prophet. God has feelings about that, and the prophet feels those, and that's where the language comes from."*
- **Comfort holding two truths in tension (both/and over either/or).** The whole episode refuses to collapse Jonah and Nahum: *"we have to hold Jonah in one hand and Nahum in the other."* Patience and discipline are kept together, not resolved into one.
- **Hebrew words carry the theology (terms as lenses, not labels).** *mishpat* and *diyn* are distinguished carefully — *"it's not the opposite of mishpat. It's just another Hebrew word for justice."* The vocabulary itself is the teaching tool.

## Recurring Through-lines

*(Motifs genuinely present in this episode, each backed by a citation. No registry consulted or modified.)*

- **Trust the story.** The closing invitation: *"He invites us to trust in his wisdom and his timing. He invites us to trust the story."* Nahum asks the oppressed to trust that God's timing on evil is good even when it is slow.
- **The anti-story / anti-shalom.** *"as you are the anti-shalom to my shalom, I will put an end to this oppression and injustice, because I've got to put the world back together."* Becoming the anti-story is what summons God's discipline.
- **God refuses to quit on his partner.** Even Israel's fall is not abandonment: *"you'll be restored, but in this moment, as you fight against my purposes... I will put an end to this oppression."* The restorative aim persists through discipline.
- **The Name of God / Exodus 34 (1000:3).** The covenant self-revelation drives the whole episode: *"I am Adonai, Adonai, compassionate and gracious. I'm slow to anger... I show love to a thousand generations... and I repay sin to the third generation."*
- **Mishpat — restorative justice.** Carried forward from earlier prophets episodes: *"Mishpat is this like restorative justice where you're trying to put things back together and make things into what they should be."*
- **The warning to God's people (losing the plot).** *"they had lost the plot of the story. Two to Israel, two to Judah."* Nahum assumes that whole prophetic warning as backdrop.
- **God is for all humanity flourishing.** Marty's recurring conviction, held in tension here: *"God is for all of us. God is for all of humanity flourishing."* It is precisely this that makes "I am against you" theologically hard — and resolvable only as being against *oppression*.

## Terminology

- **diyn** (דין) — a Hebrew word for justice, *not* the dominant one: *"when you run into diyn, it is God saying: it's that retributive justice. It's God making a pronouncement of: Discipline is on its way."* The image for the Nahum episode.
- **mishpat** (משפט) — restorative justice: *"you're trying to put things back together and make things into what they should be"* (Brent). Driven by grace, but a *"weighted coin."* Diyn is *"another side to that coin,"* not its opposite.
- **the 1000:3 ratio** — from Exodus 34: *"a thousand generations who love and three generations who hate. It's not 1000:1000... Now, it's not 1000:0. It's 1000:3."* The book of Nahum is about the three.
- **discipline** — Marty's preferred word over "wrath/punishment": *"the larger thing that God's doing is he's restoring. He's bringing order to this destructive chaos."*
- **the power pyramid** — the posture that distorts the text: *"we often read the Bible from our place on top of the power pyramid... a complete misuse of a prophecy of hope to people that were on the bottom."*
- **the anti-story / anti-shalom** — *"as you are the anti-shalom to my shalom, I will put an end to this oppression."* What triggers the 1000:3 "three."
- **Source A / Source B** — the running interpretive game: Source A = *"idolatry and morality,"* Source B = *"injustice and Empire."* A deliberate *"false dichotomy"* to surface the sub-narratives readers assume.
- **pathos** (Heschel) — *"the pathos of the prophet. God has feelings about that, and the prophet feels those, and that's where the language comes from."*
- **"verocity"** — Marty's accidental coinage, *"the combination of veracity and ferocity"* (he meant **ferocity**) — the prophet making a point with teeth.
- **staking / flaying** — the historical Assyrian atrocities framing Israel's cry: *"they would take their enemies and they would impale them on poles... They were known for flaying."*

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty says we must *"hold Jonah in one hand and Nahum in the other."* Which hand do you naturally grip tighter — the patient God or the God who finally acts — and what does that reveal about your picture of God?
2. The 1000:3 ratio insists God's justice is *"not 1000:0."* Where in your life or world do you most need the hope that God will not let entrenched evil run forever — and where do you most need his patience instead?
3. Marty warns against reading the Bible *"from our place on top of the power pyramid."* Name a passage you've heard used against someone. How would it read differently from the vantage of the oppressed it was written to?
4. God says "I am against you" to the *oppressor*, not the *person*. Where have you confused being against someone's harmful behavior with being against them — in either direction?
5. The prophet speaks with "ferocity" because, as Heschel says, *"God has feelings."* Does a God with strong feelings about injustice comfort or unsettle you? Why?
6. Marty describes becoming *"the anti-story, the anti-shalom to my shalom."* Where might you (or your community) be on the wrong side of the mishpat coin, inviting discipline rather than restoration?
7. In the Session 2 arc, the covenant partner has been unfaithful and fallen — yet God refuses to quit, disciplining the oppressor *so that* he can *"put the world back together."* Where do you need to trust God's *"wisdom and his timing"* with an injustice he has not yet addressed?

# BEMA 36: The Redemption Cycle — Study Guide

**Session 2 (The Prophets) · Book of Judges**
Primary texts: [Judges 2:10–19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%202%3A10-19&version=NIV), [Judges 3:7–15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%203%3A7-15&version=NIV), [Judges 4:1–5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%204%3A1-5&version=NIV), [Judges 6:1–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%206%3A1-10&version=NIV)
Hosts: Marty Solomon (host), Brent Billings (co-host)

## Overview

This episode re-reads the famous cycle in the book of Judges. Nearly every image search and Bible-college lesson calls it the "sin cycle," framing the book around human failure. Marty argues the same undeniable cycle is better named **God's redemption cycle**: sin occupies only two-sixths of it, while God's rescue, deliverance, and rest occupy four-sixths. The book is therefore read not as a chronicle of continual failure but of continual forgiveness — a testament to God's endless patience.

The guide tracks how the episode advances the one story. Session 1 established the **partnership**: God takes a people and plants them at the crossroads of the earth to bless all nations. Session 2 (the Prophets) now watches the partner — Israel — be chronically unfaithful, and watches God refuse to quit. BEMA 36 is the opening portrait of that stage: a people who repeatedly abandon the covenant, and a God who keeps raising up deliverers rather than walking away.

## East / West Lens

Only the interpretive categories the hosts actually engage are tagged below; each is cited.

- **Framing / naming (East vs. West reading of the same data)** — The West instinctively titles the cycle by human sin ("Genesis 3 kind of way of seeing this cycle"); the hosts re-title the identical data around God's goodness. Marty: *"Why isn't it about God's patience? Why is it about our badness and not God's goodness? Put the same cycle up there, just change the title and it changes all kinds of stuff."*
- **Both/and over either/or** — Two tellings of one history can both be true. Marty: *"there would be a beautiful place for you to poetically tell your story as a story of victory... And then there could also be a place where it's like... tell the long version of the story. There's a time and a place for both of those to be exactly right, exactly authoritative, exactly inspired."*
- **Character of God read narratively, not transactionally** — God's "patience running out" is reframed as covenant commitment, not depleted tolerance. Marty: *"It's not His patience running out. It's something totally different."*
- **Historical solidarity (communal, Hebraic reading) over individualized self-condemnation** — Marty: *"do we see these stories as an invitation to some sense of historical solidarity? Do we read these stories and just go: oh, those silly Israelites?... Or do we see in these stories — like, oh man, this is the human experience, and God is redemptive."*

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **Setup: one slide, one image search.** The hosts note that searching "Judges sin cycle" autofills instantly and returns endless graphics. Brent: *"There are definitely a lot of sin cycles, but there's also a decent number of just — they don't name it anything else."*
2. **Defining the cycle.** Marty lays out a six-part cycle: things go well → fall into sin → come under judgment → cry out for deliverance → God sends a deliverer → rescue/rest → back to the top. *"you can define the cycle in four parts, six parts, ten parts. It varies. But I'll talk about a six-part cycle."*
3. **The thesis: sin cycle vs. redemption cycle.** *"It only represents half the cycle... You could just as easily, in my mind, call it the redemption cycle... it occupies more of the cycle, four-sixths... rather than the two-sixths that sin does."*
4. **Endless patience.** *"God has endless patience, endless, endless patience"* — later illustrated with Red Robin's bottomless fries.
5. **Joshua vs. Judges as two framings of one period.** *"Joshua tells it as a story of conquest... Judges is actually the more historical account."* Brent affirms it's "very believable."
6. **Reading the cycle in the text — Judges 2.** Brent reads [Judges 2:10–19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%202%3A10-19&version=NIV); Marty: *"that's ten verses that basically described step by step... the exact cycle."*
7. **Judges 3 — Othniel.** Brent reads [Judges 3:7–15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%203%3A7-15&version=NIV): *"the land had peace for forty years."*
8. **Brent's observation — how much of the cycle is good.** *"The land had peace for 40 years... God was with them. They were following God for a long time."*
9. **Judges 4 — Deborah.** Brent reads [Judges 4:1–5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%204%3A1-5&version=NIV); *"Deborah's gonna have quite a little story and she's gonna have a song."*
10. **Judges 6 — Midian and the prophet.** Brent reads [Judges 6:1–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%206%3A1-10&version=NIV), where God sends a prophet to recount the Exodus rescue.
11. **"They forgot" — a willful ignoring, not amnesia.** Brent: *"they didn't forget forget. This is a willful, like ignore God for a while."*
12. **Skimming the roster of judges.** Othniel, Shamgar, Ehud, Gideon/Deborah, Tola, Yair, Jephthah, Samson — a rhythm of short, short, longer, long stories. (Hall-pass to Elle Grover Fricks on Jephthah.)
13. **The Samson resources.** Rob Bell's *Forgiving Flow* part 2 ("Drop the Jawbone"); Brad Gray's *Make Your Mark*.
14. **The end of Judges: the anti-story.** The Levite and his concubine as a retelling of Sodom and Gomorrah — *"They have become Sodom and Gomorrah... maybe this is a sin cycle."*
15. **What rouses God to action.** Not lost patience, but Israel *becoming the anti-story* and harming the vulnerable: *"that is what rouses God to action... because He's completely committed to His redemptive arc."*
16. **Chiasms and the missing rest.** Marty notes 40/80/40/40/6-year rests and a missing expected rest after Samson, and the book's "master chiasm."
17. **Exodus 34 — a thousand to three.** *"it's not a thousand to zero. It's a thousand to three."*
18. **Closing reflection.** Historical solidarity and continual forgiveness; Brent: *"And that's how we bless all nations."*

**Coverage verification:** All four read passages (Judges 2, 3, 4, 6) are covered. The thesis, the patience theme, the Joshua/Judges framing, the "forgot = willful" point, the judges roster, the anti-story, the chiasm discussion, and the Exodus 34 lens are all represented. Strays (resource recommendations, the Danites/Laish callback) are parked in **Other Talking Points** below.

## Key Lessons

Framed against the Session 2 arc — the partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **The same data, re-titled, re-forms the whole.** The cycle is real and undeniable, but naming it "sin" vs. "redemption" determines what the reader believes the book is *about*. Marty: *"you can see our badness in the cycle, or you can see God's ridiculous, scandalous, illogical, wasteful patience."* **Arc link:** where Session 1 asked whether the partner would say yes, Session 2 shows the partner failing — and insists the headline is still God's faithfulness, not Israel's failure.

2. **God's patience is covenantal, not transactional.** The repeated rescue is not God being used; it is God's grace. Marty: *"Why do you keep forgiving them? They keep screwing up. Because that's God's grace. That's God's patience. That's God's redemption."* **Arc link:** the partner is unfaithful and God refuses to quit — the defining movement of the Prophets session.

3. **Trying-and-failing is covered; becoming the anti-story is different.** Marty: *"It's one thing to try and follow God and fail... God's got endless patience for that... It is another thing to become the anti-story."* The end of Judges — a people abusing visitors at the crossroads meant to bless the nations — is the inversion of the mission. **Arc link:** this plants the category ("the anti-story") the session will develop; God is roused *because* He is committed to the redemptive arc, not because patience expired.

4. **Engagement produces mistakes — and that is the point.** Brent: *"If you're not out there engaging shephelah enough that you are making mistakes, you're probably not digging in enough... God will redeem that."* **Arc link:** the partnership's calling (engage the crossroads) necessarily runs through failure; God's redemption is what makes the calling survivable.

5. **Read the story as solidarity, not superiority.** Marty: *"this is the human experience, and God is redemptive... It's not a book of continual failure, but a book of continual forgiveness."* Brent ties it back to the mission: *"And that's how we bless all nations."*

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension: the surfaced problem, a classification tag, the cited quote, a verse link, and a Hebraic resolution (or "Open — dance with it").

- **Why is it universally called the "sin cycle"?** *(Interpretive-framing tension.)* Marty: *"How come none of them... nobody calls it the Redemption Cycle. They're all about our sin and our failure... How come?"* Text: [Judges 2:16–18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%202%3A16-18&version=NIV). **Hebraic resolution:** the raising-up and relenting of God occupies most of the cycle; the Western default foregrounds human sin, but the text's own repetition foregrounds divine rescue.

- **How can a people "forget" God so soon after the Exodus?** *(Logic / plausibility tension.)* Brent: *"how is that possible? It wasn't that long ago — were they not telling the stories? And then how did they know to cry out to the Lord?"* Text: [Judges 2:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%202%3A10&version=NIV). **Hebraic resolution:** Brent and Marty resolve it as *willful* ignoring, not literal amnesia — *"they didn't forget forget. This is a willful, like ignore God for a while"* — which makes God's continued forgiveness more scandalous, not less.

- **Joshua (conquest) vs. Judges (assimilation): which happened?** *(Translation / source-framing tension.)* Marty: *"both Joshua and Judges are describing the same period of history, but Joshua tells it as a story of conquest... and Judges is actually the more historical account."* Text: [Judges 1:27–28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%201%3A27-28&version=NIV). **Open — dance with it.** Marty explicitly declines to require the position: *"I'm not telling you have to love that take, or that's even how I view it."* Both tellings can be "exactly right, authoritative, inspired."

- **Does God's patience "run out" when He finally acts in judgment?** *(Character-of-God tension.)* Marty: *"It's not His patience running out. It's something totally different."* Text: [Exodus 34:6–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2034%3A6-7&version=NIV). **Hebraic resolution:** "a thousand to three," not "a thousand to zero" — God's action against the anti-story flows from commitment to redemption, specifically protecting the abused and vulnerable.

- **Why does triumphant Samson lack the expected rest?** *(Open structural question.)* Marty: *"I should have 40, but I don't have 20... Did it actually take away from the kind of rest that God's people were able to experience[?]"* Text: [Judges 15:20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges%2015%3A20&version=NIV). **Open — dance with it.** Marty raises it as a wondering, tied to Samson not being the deliverer God wanted and the Danites then abandoning their post.

## Terminology

- **Sin cycle vs. redemption cycle** — the six-part Judges cycle (go well → sin → judgment → cry out → deliverer → rescue/rest). Marty's thesis: sin is two-sixths, redemption four-sixths; the better name is the redemption cycle. *"it occupies more of the cycle, four-sixths of the cycle rather than the two-sixths of the cycle that sin does."*
- **Shephelah** — the Session-2 "in-between" lowlands between the Judah mountains (comfort) and the coastal plains (compromise); the place of trying and failing. *"If you're trying to live in shephelah, if you're trying to figure it out, and you're screwing it up... His patience... is absolutely endless."*
- **The anti-story** — becoming the *opposite* of the mission (harming visitors at the crossroads meant to bless the nations), as opposed to merely failing. *"They are working against God and His mission... literally the opposite, the anti-story of what God has asked them to do."*
- **A thousand to three** — God's self-description to Moses (Exodus 34): patience that is not "a thousand to zero." *"it's not a thousand to zero. It's a thousand to three."*
- **"Roused to action"** — the Session-1 category for why God finally moves: not depleted patience but committed love protecting the vulnerable. *"this is a great spot to remind ourselves... it's that God's patience is actually so committed to what He's doing in the world that He has to show up and protect the kids that are being abused."*

## Illustrations

- **The Google image search.** Typing "Judges s—" autofills "Judges sin cycle" before you finish the word, and returns endless sin-cycle graphics (Marty recalls a "flames of hell" version). Used to show how deeply the sin-framing is culturally embedded. Marty: *"I don't even get the word 'sin' completed, and Google autofills my query."*
- **Red Robin's bottomless fries.** Marty's wife's favorite restaurant; the fries are "bottomless," but *"I bet if I stayed long enough, I could find the bottom... God's patience, it's more bottomless than the fries at Red Robin."* An image for endless (truly bottomless) divine patience.
- **Telling your own life story two ways.** Brent's image for Joshua vs. Judges: *"I can totally see myself wanting to talk about my life and paint it in this beautiful light... And then in reality... I know the compromises I've made along the way."*
- **The Levite's concubine as Sodom retold.** The book's final story — visitors abused, a corpse dismembered and sent to the tribes — as a mirror of Sodom and Gomorrah, demonstrating Israel has *become* the anti-story.

## Recurring Through-lines

Motifs genuinely engaged in this episode (each with a cited quote):

- **God's endless patience / "roused to action."** *(Explicit.)* The organizing claim of the episode: *"God has endless patience, endless, endless patience"*; and the distinction that He acts not from lost patience but committed love.
- **Blessing all nations / the crossroads.** *(Explicit.)* The mission is the measuring stick that defines the anti-story and closes the episode: *"God put them at the crossroads of the Earth to bless all nations."*
- **The anti-story.** *(Explicit, newly named.)* The inversion of the mission — becoming the opposite of what God asked — introduced here and flagged as recurring in Session 2.
- **Shephelah / trying-and-failing in the in-between.** *(Explicit.)* The place where mistakes are expected and covered: *"If you're not out there engaging shephelah enough that you are making mistakes, you're probably not digging in enough."*
- **Both/and reading of inspired texts.** *(Implied.)* The Joshua/Judges discussion models holding two true tellings together as "exactly inspired" — a recurring interpretive posture, though left optional here by Marty himself.

## Discussion Questions

1. Why do you think the Western, Bible-college tradition instinctively names this the "sin cycle" rather than the "redemption cycle"? What changes in your reading when the title changes but the data stays the same?
2. Brent distinguishes "forgetting" from "willfully ignoring" God. Where in your own life do you *willfully ignore* what you already know — and how does it reframe grace to know God keeps forgiving that?
3. Marty draws a line between *trying and failing* (endless patience) and *becoming the anti-story* (what rouses God to action). How do you tell the difference in practice, personally or communally?
4. Are you engaged enough in the "shephelah" to be making mistakes? If not, what comfortable mountain or compromising plain are you living in instead?
5. "A thousand to three." How does hearing God's self-description as overwhelmingly patient — rather than quick to judge — reshape the way you read the hard, violent end of Judges?

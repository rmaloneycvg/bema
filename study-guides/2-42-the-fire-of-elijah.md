# The Fire of Elijah

BEMA 42 · Session 2
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/2-42-the-fire-of-elijah.md`
Book: 1 Kings 17:1-9; Deuteronomy 11:16-18; 1 Kings 18:1-2; 1 Kings 18:16-46; 1 Kings 19:1-18
Host: Marty Solomon · Co-host: Brent Billings · Guest: Reed Dent

> **Session 2 overarching arc: the Prophets — the partner's (Israel's) unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Session 1 was *partnership*: God sought a partner who "hears the cry" and invited a whole people to be a kingdom of priests. Session 2 is what happens *after* the partner breaks faith. By the time Elijah appears, Solomon's lust for empire has fractured the kingdom (Rehoboam's "scorpions"), Jeroboam has planted golden calves at Bethel and Dan, and Omri's Phoenician treaty has married the Asherah high priestess **Jezebel** to **Ahab** — Baal and Asherah are now Israel's gods. The partner is spectacularly unfaithful. And yet the whole episode is God *refusing to quit* on that partner — not with the flashy right-handed power Elijah keeps reaching for (drought, fire, slaughter), but with the still small voice, a reserved 7,000, and a new strategy: discipleship. This episode advances the one story by exposing the *limit of coercive power to restore a broken partnership* — "all the fire and all the wind and all the earthquakes and all the right-handed power does not change the human heart" (Marty) — and by naming love-trained-in-discipleship as how God means to win the partner back.

## Key Lessons

- **Fire that "works" on everyone except the one it was for.** Elijah wins the Carmel contest decisively — God's fire falls, the people cry "The LORD, he is God!" — but the one person the whole project was aimed at never changes. Reed: *"This has worked on everybody but the one person it was supposed to work on. And so yeah, the question is: what is the kind of power that actually can create the sort of change... Ahab has not changed."* Against the Session 2 arc, this is the diagnosis of why spectacle cannot restore a faithless partner.
- **Right-handed vs. left-handed power.** Reed names the frame that organizes the episode: *"the power of the right hand is like the power of display of force... compliance is not necessarily the same as genuine change. And... the power of the left hand is more like the power of a loaf of bread fresh out of the oven to compel people to come to the table."* Elijah is a virtuoso of right-handed power; God is after the left-handed kind.
- **God is not in the fire.** The story the hosts both grew up loving for its fire climaxes with a stunning reversal: God is *not* in the wind, the earthquake, or the fire, but in "a gentle whisper." Reed: *"in the very next instance of the story... God is like: I'm not in the fire."* The arc's point: God refuses to quit, but also refuses to win his partner back by the very means Elijah trusts.
- **Zeal is nuclear.** Elijah's defense is "I have been very zealous" — and the episode treats zeal with deep ambivalence. Reed: *"it's almost like atomic energy... it can be harnessed to power a city, but it can also become a thing that blows up that same city. And so zeal is worth maybe having in moderation."* God can *use* the fire, but the unchanged, verbatim-repeated complaint shows zeal alone cannot restore the covenant.
- **Being right can still make innocent bystanders pay.** Elijah is *right* that Deuteronomy applies — and God still seems to push back on the collateral damage of his judgment. Marty: *"I can even be completely right in my righteous judgment, and God's like: The problem is if we act, other people along the way deal with the consequences."* The ravens (stealing a starving child's food) and the rationing widow are God nudging his zealous partner toward restraint.
- **God refuses to quit — the reserved 7,000 and a successor.** Elijah's despair ("I alone am left") is answered not by vindication but by correction: God has "seven thousand in Israel... whose knees have not bowed down to Baal," and Elijah is to anoint Hazael, Jehu, and especially **Elisha**. God's answer to the broken partnership is to keep a faithful remnant and start the first rabbi–disciple relationship.
- **Love — not fire — changes the human heart, and love is trained in discipleship.** Marty's conclusion lands the arc: *"all the right-handed power does not change the human heart... So I need you to go find a disciple... love is the only thing: that still, small voice... that love can only be really trained and taught in discipleship."* This is how the episode advances the story: from Session 1's partnership-sought to Session 2's partnership-broken-and-pursued, with discipleship named as the way God means to win his unfaithful partner back.
- **These are complex people, not cardboard heroes and villains.** Before the story even starts the hosts refuse the hero/villain flattening — of Saul, David, Solomon, Jeroboam, even Jezebel and Elijah. Reed: *"I love the both-and-ness rather than the either-or-ness."* The unfaithful partner is us; "their story is similar to our story" (Marty).

## East/West Lens

- **Knowing/relationship over mechanics — "binding God to his text" (Hebraic).** The Jewish reading has Elijah invoke God's *own* promise rather than wait for a dictated word: Marty: *"the Jews call this, 'binding God to his text'... You said, God, in your word that this would happen... God has to go by His word, because He promised you."* Covenant faithfulness is relational leverage, not a magical formula.
- **The word carries sexualized, embodied force the English flattens (Hebraic philology).** The verb for "waver" in 1 Kings 18:21 is read as a cultic sexual gyration: Marty: *"That word that he used for waver is the word for like a sexual gyration, a dance that you would do for shrine prostitution... how long are you going to sexually gyrate between Baal and Adonai?"* The taunt at the Baal prophets is likewise "far more sexually explicit than we read into it." Meaning rides on the embodied Hebrew image.
- **Reading the Text in its own ancient-Near-Eastern world (the Baal/Asherah fertility myth).** The contest only makes sense against Phoenician mythology: Marty: *"Baal, the god of agricultural fertility... his animal is the bull. Lightning is his weapon. Thunder is his voice, and rain is his seed."* Elijah grants Baal "home-court advantage" — his mountain, altar, bull, and specialty (rain/lightning) — then beats him on his own turf.
- **Numeric/ritual symbolism as theology (twelve stones, the Sukkot water).** The twelve stones and the four-jars-poured-three-times (= twelve) are read as reunifying the fractured tribes and as a *Sukkot rain-petition*, not showmanship. Marty (via Ray): *"a later reader of this story is certainly going to hear him pouring water out like you do at Sukkot when you ask God for rain. Elijah's asking God for rain. He's not just trying to win an argument."* Brent: *"twelve pourings... it's almost like he's calling the entire nation back together."*
- **The still small voice and *sod* — intimacy as the locus of transformation (Hebraic).** The rabbis link the "gentle whisper" to the lovers' secret: Marty: *"The rabbis connected the still small voice... to the secret whisper, the sod... that two lovers whisper to one another in bed, it is love that has the power to change the human heart."* An Eastern category of knowledge-by-intimacy over Western proof-by-demonstration.
- **Both-and complexity over Western either/or moralizing.** Reed: *"I love the both-and-ness rather than the either-or-ness... that's why I like the complex nature of these Bible characters, is because it feels most true to what I know myself to be."* The lens resists sorting characters into heroes and villains — including Elijah ("not quite so purely heroic").
- **Midrash fills the silences (Hebraic interpretive humility).** Where the Text withholds Elijah's lineage and origin, Jewish tradition supplies readings rather than forcing the gap shut. Marty: *"Jewish tradition is gonna fill in some gaps for us in the Midrash... It's almost like a Melchizedek character."*

## Recurring Through-lines

- **The partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit (Session 2 arc).** The spine of the episode: Israel has gone whole-hog into Baal/Asherah worship, yet God keeps pursuing — ending the drought "for the people that need it," reserving 7,000, and commissioning a successor. Marty: *"love-love is the only thing... Elijah, join me in being about the work of human heart transformation."* God will not abandon the faithless partner, but will change *methods*.
- **Right-handed vs. left-handed power.** The episode's organizing motif (Reed): force/coercion gets compliance; invitation (fresh bread) changes the heart. Fire, drought, and slaughter are right-handed; the whisper and discipleship are left-handed. *"just because it looks big and flashy like a fire from heaven doesn't necessarily mean that it's accomplishing the thing."*
- **"It's in the Text" — knowing your Bible.** The running refrain that Elijah's confidence is grounded in Scripture (Deuteronomy 11): Reed, Brent, and Marty in unison — *"It's in the Text."* The prophet's authority is textual, not magical.
- **Discipleship as God's chosen strategy.** The still small voice resolves into a commission to make a disciple — "in the Jewish world, this is the first rabbi-disciple relationship" (Marty), Elijah → Elisha. The motif that love is trained person-to-person, pointing forward in the one story toward how God forms a faithful people.
- **The story behind the story (two sources; empire beneath idolatry).** The Chronicler's lens from the prior episodes carries in: Samuel/Kings indicts idolatry/immorality, but there is "a story behind the story" of imperial lust (Solomon → Rehoboam's cruelty → the split). Marty: *"there's also this other side of the story that goes: Wait, there's a story behind the story, which is why we're in this mess in the first place."*
- **Complex humans, not heroes and villains.** The both-and reading of Saul, David, Solomon, Jeroboam, Jezebel, and Elijah; "their story is similar to our story. They are not different than us" (Marty). The unfaithful partner is a mirror.
- **Being right vs. the cost to bystanders.** The ravens-and-widow motif: even righteous judgment, pressed as right-handed power, makes "innocent bystanders pay the price" (Marty).

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Cold open — childhood memories of the fire.** Brent first heard the story in a middle-school music class ("absolutely blew my mind"); Reed from a Pentecostal upbringing — "We were all about that heavenly fire, baby"; Brent: "God owned those prophets of Baal."
2. **Review — one story, two sources.** Marty recaps Samuel/Kings (real-time, Israel's perspective, agenda of immorality/idolatry) vs. Chronicles (centuries later, Judah's perspective, agenda of empire/injustice); Saul the donkey-herder of Benjamin, David the shepherd of Judah, Solomon the wise-and-empire-lustful.
3. **Reed on the complexity of being human.** Both-and over either-or; David as both the pursued victim pleading against "men of blood" and the "man of blood who needs to be absolved"; criminality is complex when the lens turns on ourselves.
4. **Backstory of the split.** Solomon → Rehoboam's "my pinky is as thick as my father's waist... I will scourge you with scorpions"; the northern ten tribes' legitimate protest pursued "outside of God's parameters"; Jeroboam's golden calves at Bethel and Dan for political expediency; "the sins of Jeroboam."
5. **The dynastic chain to Ahab.** Jeroboam → Baasha → Elah → Zimri → Omri (the diplomat, heavily attested extra-biblically); Omri sustains the Treaty of Phoenicia begun by **Solomon** (masons/bronze from Hiram; 20 Galilee towns given).
6. **The Baal/Asherah fertility myth.** Phoenicia's gods arrive with the treaty; Baal (agricultural fertility — bull, lightning, thunder, rain-as-seed) and Asherah (sexual fertility); the seasonal underworld myth; the high priestess **Jezebel** married to **Ahab**. Marty flags his own flattening of Jezebel against the just-stated complexity point.
7. **Reed's complexity caution restated.** Trace back past "the sins of Jeroboam" to Rehoboam's oppression; Reed links Jesus' "the Gentiles lord it over them... not so with you" to the Rehoboam failure.
8. **1 Kings 17:1-6 — Elijah appears from nowhere.** No lineage, unknown Tishbe, a "Melchizedek character"; the bold word that it won't rain.
9. **"Binding God to his text."** The Jewish reading: God did *not* tell Elijah to declare the drought (the narrator only later says "the word of the LORD came," 17:8); Elijah's *chutzpah* invokes Deuteronomy 11:16-17. Brent reads 17:7 then 17:8 to show the Text knows how to flag God's speech.
10. **Deuteronomy 11:16-18 read.** "He will shut up the heavens so that it will not rain"; Reed: Ahab/Jezebel are worshiping other gods — "Deuteronomy applies."
11. **The ravens and the widow as God's nudge.** Ravens = unclean scavengers taking someone else's food in a famine; then the rationing widow of Zarephath — God signaling that slung judgment makes innocents pay, even when Elijah is right.
12. **1 Kings 18:1-2 — God finally speaks: "I will send rain."** After three years, God's first word is to *end* it ("I wonder if he was trying to get Elijah to loosen up"); Reed: Ahab still hasn't changed.
13. **1 Kings 18:16-20 — "troubler of Israel" and the Carmel summons.** God said only "go tell Ahab it's going to rain"; Elijah "makes a thing out of it," summoning the 450 prophets of Baal and 400 of Asherah.
14. **18:21 — "waver between two opinions."** The Hebrew = cultic sexual gyration; the people say *nothing* (contrast Joshua's "we will serve Adonai!"). Marty's teacher's callback question: worse spot, or memory of Joshua?
15. **18:22-24 — the contest terms; Baal's home-court advantage.** No altar needed for Baal (one already exists); Elijah will later have to *build* an altar to Adonai; "the god who answers by fire — he is God."
16. **18:25-29 — Baal's prophets fail; Elijah's trash talk.** Dancing, slashing, "frantic prophesying"; the taunt ("deep in thought... traveling... sleeping") read as more sexually explicit in Hebrew.
17. **18:30-35 — twelve stones, the Sukkot water.** Not showmanship but a rain-petition (Ray); twelve stones reunite the tribes; four jars × three = twelve pourings; Brent: "calling the entire nation back together."
18. **18:36-46 — the fire falls; the slaughter; the rain; the run.** "The LORD — he is God!"; the Baal prophets executed at the Kishon (which God did not command); the hand-sized cloud; Elijah outruns Ahab's chariot to Jezreel. Marty on Elijah's relentless physical exertion (up and down Carmel 3×, then a half-marathon).
19. **"Like it works."** Marty emphasizes the contest *succeeds* on its own terms — God wins the argument, the people confess.
20. **1 Kings 19:1-5 — Jezebel's threat; Elijah collapses.** Despite the public victory, one threat sends Elijah fleeing to a broom bush praying to die — "where was all the people?" The victory didn't hold.
21. **Reed on right- vs. left-handed power.** The hammer vs. the fresh loaf; Elijah's moves were all right-handed; "who was this whole thing actually for? ... It was Ahab. And Ahab has not changed."
22. **19:6-10 — fed by the angel; Horeb; "I alone am left."** Forty days to Horeb; Elijah's zealous complaint.
23. **19:11-14 — God not in wind, earthquake, or fire, but the gentle whisper.** The famous reversal; Elijah repeats his complaint *verbatim* — unchanged. Jewish tradition: Elijah is so stubbornly zealous God will later "retire" him in the chariots.
24. **19:15-18 — the commission and the 7,000.** Anoint Hazael, Jehu, and Elisha; the reserved 7,000 who have not bowed to Baal.
25. **Conclusion — love, the still small voice, and discipleship.** Right-handed power can't change the heart; the *sod* / Song of Solomon lovers' whisper; Elijah → Elisha as the first rabbi–disciple pair; "love is the thing that God is up to."
26. **Final thoughts on zeal.** Reed: zeal as nuclear energy — power a city or blow it up; "in moderation." Brent and Reed close on still loving the story, now in "a different light."

## Terminology

- **binding God to his text** — the Jewish phrase for invoking God's own written promise to compel his action. Marty: *"the Jews call this, 'binding God to his text'... You said, God, in your word that this would happen... God has to go by His word, because He promised you."* Elijah declares the drought (1 Kgs 17:1) by binding God to Deuteronomy 11:16-17.
- **chutzpah** — audacious boldness/nerve. Marty: *"Elijah's fiery. Like he has some chutzpah. It's going to take some chutzpah to show up and say it's not going to rain if God did not tell you."* Characterizes Elijah's whole right-handed posture.
- **"waver between two opinions"** (1 Kgs 18:21) — the Hebrew carries the sense of a cultic sexual gyration / shrine-prostitution dance. Marty: *"That word that he used for waver is the word for like a sexual gyration... how long are you going to sexually gyrate between Baal and Adonai?"*
- **Baal and Asherah** — Phoenician fertility deities imported via the Solomon→Omri→Ahab treaty: *Baal* = agricultural fertility (bull his animal, lightning his weapon, thunder his voice, rain his "seed"); *Asherah* = sexual fertility. Marty: *"Baal, the god of agricultural fertility... Asherah, who is the goddess of sexual fertility."* The Carmel contest attacks this myth on its own turf.
- **the sins of Jeroboam** — the refrain for the northern kingdom's golden-calf cult at Bethel and Dan, set up for political expediency. Marty: *"all throughout the story, it will keep calling them back to the sins of Jeroboam."*
- **right-handed power / left-handed power** (Reed) — *right-handed* = coercive force (a hammer) producing compliance, not change; *left-handed* = invitation (fresh bread drawing people to the table) that changes the heart. Reed: *"compliance is not necessarily the same as genuine change... the power of a loaf of bread fresh out of the oven to compel people to come to the table."*
- **the Sukkot water ceremony** — the festival rite of pouring water to petition God for rain; Elijah's drenching of the altar (four jars × three) is read as this petition, not showmanship. Marty: *"a later reader of this story is certainly going to hear him pouring water out like you do at Sukkot when you ask God for rain."*
- **the still small voice / gentle whisper** (1 Kgs 19:12) — the mode in which God is present *after* wind, earthquake, and fire; the opposite of right-handed display. Brent reads: *"And after the fire came a gentle whisper."*
- **sod** (סוד) — "secret"; the rabbis link the still small voice to the intimate whisper of lovers (Song of Solomon). Marty: *"The rabbis connected the still small voice... to the secret whisper, the sod... that two lovers whisper to one another in bed."* Love, not force, transforms.
- **rabbi–disciple relationship (Elijah → Elisha)** — read in Jewish tradition as the *first* such pairing. Marty: *"In the Jewish world, this is the first rabbi-disciple relationship... in most Jewish thought, the first rabbi-disciple is Elijah and Elisha."* God's left-handed strategy for heart-change.
- **zeal** — fervent devotion; the episode treats it ambivalently. Reed: *"it's almost like atomic energy... it can be harnessed to power a city, but it can also become a thing that blows up that same city... zeal is worth maybe having in moderation."* Elijah's self-defense ("I have been very zealous") is also his flaw.

## Illustrations & Stories

- **The hammer vs. the fresh loaf of bread (Reed).** The defining image of the two powers: a hammer *forces* a nail; fresh bread *draws* people to the table without force. *"the power of a loaf of bread fresh out of the oven to compel people to come to the table... It is inherently compelling... the kind of thing that can actually change a person deep down."*
- **The Grand Canyon ravens stealing the picnic (Reed).** A living illustration of the scavenger-raven point: *"these ravens, they would just hop right up on your picnic table and take — I mean you would be sitting... I'm like, dude, it's my food."* Dramatizes God feeding Elijah by birds that snatch someone else's lunch in a famine.
- **"My pinky is as thick as my father's waist... scorpions" (Marty, retelling Rehoboam).** Rehoboam's boast of harsher oppression — "you haven't seen nothing yet" — the imperial cruelty that fractures the kingdom and seeds the whole crisis.
- **The Baal–Asherah underworld myth (Marty).** The PG-rated retelling of why the seasons turn: Baal chasing Asherah into the underworld (winter/death), their "fun" producing the rains — the mythic logic Elijah's drought-and-fire directly refutes.
- **Elijah up and down Carmel 3× then outrunning a chariot (Marty/Brent).** The prophet's relentless physical exertion — climbing the mountain repeatedly, slaughtering 450 at the brook, then running a "half marathon to beat a chariot" — Brent: "I don't feel like running more than about two hundred meters." A portrait of raw right-handed zeal.
- **Zeal as nuclear/atomic energy (Reed).** The closing image: zeal "can be harnessed to power a city, but it can also become a thing that blows up that same city" — hence "in moderation."

## Discussion Questions

1. Reed distinguishes right-handed power (the hammer — force that gets compliance) from left-handed power (fresh bread — invitation that changes the heart). Where in your own life or leadership do you default to the hammer, and what would the "fresh bread" alternative actually look like in that exact situation?
2. The contest "worked" on everyone *except* Ahab — the one person it was for. Where have you poured energy into a flashy, convincing effort that moved a crowd but never reached the one heart it was meant to change? What does that reveal about the limits of being persuasive?
3. God is pointedly "not in the fire" — the very thing the story is famous for and the thing Elijah (and Reed's Pentecostal upbringing) most loved. What "fire" are you most attached to as the sign of God's presence, and how might you be missing the "gentle whisper"?
4. Elijah repeats his complaint — "I alone am left" — word-for-word even after the mountaintop encounter. What is a complaint or wound you keep repeating unchanged, and what would it take for the still small voice, rather than more proof, to actually move you?
5. God's answer to a faithless partner is not to quit but to change methods — reserve a faithful 7,000 and *make a disciple*. If love-trained-in-discipleship is how God means to win his partner back, who is the one person (not the crowd) God might be asking you to invest in that way?
6. Reed calls zeal "atomic energy" — able to power a city or blow it up. Where is your zeal currently powering good, and where is it at risk of detonating? What would "zeal in moderation" look like without becoming apathy?
7. The hosts refuse to flatten Saul, David, Solomon, Jezebel — even Elijah — into simple heroes and villains. Pick a "villain" in your own story: what does turning the lens of complexity (as Reed does with his own motives) change about how you'd respond to them?

**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings
**Primary text:** The whole book of Obadiah (one chapter) — [Obadiah 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Obadiah+1&version=NIV)

## Where This Sits in the One Story

Session 1 built the picture of **partnership**: God rescues a people and invites them to become a kingdom of priests who care for the vulnerable and reflect his character to the nations. Session 2 turns to the **Prophets**, and the arc of this stage is the covenant partner's (Israel's) **unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Obadiah advances that arc from a startling, outside angle. Every other prophet so far has aimed its condemnation at the people of God — "all the other prophets have been the condemnation of the people of God for what they've done." Obadiah is "unique because it speaks to somebody outside that covenant relationship with God." It is addressed not to Israel or Judah but to **Edom** — the long-lost brother. Yet it still serves the one story: the God who refuses to quit on his unfaithful partner also refuses to let that partner be preyed upon in its hour of ruin. As Babylon crushes Judah (the very defeat Session 2's Babylonian prophets presuppose), Edom stands aloof and then loots and betrays its own brother — and God, who "cares about people," will not let that pass. The arc's refusal-to-quit here reaches past the covenant line: God holds even the brother-nation accountable because he expects "a decent level of respect for humanity and how you treat your brother."

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 2 arc — the covenant partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **God's refusal to quit extends past the covenant line.** Where every prior prophet condemned the people of God, Obadiah shows that God's concern for his battered partner reaches even to the brother-nation that preys on it. God holds Edom accountable *for how it treated Judah*.
   - *Marty:* "all the other prophets have been the condemnation of the people of God for what they've done... yet this prophet is unique because it speaks to somebody outside that covenant relationship with God and says, 'Even from you, I expect — even from you, I expect a decent treatment of how you treat people.'"

2. **God cares about people — and is *furious* when the vulnerable are exploited.** The arc's refusal-to-quit is grounded in a God who notices the cry of the oppressed partner; preying on Judah in its ruin provokes not just displeasure but fury.
   - *Marty:* "God cares about people. He cares about how you treat people. It makes Him upset when you don't treat people well, but it makes Him furious when you actually take advantage of people in need."

3. **Standing aloof is itself a sin; indifference is the first step down.** The indictment begins not with active cruelty but with doing nothing — a convicting lesson in a story about a people called to notice suffering.
   - *Marty:* "it starts off by just standing aloof and doing nothing, which is in itself a convicting lesson. It's not okay to just stand there while other people suffer and do nothing about it; but then it just keeps escalating."

4. **Pride in your own defenses deceives you.** Edom trusted its impenetrable rock fortress; God confronts the pride that says no one can bring it down. The partner's enemies — and the partner itself — are undone by self-sufficiency, not by lack of security.
   - *Marty:* "You think you're so safe, you live in the clefts of the rock. You've got this pride in your heart that says who can bring me down to the ground."

5. **The prophets are not an academic exercise — they indict us.** The point of studying this condemnation is to be challenged about how *we* treat people, whatever our defaults. This keeps the Session 2 arc from becoming merely historical.
   - *Marty:* "I know that we make the prophetic study... an academic exercise, but I hope that all of our listeners... are taking to heart what this means in our day and age. What does it mean to care about people?... this is the challenge in the condemnation of the prophets."

## East/West Lens

Only categories genuinely engaged by this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **God who feels — ethos/pathos (East).** The language is driven by God actually being upset and furious about mistreatment, not by abstract legal verdict.
  - *Marty:* "It makes Him upset when you don't treat people well, but it makes Him furious when you actually take advantage of people in need."

- **Kinship and communal obligation over individual contract (East).** The entire weight of the prophecy rests on *brotherhood* — Edom's duty is framed by shared blood and story, not by treaty terms.
  - *Marty:* "Edom happens to be the long-lost brother of the people of Judah... They have maybe a unique or even a more covenantal relationship with God, and so God has something to say about them."

- **Image/story thinking over systematic abstraction (East).** The book is taught through a concrete, memorable image — Petra, the rock fortress — rather than a doctrinal proposition; the whole prophetic review runs on images.
  - *Marty:* "The image that we're going to use for Obadiah is going to be the image of Petra."

- **Geography and embodied place over placeless ideas (East).** Marty insists Western readers neglect biblical geography, and that *seeing where Edom sits* changes how the text lands.
  - *Marty:* "Westerners, we're really bad about biblical geography, we don't typically care, but it's helpful to see where Edom lies in reference to Israel."

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension states the problem, tags its type, cites the transcript, links the passage, and gives a Hebraic resolution (or marks it open).

1. **Why does God condemn a nation *outside* the covenant?**
   - *Type:* Apparent contradiction / scope question (theological).
   - *Problem:* Every other prophet addresses the people of God; Obadiah's indictment lands on Edom, who never signed the Sinai covenant.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "this prophet is unique because it speaks to somebody outside that covenant relationship with God and says, 'Even from you, I expect a decent treatment of how you treat people. I expect everyone to have a decent level of respect for humanity.'"
   - *Passage:* [Obadiah 1:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Obadiah+1%3A10&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The obligation flows from *kinship*, not contract. Edom is the brother (Esau), a child of Abraham, so a baseline of human decency and family loyalty is expected of them. God's moral concern is for all people's treatment of one another, and it is heightened by the closeness of the brotherhood.

2. **How can the God who "cares about people" leave *no survivors* of Esau?**
   - *Type:* Values clash / theodicy (the problem of evil).
   - *Problem:* The episode insists God cares about people, yet the book ends with Esau as stubble, "no survivors," while Jacob is restored.
   - *Cited:* *Brent:* "Not a lot of hope for Esau though." / *Marty:* "No, sir." And the text: "Esau will be stubble and they will set him on fire and destroy him. There will be no survivors from Esau."
   - *Passage:* [Obadiah 1:18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Obadiah+1%3A18&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The hosts hold the hope and the judgment together without flattening them — the same two-handed posture seen with Jonah/Nahum. The judgment language ("just as you have done, so it will be done to you") is measured by Edom's own deeds; the enduring thrust is that God "cares about people" and will finally put the world back together ("the kingdom will be the LORD's"). The disparity between Jacob's inheritance and Esau's stubble is left in the tension of the prophetic word rather than resolved into a system — **Open; sit with it.**

3. **Can a people this impressive and advanced still be brought down?**
   - *Type:* Dramatic irony / reversal (narrative).
   - *Problem:* The Idumean-Nabataeans were among the wealthiest, most sophisticated people of the ancient world, in a fortress even Rome couldn't take — yet God pronounces their undoing.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "They thought it was impenetrable. They thought it was a great defense and that's going to play into the tone and what we hear from the Prophet Obadiah." And the text: "Though you soar like the eagle and make your nest among the stars, from there I will bring you down, declares the Lord."
   - *Passage:* [Obadiah 1:3-4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Obadiah+1%3A3-4&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The very security that looks impenetrable is the seat of the deadly pride ("who can bring me down to the ground?"). In the story's logic, no fortress is a substitute for faithfulness to one's brother; the reversal is exactly the point.

## Terminology

- **Obadiah / Ovadiah** (עֹבַדְיָה) — the prophet whose single chapter is the shortest book in the Tanakh; a Babylonian-period prophet; image: **Petra**.
  - *Brent:* "Today we explore the shortest prophet in Tanakh, the book of Obadiah — Ovadiah — and its condemnation of the nation of Edom for their lack of care for their brother and their neighbor."
- **Edom** — the descendants of Esau; the "long-lost brother" of Judah, a child of Abraham, more closely tied to the story than Moab, Ammon, Philistia, or Phoenicia; the audience of Obadiah.
  - *Marty:* "Edom is the descendant of Esau and Edom happens to be the long-lost brother of the people of Judah."
- **chutzpah** — gutsy audacity; the kind of life Jacob introduced, carried into the previous episode's Habakkuk; God tends to use people who have it.
  - *Marty:* "we had a particular biblical character that introduced us to the concept of having a life built around chutzpah. Not Abraham, but his grandson... Jacob."
- **Idumeans / Nabataeans ("Idumean-Nabataeans")** — the historical descendants of Edom; spice-trade merchants of immense wealth; nomadic for roughly a thousand years before mysteriously building cities.
  - *Marty:* "Edom becomes known in history... as the Idumeans... the Idumeans are synonymous with the Nabataeans. The Nabataean-Idumeans."
- **Petra** — the Idumean-Nabataean rock-cut city in modern Jordan; the image for Obadiah; its facades are mostly shallow tomb-fronts; natural, "impenetrable" fortress.
  - *Marty:* "history shows us the Nabataeans go from being nomadic to building these unbelievable cities. The image that we're going to use for Obadiah is going to be the image of Petra."
- **diyn / mishpat** (reviewed) — two Hebrew words for justice: diyn, a pronouncement of (more retributive) justice; mishpat, distributive justice that puts everything in its right place and brings shalom.
  - *Brent:* "Mishpat." / *Marty:* "That's what kind of justice?" / *Brent:* "That is distributive." / *Brent:* "Putting everything in its right place." / *Marty:* "Bringing Shalom."
- **t'shuvah** (reviewed) — "return"; Zephaniah's image.
  - *Brent:* "Zephaniah, and his image was t'shuvah, as in 'return.'"

## Illustrations & Images

- **Petra, the impenetrable rock fortress.** The governing image: a city reachable only single-file through narrow rock passages, a natural stronghold its people thought could never be taken — the seedbed of Edom's pride.
  - *Marty:* "You could have never gotten an army back in there... It serves a natural fortress. They thought it was impenetrable... that's going to play into the tone and what we hear from the Prophet Obadiah."
- **"He went that way."** Marty's Bible-college image: Judah flees into the rock fortresses chased by Babylon; Edom refuses shelter — "Sorry, we can't help you, keep on moving" — and points the pursuers after their own brother.
  - *Marty:* "it's almost like the people of Edom point and say, 'He went that way.' They have no concern for the needs of their brother and neighbor, Judah."
- **The escalating indictment (disaster, disaster, disaster).** Brent notices the hammering repetition; the sin ratchets from standing aloof, to gloating, to looting, to cutting down fugitives.
  - *Brent:* "don't forget disaster and disaster and disaster. I felt a little bit of a theme there as I was reading."
- **The Aladdin "cave of wonders" (for Lamentations).** In review, the single nugget of hope buried in Lamentations' chiasm is likened to the lamp lit in the dark on the giant staircase.
  - *Brent:* "It's like Aladdin and the giant... the giant staircase with the lamp and the little spot of light."
- **The Indiana Jones facade.** Petra's famous rock face is the temple front from *The Last Crusade* — anticlimactic in reality, "only like 20 feet deep," just a tomb.
  - *Marty:* "There's a rock facade there that is the temple that he goes into... it's super anticlimactic if you get there... it's only like 20 feet deep."

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty says Obadiah is unique because God condemns a nation *outside* the covenant — "Even from you I expect a decent treatment of how you treat people." Where do you tend to assume God's moral expectations only apply to "insiders," and how does Edom's brotherhood obligation challenge that?
2. The indictment "starts off by just standing aloof and doing nothing, which is in itself a convicting lesson." Where in your own life or community is *doing nothing* while others suffer the real sin — before any active cruelty is even in view?
3. Edom trusted an "impenetrable" rock fortress and told itself "who can bring me down to the ground?" What are the "clefts of the rock" — the securities and defenses — that most tempt you toward that same pride?
4. Marty insists the prophets are "not just an academic exercise" and that "whatever your perspective is, you're being challenged to consider the way you treat people." Pick one concrete relationship or group you're tempted to treat with indifference. What would it look like to care instead?
5. The book ends with hope for Jacob but Esau as stubble — Brent notes "not a lot of hope for Esau though." How do you hold God's fierce defense of the exploited *and* his care about all people together without resolving the tension too quickly?

# Our Harps in the Trees

BEMA 44 · Session 2
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/2-44-our-harps-in-the-trees.md`
Book: Psalms (overview) — Psalm 104; 19; 136; 13; 22; 88; 137; 30; 40 (plus 1–2, 8, 51, 82, 119, 139, and the Songs of Ascent 120–134, all named in passing).
Co-host: Brent Billings · Guest co-host: Reed Dent (the Psalms / wisdom-literature specialist) · (Marty Solomon does **not** appear in this episode; this is a reboot/expanded re-recording)
Core frame leaned on throughout: Walter Brueggemann's three movements — **Psalms of Orientation, Disorientation, and New Orientation** — and Reed's closing thesis that *to bring anything honestly to God — even rage or lament — is itself an act of praise.*

> **Session 2 overarching arc: the prophets — the partner's (Israel's) unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Session 1 built the *partnership*: God and a people bound in covenant, "you create the space and I'll fill it." Session 2 sits in the long era when the partner falters, is carried into exile, and still cannot be let go. This episode advances the one story by handing the partner a *voice for the whole relationship* — not just for the days it goes well. The Psalter's center of gravity, for Reed, is honesty in every season: "a life lived with God and a life lived before God." That is partnership continued *under strain*. The disorientation psalms give words to a partner who feels abandoned — "How long, Lord, will you forget me?" (Ps 13), "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Ps 22), a psalm (88) that ends in the dark with no resolution, and Babylon's rivers where "we hung our harps" (Ps 137) in the exile the prophets watched arrive. Yet the refusal to quit runs the other direction too: for the psalmist, God's very *absence* "produces argument, not atheism" — "I'm just gonna keep yelling at you more, until you show up." Where Session 1 asked whether the partner would say *yes*, this episode shows the partner learning to keep the conversation going when the honest word is *How long?* — and names that unbroken, honest engagement as the deepest praise. The Psalms are also the bridge into the prophets by way of their chief interpreter: Brueggemann, Reed notes, "is going to become a very important scholar for us and the ways that we read the prophets especially."

## Key Lessons

- **The Psalms are not window dressing — they are "serious stuff," the honest voice of a whole life with God.** Reed: *"the Psalms are not simply the window dressing to the more serious parts of the Bible... They grow out of the whole gamut of experience in a life lived with God and a life lived before God. They are serious stuff. They are earnest expression. They are earnest wrestling and grappling."*
- **Brueggemann's three movements map the terrain: Orientation → Disorientation → New Orientation.** Reed walks all three: Orientation = "a sense of things making sense... an order to things, a rightness to things" (Ps 104, 19, 136); Disorientation = "alienation from God, a sense of anger... complaining or lamenting their own suffering" (Ps 13, 22, 88, 137); New Orientation = joy and trust "that feels really earned... they know enough to know how it can go wrong and that it has gone wrong" (Ps 30, 40).
- **The categories are not fixed, static, or a one-way staircase — Psalm 22 moves from disorientation to new orientation inside one psalm.** Reed: *"this is a movement from one state into another... they're not fixed categories, and I don't even think it's just necessarily a linear cycle."* Brent catches that Psalm 22 is read twice, under two headings — "Wait a second."
- **God can handle — and actually invites — all of you, including the ugly parts.** Reed: *"it's not just that God can handle all of you. But it's like, I think God invites all of you."* The editors kept the hard psalms: *"the Bible we have has things that speak to all of you."*
- **Honesty with God is the point; decorum at the expense of honesty makes no sense.** Reed: *"if there are some of us who feel like we need to maintain decorum with God at the expense of honesty... just read the Psalms. God is already what is nearest to your thoughts. So what sense does it make to hide what is deepest inside you?"* Brent: *"if I really believe that God knows everything, why would I try to pretend?"*
- **The Psalms both give voice to what's already in you and stretch you into registers you don't naturally speak.** Reed: they "put words to where I'm already at," but also "give me a vocabulary for language that I don't speak as well" — pushing the brooder to rejoice and the natural rejoicer to lament.
- **The Psalms are theological, but not systematically so — you can't build a doctrine on a psalm alone.** Reed: *"They are theological, but not abstractly so."* Brent (from Marty): *"you can't build a theology only on the Psalms. But you can... check it against Torah or some other part of the Bible."*
- **The imprecatory (vengeance) psalms are a release valve: you voice the rage to God in a song so you don't act it out in the world.** Reed (from Brueggemann): *"you make this plea to God... that we'd be happy to dash the babies against the rocks. But you do that so that you then don't actually go out and do that in the real world... vengeance actually is yours."*
- **Why "Tehillim" ("Songs of Praise") fits even a book full of lament: to bring anything honestly to God is itself praise.** Reed: *"maybe to bring anything honestly to God is an act of praise... a deep commitment to hang on to God as God... no matter what, in whatever circumstance."* Not the "this is fine" dog in the burning room; honest praise, not "cheap sentimentality."
- **God is the appropriate dialogue partner for every season — so keep the conversation going.** Reed (quoting Derek at CCF): *"The Psalms show that God is the appropriate dialogue partner for every season of life."* The pastoral charge: *"keep the conversation going. Don't hang up"* — not when it's hard, not when it's great.

## East/West Lens

*(Only categories the episode actually engages, each with a citation.)*

- **Hearty over heady — the Hebraic whole-person vs. the Western head-first default.** Reed frames the Psalms as "a little less heady, a little more hearty," engaging the whole person's emotional life, not just the intellect — while insisting there is still "something to engage the mind about." The Eastern instinct is to pray the feelings, not merely analyze them.
- **Relational/experiential theology vs. Western systematic theology.** Reed: the Psalms are "theological, but not abstractly so"; expecting "a cleanly systematic or perfectly logical diagram of God" will frustrate you, because the same David says "conceived in sin" (Ps 51) *and* "you knit me together in my mother's womb" (Ps 139). Truth is held as lived tension, not resolved into a flowchart.
- **God as the addressed Other (object/hearer), not a subject of study.** Reed: *"God is not a subject really... He is the hearer toward which the Psalmists direct their words."* Even God's absence "produces argument, not... atheism" — the Eastern posture of staying in dialogue, "always trying to pull God back onto the mat."
- **Communal AND individual function held together (Eastern collectivist + personal).** Reed: the Psalms served "a communal function... almost a formal sort of liturgy" (the Songs of Ascent sung on the pilgrimage road to Jerusalem) *and* "a very personal purpose of confession... affection for God or wrestling with God."
- **Reading a psalm against its lived narrative setting (Eastern story-rootedness vs. Western abstraction).** Reed's "fun thought experiment": place a Davidic psalm inside the story of 1–2 Samuel — explicit in Psalm 51 ("after David was confronted by the prophet Nathan... about Bathsheba"), inviting the same imaginative placement for the rest.

## Recurring Through-lines

*(Motifs genuinely surfaced by this episode — explicit callbacks the hosts name, plus genuinely-implied threads. No motif forced. Registry not consulted per task overrides.)*

- **God's refusal to quit the partnership (Session 2 arc).** For the psalmist, God's absence does not end the relationship — it fuels it: *"God's absence produces argument. They're like: You're not here, I'm just gonna keep yelling at you more, until you show up."* The partner keeps talking; the covenant conversation never fully breaks.
- **Grappling / wrestling with God (callback to Jacob, Session 1).** Reed names the Psalms as "earnest wrestling and grappling," and the disorientation psalms as the partner refusing to let go even in the dark — the same Peniel-shaped faith the series has tracked since Genesis.
- **"How long?" — the lament that doesn't demand an answer.** The signature question of disorientation (Ps 13): *"How long? And that's a question that doesn't have to be answered... It's just that longing of: How long?"* A thread the prophets will take up as the exile deepens.
- **Honesty over decorum (yada-style relationship).** The whole episode presses toward unguarded truth-telling with God; Brent: *"why would I try to pretend? Because it's not like anything's going to surprise God."* Relationship, not performance.
- **Exile and the hung-up harps (Session 2 / the prophets' world).** Psalm 137 — "By the rivers of Babylon we sat and wept... There on the poplars we hung our harps" — sets the title and roots the episode in the exile the prophets witness: a people unable to "sing the songs of the Lord... in a foreign land."
- **Creation and Torah as grounds of trust (callback to Genesis and Sinai, Session 1).** The orientation psalms rest on "reflecting on Creation, on how the world works" (Ps 104) and on the Law being "trustworthy... right... radiant" (Ps 19) — the created order and the covenant Law from Session 1 reappearing as the floor beneath praise.
- **The pit / rescue and "joy comes in the morning."** New-orientation psalms recur to being "lifted up and out" of the pit (Ps 30, 40) — "weeping may stay for the night, but rejoicing comes in the morning" — a death-and-rescue shape the series keeps surfacing.

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Framing / a reboot without Marty.** Brent introduces Reed Dent to discuss "the collection of ancient songs known as the Psalms" — Reed's first solo reboot episode. He notes Marty had said the Psalms were "a little less heady, a little more hearty, maybe... not totally his jam."
2. **The name: Tehillim.** Reed: in Hebrew the title is *Tehillim*, "songs of praise" — "kind of interesting to call the whole thing that," given how diverse the 150 are. He flags he'll return to this at the end.
3. **Grammar / counting asides.** It's "Psalm 22," singular, not "Psalms 22"; the 150 aren't really "chapters" like a novel. The playful YouVersion call-out ("please... change it to be Psalm 22").
4. **The Psalms are serious, not decoration.** Reed's thesis that the Psalms "are not simply the window dressing" — "earnest expression... earnest wrestling and grappling" — and serve both a **communal/liturgical** function (the Songs of Ascent, Pss 120–134, sung going up to Jerusalem) and an **individual** function (confession, affection, wrestling). Overarching subject: "honest worship and relationship with God... in every phase of life."
5. **Brent's observation about authorship.** Brent expected David to open the book, but Psalm 1 and Psalm 2 are anonymous; David arrives at Psalm 3. Reed: a hint of the **compiled/edited** nature — Pss 1–2 as an *introduction*; the Psalter's five-book "seams" (per the BibleProject); roughly half the psalms are Davidic.
6. **The 1–2 Samuel thought experiment.** Reed loves placing Davidic psalms inside the Samuel narrative; Psalm 51 is explicit ("after David was confronted by the prophet Nathan... about Bathsheba"); the "treasure hunt" is to imagine where the others fit — "the Psalms as emerging out of David's real experience."
7. **Introducing Brueggemann.** Brent: Walter Brueggemann, soon "a huge source" for the prophets. Reed lays out the three-part framework and stresses it's "not written in stone — the Psalms do not identify themselves in these three categories."
8. **Psalms of Orientation.** "Things making sense... an order... a rightness... well-being... gratitude," grounded in Creation and Law. Examples read: **Ps 104** (Creation; "He set the earth on its foundations. It can never be moved"), **Ps 19** (the Law "perfect... trustworthy... right... radiant"), **Ps 119** (named), and **Ps 136** ("Give thanks to the Lord, for He is good. His love endures forever" — the repetition that "works it down into you"). The "porch at dusk with lemonade" picture.
9. **Psalms of Disorientation.** "Alienation... anger... complaining or lamenting." The preeminent question — **Ps 13** "How long?" — then **Ps 22** ("My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"), **Ps 88** (ends "darkness is my closest friend" — "full stop, curtain drops," no resolution), and **Ps 137** ("By the rivers of Babylon... we hung our harps... happy is the one who seizes your infants and dashes them against the rocks").
10. **Brent on the editors' juxtaposition.** Brent notes 136 (orientation) sits immediately beside 137 (disorientation) — "intentionally by the compilers, juxtaposed." The "total whiplash" between "His love endures forever" and "dashes them against the rocks."
11. **A first pass at the imprecatory problem.** Reed flags the vengeance language, promises to return to it, and says the Psalms "have a place for even that state."
12. **Psalms of New Orientation.** Joy/trust *beyond* the pit, "earned." Examples read: **Ps 30** ("you spared me from going down to the pit... weeping may stay for the night, but rejoicing comes in the morning"), **Ps 40** (U2's "40" — "He lifted me out of the slimy pit... set my feet on a rock"), and then **Ps 22** again.
13. **The Psalm 22 "wait a second" — categories aren't fixed.** Brent realizes he already read Ps 22 under disorientation; Reed uses the end of Ps 22 ("I will declare your name... all the ends of the earth will remember and turn to the Lord") to show a psalm *moving* from disorientation into new orientation "as if... the rescue hasn't even really happened yet" — anticipation becoming assurance. Lesson: wherever you are, you can anticipate movement; it's not linear.
14. **"There's an app for that" — the kaleidoscope.** Reed revives Marty's old tongue-in-cheek line: the Psalms "come in as many flavors as there are human experiences" — depression, elation, wonder (Ps 8, Ps 139 "fearfully and wonderfully made"), rage, confusion, trust, complaint, praise; wisdom-flavored psalms that "start to feel like Proverbs and Ecclesiastes."
15. **Disorientation from within — our own fallibility.** Not only outside trouble but "disappointment" in ourselves: "Surely I was conceived in sin" (Ps 51); the same David who hates "men of blood" (Ps 139) can confess "I am that man of blood"; "Restore to me a clean heart."
16. **The imprecatory psalms, properly.** Reed returns to the vengeance question: whether "okay or not okay, it is what it is to feel this." Brueggemann's reading — voice the desire to God in a psalm "so that you then don't actually go out and do that" — "vengeance actually is yours, God; here it is."
17. **The editors kept the hard ones — God invites all of you.** They didn't scrub the Psalter to only "give thanks to the Lord for he is good." "It's not just that God can handle all of you... God invites all of you." Decorum at the expense of honesty makes no sense when "God is already what is nearest to your thoughts."
18. **The Psalms name you and stretch you.** They "put words to where I'm already at," and also give "a vocabulary for language that I don't speak as well" — the brooder learning to rejoice (Reed's friends Rocky/Noah at CCF), the rejoicer learning to lament.
19. **Brent on skipping the uncomfortable verses.** A concert that performed Psalm 139 but "skipped over the part where... I hate the enemies." "In the Bible, it's right there... in the mix with the rejoicing and with the awe and wonder." Reed's self-examination: don't let it become "a for me and not for thee thing." (They note a later-season Psalm 139 episode.)
20. **Are the Psalms theological?** Both-and, not either-or. Not "a cleanly systematic... diagram of God." Ps 51 "conceived in sin" vs. Ps 139 "you knit me together"; "you hide your face" vs. "you hem me in behind and before" — held as lived tension. **God is not a subject of study but the hearer addressed**; even absence "produces argument, not... atheism."
21. **Brent + Michael Heiser caution.** From Marty: "you can't build a theology only on the Psalms... check it against Torah or some other part of the Bible." The psalmists are "connected to God in a way that is special... inspired," so there *is* theological insight — but Heiser's use of **Psalm 82** is an example of drawing carefully, "without any consideration for any other part of the biblical text" being the error. Reed adds: ask "what kind of psalm is this?" (in-the-feelings vs. stepping-back observation) before deciding how much weight to put on it.
22. **A discussion-group exercise.** Brent: "a good wrestling match for discussion groups" — take a few psalms and ask, "Is this orientation? Is it disorientation?" (or any other framework).
23. **Why "Songs of Praise," after all that.** Reed's closing thesis: "maybe to bring anything honestly to God is an act of praise" — a commitment "to hang on to God as God... no matter what." Not the "this is fine" dog in the fire; not "cheap sentimentality"; not "lazy cynicism" either. Over 150 psalms, "real relationship... easy and hard, good and bad... honestly acknowledged and brought to God, is a kind of praise."
24. **"The appropriate dialogue partner for every season."** Reed quotes Derek (CCF): keep the conversation going — "Don't hang up." The Cincinnati church story: a service that "left a space" for people to voice what was "crushing them," where Reed "sobbed, and... wept" — "this is what it is like to honestly engage wholeheartedly with a Psalm."
25. **Resources + sign-off.** Reed names the sources (Brueggemann, Peterson, Alter, Merton, the genre chart) and both urge discussion groups to bring a favorite psalm, place it in Brueggemann's framework, and "wrestle through this stuff."

## Terminology

- **Tehillim** (תְּהִלִּים, "Songs of Praise") — the Hebrew title of the book. Striking given its diversity; Reed's closing claim is that honest address to God in *any* register is itself praise. Reed: *"in Hebrew, the title of this book is Tehillim. Which means songs of praise."*
- **Psalm vs. Psalms (and not "chapters")** — grammar/counting note: "Psalm 22" (singular) for one; "the Psalms" for the book; the 150 are "not really chapters in any sense like a novel has chapters." Reed: *"it is Psalm 1, Psalm 2, not Psalms 1 and Psalms 2."*
- **Psalms of Orientation** (Brueggemann) — psalms expressing "a sense of things making sense... an order to things, a rightness," grounded in Creation (Ps 104) and Law (Ps 19, 119, 136). Reed: *"you know your directions, you know your bearings."*
- **Psalms of Disorientation** (Brueggemann) — psalms of "alienation from God... anger... complaining or lamenting." The signature question is "How long?" (Ps 13); also Ps 22, 88, 137. Reed: *"suddenly the compass isn't working and you're totally lost."*
- **Psalms of New Orientation** (Brueggemann) — "joy... gratitude... trust, but in a way that feels really earned," spoken from *beyond* "the pit" (Ps 30, 40, and the end of Ps 22). Reed: *"they know enough to know how it can go wrong... and they have also seen the rescue of God."*
- **"the pit"** — a recurring Psalter image for "dire straits... desperate disorientation" and the rescue out of it (Ps 30 "going down to the pit"; Ps 40 "the slimy pit"). Reed: *"They know what it is to be in the pit... and then to also have been rescued out of that."*
- **imprecatory psalms** — the vengeance psalms (Ps 137; the hatred language of Ps 139). Brueggemann's reading: voice the desire to God *in a psalm* so you don't enact it. Reed: *"I want vengeance, but here it is in a song and a prayer, and it can now be yours."*
- **Songs of Ascent** (Psalms 120–134) — a section "meant to serve a purpose of being recited or sung on the road... on the way to Jerusalem, up to the Temple" — the communal/liturgical function (vs. the deeply personal function). Reed: *"there's a whole section... from 120 to 134... sung on the road."*
- **the five books of the Psalter** — the Psalter has editorial "seams" dividing it into five books; **Psalms 1 and 2** (anonymous) serve as an introduction before the Davidic psalms begin at Psalm 3. Roughly half are Davidic. Reed: *"Psalms 1 and 2 were put there as like an introduction to the whole thing."*
- **the Psalter** — "as... fancy church people call it," the whole body of 150 psalms; "a kaleidoscope of expression... depression and... elation... wonder and... rage." Reed: *"the Psalms are like this kaleidoscope of expression."*

## Illustrations & Stories

- **The porch at dusk (Reed).** Orientation feels like "sitting on the porch... a beautiful evening... my glass of lemonade and the kids are playing in the yard and everything is right... God is good, his love endures forever... Everything's gonna continue going the way it should."
- **The spinning compass (Reed).** Disorientation: "it just feels like you're spinning and suddenly the compass isn't working and you're totally lost... Everything is spinning around."
- **Brent's "out in your field... anointed as king" (Brent & Reed).** David going from tending sheep to being anointed king as the picture of orientation ("Everything's going my way") — immediately undercut by "look what happens to David after that," his psalms of disorientation.
- **"There's an app for that" (Reed, reviving Marty's line).** Whatever season you're in, there's a psalm for it — "they come in as many flavors as there are human experiences and emotions."
- **U2's "40" (Reed/Brent).** Psalm 40 set to music by U2 — a new-orientation psalm, "He lifted me out of the slimy pit... set my feet on a rock."
- **The "this is fine" dog (Reed).** What honest praise is *not*: "the meme of that dog sitting there with all the building on fire... saying, 'This is fine.'" Praise "propped up on... cheap sentimentality" — contrasted with honest, hard-won praise.
- **The Psalm 139 concert that skipped the hard verses (Brent).** A performance of Psalm 139 that "skip[ped] over the part where... I hate the enemies" — the impulse to edit out discomfort that the Bible itself leaves "right there."
- **The Cincinnati church service (Reed).** After the death of his friend Josh, a Sunday service "left a space... for people to be able to voice specifically things that were... crushing them" — Reed "sobbed, and... wept": "this is what it is like to honestly engage wholeheartedly with a Psalm... hard, but it's also a relief."

## Discussion Questions

1. Brueggemann's three movements are Orientation, Disorientation, and New Orientation. Which one best describes your season right now — and can you name a specific psalm that gives it words?
2. Bring a favorite psalm to the group. Where does it land in Brueggemann's framework? Does any part of it make you uncomfortable enough that you'd be tempted to "skip those verses" the way the Psalm 139 concert did?
3. Psalm 88 ends in the dark with no resolution, and the hosts say "we don't have to rush to resolve these." How hard is it for you (or your community) to let a lament stay unresolved? What do we lose when we always demand a tidy ending?
4. Reed says the imprecatory psalms let you voice rage *to God in a psalm* "so that you then don't actually go out and do that in the real world." Is that a satisfying way to handle Psalm 137's violence? Where do you take your own anger?
5. Reed argues the Psalms both "put words to where I'm already at" and "give me a vocabulary for language that I don't speak as well." Are you more naturally a lamenter or a rejoicer — and which psalms might stretch you into the register you avoid?
6. In Session 1 the question was whether the human partner would say *yes*; in Session 2 the partner is faltering toward exile. What does it mean to you that the honest cry "How long, Lord?" — and even God's apparent *absence* — are treated here as part of the partnership rather than its end?
7. Reed's thesis is that "to bring anything honestly to God is an act of praise." Does that expand or unsettle your definition of worship? In what ways do your gatherings "honor this diverse experience" — or squeeze everything "through one tiny pinhole of... it's all good or it's all bad"?

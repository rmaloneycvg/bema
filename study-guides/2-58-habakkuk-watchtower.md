**Session:** 2 (The Prophets)

**Primary text:** The whole book of Habakkuk — [Habakkuk 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Habakkuk+1&version=NIV), [Habakkuk 2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Habakkuk+2&version=NIV), [Habakkuk 3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Habakkuk+3&version=NIV)

## Where This Sits in the One Story

Session 1 built the picture of **partnership**: God rescues a people and invites them to become a kingdom of priests who join his restoration project. Session 2 turns to the **Prophets**, and the arc of this stage is the covenant partner's (Israel's) **unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Habakkuk sits "dead center" in the prophets — the first of three Babylonian-period prophets, after two full periods (pre-Assyrian: Amos, Hosea, Micah, 1 Isaiah; Assyrian: Jonah, Nahum, Zephaniah, 2 Isaiah) and after the opening Babylonian pair (Jeremiah the weeping prophet, Lamentations).

Where most prophets *receive* a word and *deliver* it, Habakkuk **argues back** — a structure of complaint, divine answer, renewed complaint from the watchtower, a fuller answer, and finally worship. The episode's quiet pivot is the realization that God's long indictment of Babylon's empire-building (chapter 2) echoes "word-for-word" the charges God leveled at *his own people* through every prior prophet. That is exactly this stage of the story: the unfaithful partner has become indistinguishable from the empire — and yet God does not quit. He grants Habakkuk a glimpse of his plan, and Habakkuk, staring at the coming destruction of his world, still ends in trust: "yet I will rejoice in the LORD." Relative to Session 1's partnership, this episode shows the partner humbled into recognizing its own guilt, and God refusing to let the story end in despair.

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **Framing and review** — Habakkuk as one of Marty's favorite prophets; locating the moment ("dead center") in the prophets; a full review of the prior images (Amos = plumb line/ripe fruit, Hosea = prostitute, Micah = judge, 1 Isaiah = vineyard, Jonah = potential, Nahum = diyn's two sides, Zephaniah = t'shuvah, 2 Isaiah = woe, Jeremiah = weeping, Lamentations = lament + hope / ACA).
2. **Habakkuk's first complaint (ch. 1)** — "How long, LORD?"; the wicked hem in the righteous; justice is perverted; Habakkuk digs in his heels like Abraham, the closest rival to Jonah in arguing with God.
3. **God's answer** — "I am raising up the Babylonians," described as ruthless, dreaded, a law to themselves; from Habakkuk's seat this *sharpens* the problem.
4. **Habakkuk's second complaint** — "Your eyes are too pure to look on evil"; why tolerate the treacherous and let the wicked swallow those more righteous?; the fish/dragnet image.
5. **The watchtower (2:1)** — "I will stand at my watch... I will wait to see what answer God will give me"; the gutsy chutzpah posture: that answer didn't satisfy me, so I'll wait.
6. **God's fuller answer (ch. 2)** — "Write down the revelation... though it lingers, wait for it"; "the righteous person will live by his faithfulness" (2:4); a cascade of woes fusing Source A and Source B; the "divine mic drop": "The LORD is in his holy temple; let all the earth be silent before him."
7. **Whose condemnation is it, really?** — the indictment of Babylon echoes God's charges against his own people; Habakkuk realizes he cannot point the finger; humbled.
8. **Genesis callback (2:14-15)** — Brent catches "the waters cover the sea" + drunkenness/nakedness as Flood + Noah-in-the-vineyard; God remaking creation.
9. **Habakkuk's response — the hymn (ch. 3)** — a complete change of posture: "LORD, I have heard of your fame... in wrath remember mercy"; knees tremble; why God sometimes grants a glimpse of the plan and sometimes does not (contrast Job).
10. **Exodus / David-and-Goliath callbacks** — "you came out to deliver your people," "you trampled the sea with your horses," "with his own spear you pierced his head"; Habakkuk walks through his people's history.
11. **Marty's life verse (3:17-19)** — "Though the fig tree does not bud... yet I will rejoice in the LORD"; uttered facing a dismal future, the opposite of prosperity gospel; the musical tag marks it as *art*, a song to get the people through.

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 2 arc — the covenant partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **The partner discovers it has become the empire it accuses.** Habakkuk begins certain of a clean line between "the righteous" (us) and "the wicked" (Babylon); God's answer dissolves that line, because the indictment of Babylon is the same indictment leveled at Israel. This is the unfaithfulness of the arc made personal.
   - *Marty:* "the condemnation here, if it is aimed at Babylon, sure echoes all of the exact same condemnation that God has leveled at His own people, and all the other prophets we've studied up to now."
   - *Marty:* "How can I point the finger at Babylon when that same empire building is what we were dedicating so much of our own life and our own struggle to."

2. **God refuses to quit by letting the partner argue — and then answering.** Habakkuk's chutzpah is not rebuked; God engages the complaint, invites the watchtower wait, and gives a fuller answer. God's refusal to quit includes a refusal to shut down the honest protest of his partner.
   - *Marty:* "A little chutzpah, a little gutsy for the prophet to take this position. God's going to respond."
   - *Marty:* "he starts with a complaint and God answers him. Habakkuk says, 'Yes, that doesn't cut it for me... I'm going to wait for an answer that satisfies me.'"

3. **The righteous live by faithfulness in the dark, not by having the future explained.** The arc's hope is not relief from exile but trust through it. Habakkuk is granted a glimpse and it makes him tremble; faithfulness is what carries the partner when the answer is Babylon.
   - *Marty:* "'the righteous person will live by his faithfulness.'"
   - *Marty:* "sometimes we think we want answers but we really don't realize what we're asking for... I believe that sometimes God keeps the future from us in order to enable us to walk into it."

4. **Trusting the story means worshiping while the world is being laid waste.** Habakkuk's closing is sung into certain catastrophe — this is the partner's faithfulness answering God's refusal to quit, not denial of the coming judgment.
   - *Marty:* "Habakkuk is staring at a dismal future when he utters these words. This isn't happy, prosperity gospel, pie in the sky. This is: Babylon is coming."
   - *Marty:* "Habakkuk has enough trust in the story that God's telling. He's able to catch a glimpse of the future, stare opposition in the face, and know that the story of God will get the last word."

5. **God's judgment is restoration — putting things back in their proper place.** The Genesis echoes reframe the coming destruction not as abandonment but as God doing "the thing that I do": remaking creation, as in the flood, so the earth can be "filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD."
   - *Marty:* "'I am doing the thing that I do. I am making creation new... When the world gets bad enough'... 'I put the things back in their proper place, Habakkuk.'"

## East/West Lens

Only categories genuinely engaged by this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Contending with God as faithful relationship (East).** Arguing, bargaining, and "digging in your heels" are treated as legitimate, even admirable prophetic postures — Habakkuk is placed alongside Abraham and Jonah rather than rebuked.
  - *Marty:* "you got this Habakkuk that reminds me of Abraham here a little bit like, 'Hey, I'm not just going to hear this and roll over. This doesn't seem right to me.'"

- **Trust the story over systematic answers (East).** Resolution comes not through a propositional explanation of theodicy but through being drawn into the ongoing story and trusting its author to "get the last word."
  - *Marty:* "Habakkuk has enough trust in the story that God's telling... know that the story of God will get the last word."

- **Mystery honored over full disclosure (East).** The episode leaves *why* God shows some people the plan and not others (Habakkuk yes, Job no) as an unresolved mystery to be lived with, not solved.
  - *Marty:* "I don't know why he does and why he doesn't, but Habakkuk gets a glimpse."

- **Scripture as art and song, not only proposition (East).** The closing is read as music meant to carry a community through suffering — truth communicated as art, not merely "words and ideas."
  - *Brent:* "This is what they sing in a group like, 'This sucks, but we're going to make it.'" / *Marty:* "This wasn't just words and ideas communicated. This is art."

- **Reading intertextually through the whole story (East).** Meaning is found by hearing a text echo earlier texts — Genesis, Exodus, David and Goliath — rather than reading Habakkuk in isolation.
  - *Marty:* "I wonder if he is walking through their history. I'm calling back at least to their history."

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension states the problem, tags its type, cites the transcript, links the passage, and gives a Hebraic resolution (or marks it open).

1. **Why would God answer "Stop the wicked!" by raising up an even more wicked nation?**
   - *Type:* Apparent contradiction / theodicy (the problem of evil).
   - *Problem:* Habakkuk protests that the wicked overpower the righteous; God's reply is to empower Babylon — the very wickedness Habakkuk is objecting to.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "God's answer is, 'Habakkuk, listen. I'm doing something that you wouldn't believe even if I told you. I am raising up the Babylonians.' ... if I'm in Habakkuk's shoes, I'm going, 'That's what I'm getting at! How could you be doing this?'"
   - *Passage:* [Habakkuk 1:5-6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Habakkuk+1%3A5-6&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The tension breaks open when Habakkuk realizes the indictment of Babylon is also the indictment of Israel — the "righteous vs. wicked" line he assumed does not hold. God's answer is not endorsement of Babylon but a glimpse that judgment is coming on *all* empire-building, his own people included, in service of restoration.

2. **How can "your eyes are too pure to look on evil" coexist with God tolerating the treacherous?**
   - *Type:* Apparent contradiction (theological).
   - *Problem:* Habakkuk confesses God cannot stand evil, then accuses God of silently watching the wicked "swallow up those more righteous than themselves."
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "'You can't stand evil, and yet you're going to just sit there and let this happen. I just don't understand.'"
   - *Passage:* [Habakkuk 1:13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Habakkuk+1%3A13&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* God's fuller answer (ch. 2) insists the revelation "awaits an appointed time... though it lingers, wait for it." God's apparent silence is not indifference but delay with purpose; "the righteous person will live by his faithfulness" in the gap between the complaint and the appointed time.

3. **Does Habakkuk's demand for a better answer presume too much?**
   - *Type:* Open interpretive question (posture of the prophet).
   - *Problem:* Habakkuk, dissatisfied with God's first reply, climbs the watchtower and refuses to leave until he gets an answer that "satisfies me" — a striking amount of nerve toward God.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "'Yes, that doesn't cut it for me. I'm going to sit right here in my watchtower... I'm going to wait for an answer that satisfies me.' ... A little chutzpah, a little gutsy for the prophet."
   - *Hebraic resolution:* **Open — dance with it.** The episode presents the chutzpah as faithful contending (like Abraham over Sodom) that God honors with a fuller answer, yet it also ends with Habakkuk humbled and trembling, "at odds to be able to comprehend... the mind and mission of God." Both the boldness and the humbling are held together without being resolved into a rule.

4. **Why does God grant Habakkuk a glimpse of the plan but not Job (or others)?**
   - *Type:* Open interpretive question (mystery).
   - *Problem:* Some "main characters in the text" are left completely in the dark; Habakkuk is given a glimpse; the episode offers no principle for why.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "I don't know why God doesn't tell Job, he doesn't tell some people, some really main characters in the text. Sometimes he does it, sometimes he doesn't. I don't know why he does and why he doesn't."
   - *Passage:* [Habakkuk 3:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Habakkuk+3%3A2&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* **Open — dance with it.** Marty names the mystery directly and reframes it pastorally: God may keep the future hidden "in order to enable us to walk into it," since knowing what waits might make us "run for our lives." The *why* is left unresolved; the invitation is to trust rather than to explain.

## Terminology

- **Watchtower** — the image for Habakkuk; the posture of standing on the ramparts to wait for God's answer (2:1), rendered in a translation Marty loved as "I will climb up in my watchtower and station myself on the wall."
  - *Marty:* "'I will climb up in my watchtower and station myself on the wall. I will wait to see what answer God will give me.'"
- **chutzpah** — gutsy nerve/audacity; the quality of Habakkuk's refusal to accept God's first answer and his insistence on waiting for a satisfying one.
  - *Marty:* "A little chutzpah, a little gutsy for the prophet to take this position."
- **"the righteous will live by his faithfulness" (2:4)** — the famous line at the heart of God's fuller answer; faithfulness, not explanation, is how the righteous endure the gap before the appointed time.
  - *Marty (reading):* "'for the righteous person will live by his faithfulness.'"
- **shigionoth** (3:1) — an obscure heading, probably a literary or musical direction, whose meaning even scholars cannot define.
  - *Brent:* "On shigionoth... whatever that is." / *Marty:* "Your guess is as good as mine... they don't even know either."
- **t'shuvah** (recalled in review) — "to return"; often translated "repent," better understood as making a U-turn back to the original path. Carried over from Zephaniah in the prophet review.
  - *Brent:* "To return." / *Marty:* "making a U-turn and coming back to the original place where you started."
- **Source A / Source B** — the running interpretive test: idolatry/morality (A) versus injustice/empire (B); Habakkuk 2's woes fuse both.
  - *Marty:* "It's got some idolatry; it's got plenty of talk about how you treat people, how you build your houses with unjust gain."
- **ACA — alphabet chiastic acrostic** (recalled in review) — the structural label for Lamentations' lament-with-a-nugget-of-hope, named again in the prophet recap.
  - *Brent:* "The alphabet chiastic acrostic."

## Illustrations & Images

- **The watchtower / rampart wait.** Habakkuk climbing his watchtower to station himself on the wall and refuse to come down until God answers — the governing image of the book.
  - *Marty:* "'I'm going to sit right here in my watchtower. I'm going to wait for you to answer my question, because you gave me a dodgy answer.'"
- **God's "divine mic drop."** After the long cascade of woes, God simply says, "The LORD is in his holy temple; let all the earth be silent before him" — a conversation-ending gesture.
  - *Marty:* "God just closes with his own divine mic drop here. The LORD is in his holy temple. Let all the earth be silent before him."
- **The fishhook and dragnet (1:14-17).** Babylon hauling up peoples like fish with hooks and nets, then sacrificing to his net — Habakkuk's image for predatory empire that worships its own power.
  - *Brent (reading):* "The wicked foe pulls all of them up with hooks, he catches them in his net... Therefore he sacrifices to his net and burns incense to his dragnet."
- **"The stones of the wall will cry out."** The built-up house of unjust gain testifies against its builder — the materials of empire become witnesses.
  - *Marty (reading):* "The stones of the wall will cry out, and the beams of the woodwork will echo it."
- **Though the fig tree does not bud (3:17).** The barren farm — no figs, no grapes, no olives, no sheep, no cattle — as the concrete picture of total loss over which Habakkuk still chooses to rejoice.
  - *Marty:* "Though the fig tree does not bud and there are no grapes on the vines, though the olive crop fails... yet I will rejoice in the Lord."
- **A song for hard times.** The musical tag ("for the director of music, on my stringed instruments") reframes the ending as communal art — what you sing together to survive.
  - *Brent:* "the songs are what get them through these hard times, right?" / "This is what they sing in a group like, 'This sucks, but we're going to make it.'"

## Discussion Questions

1. Habakkuk assumes a clean line between "the righteous" (us) and "the wicked" (Babylon) — and God's answer dissolves it. Where might we be drawing a confident line between ourselves and the "empire" we accuse, only to find the same indictment lands on us?
2. God does not rebuke Habakkuk's chutzpah; he engages the complaint and invites the watchtower wait. What would it look like to bring your own honest, heel-digging protest to God as an act of faithfulness rather than a failure of it?
3. "The righteous will live by his faithfulness" (2:4) describes trust in the gap between the complaint and the "appointed time." Where are you currently waiting on a watchtower for an answer that satisfies — and what does faithfulness look like in the meantime?
4. Marty says "God keeps the future from us in order to enable us to walk into it." Do you experience the hiddenness of the future as mercy or as abandonment — and how does Habakkuk's trembling-yet-trusting posture speak to that?
5. Habakkuk sings "yet I will rejoice in the LORD" while staring at the certain loss of everything — "no grapes on the vines... no cattle in the stalls." What is the difference between this and prosperity-gospel optimism, and what song gets *you* through hard times?

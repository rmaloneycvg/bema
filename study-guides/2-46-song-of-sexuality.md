# Song of Sexuality

BEMA 46 · Session 2
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/2-46-song-of-sexuality.md`
Book: Song of Songs 1:1-2 (read); 1 Corinthians 7:8-9; 1 Timothy 4:1-5 (brought in from the New Testament). The transcript prints the NET; Brent reads aloud from the NIV.
Host: Marty Solomon · Co-host: Brent Billings · (A **reboot** of the 2017 original episode — Marty revisits and "grounds" his earlier take.)
Core move throughout: Song of Songs is wisdom literature not because *sex* is the wisdom, but because of *everything behind* sex — **intimacy** — "everything that sex can teach us, not just in those relationships, but about all relationships."

> **Session 2 overarching arc: the prophets — the partner's (Israel's) unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Session 1 built the *partnership*: God marrying a people at Sinai, "you create the space and I'll fill it," the tabernacle as the "honeymoon suite." Session 2 lives in the long collapse of that partnership — the monarchy spinning out, "empire spinning out of control." This episode sits in the *wisdom* detour inside that era: with the narrative paused on David, Solomon, and the kings, the hosts open the toolbox the people need "while you're walking this path of crazy dysfunction." This guide advances the one story by naming what God's refusal to quit *looks like intimately*: the rabbinic tradition hears Song of Songs as the allegory of the **communion** between God and his people — "the marriage at Sinai," the tabernacle where God "chooses to dwell with, to approach, to draw near." Where Session 1 asked whether the partner would say *yes* to the covenant romance, this episode holds up the picture of that romance itself — erotic poetry *and* divine allegory — precisely in the age when the human partner is failing, as a reminder that the relationship God wants was never a contract but a marriage He will not abandon.

## Key Lessons

- **Song of Songs is wisdom literature because the wisdom is *intimacy*, not the act of sex.** Marty: *"the act of sex is not the wisdom. It's everything that lies behind that. It's everything that sex can teach us, not just in those relationships, but about all relationships. And that is the place of intimacy."*
- **The mature reading holds *both* the erotic poetry *and* the allegory — refusing both the "la-la-la, don't talk about it" dodge and a flat "it's only about sex" reductionism.** Marty names his own 2017 over-correction: *"what I missed the first time around is the beauty that does lie in the allegory... Okay, I see the sex, and now I go kind of beyond that to say, what is the allegory, what is the intimacy?"*
- **We are sexual beings, and refusing to say so manufactures dysfunction.** Marty: *"We are sexual beings... whether we're married or not... To not acknowledge this... is to set us up for dysfunction."*
- **The church's failure to teach sexuality well leaves "an untouched oasis of misunderstanding."** By only ever litigating the moral code ("what's allowed, what's not allowed") and purity culture, we abandon youth to assemble scraps from media and peers. Marty (reprising his own phrase): *"It leads to an untouched oasis of misunderstanding."*
- **A healthy romance needs all three Hebrew loves working together — rayah, ahava, and dowd.** Via Rob Bell's NOOMA: feeling (*rayah*), commitment (*ahava*), and erotic love (*dowd*). Marty: *"All three of these show up in a healthy romantic relationship... They're designed to work together."* The bonfire image: three small flames become one blaze.
- **Different cultures order the loves differently — and ours is not obviously "figured out."** Ancient arranged marriage: *ahava* → *dowd* → *rayah*. Modern West: *rayah* → *ahava* → (meant to wait for) *dowd*. Hookup culture: *dowd* + *rayah* with no *ahava* — "empty... destructive." Marty: *"I don't think we would look at our culture and be like: I'll tell you what, we have nailed the whole sexual relationship thing."*
- **Make sure the wedding is *rooted in ahava, not rayah*.** Western weddings "scream *rayah*" (colors, candles, photography, DJ) — which is fine — "but the wedding is about *ahava*," commitment. Marty: *"We don't need less rayah. We just need to make sure that the wedding isn't rooted in rayah."*
- **Scripture affirms *both* marriage and singleness; forbidding either is called "teachings of demons."** 1 Corinthians 7 honors celibacy as "a cool calling" *and* says "better to marry than to burn"; 1 Timothy 4 brands forbidding marriage and foods demonic because "everything God created is good." Marty: *"The wisdom of Song of Songs is not just for the married."*
- **Our ethic must *grow*, not change — disciple the "why" of the struggle, not just "don't."** As culture pushed marriage later (Industrial/educational/economic shifts), the church stopped being honest about the resulting struggle. Marty: *"We're not changing our ethic. We are growing in our ethic with the world around us"* — toward "the ethics of human flourishing and human dignity."
- **This conversation is personal and pastoral — and it is why shame flourishes when we stay silent.** Marty names his own teenage struggle and the purity-culture silence around it: *"nobody discipled me in why does this struggle exist?... It leads to shame. It leads to shame whenever we make a mistake."*

## East/West Lens

*(Only categories the episode actually engages, each with a citation.)*

- **Hebrew words name textures of love the Greek/English flatten (Eastern concreteness vs. Western abstraction).** Where "love" is one blurry English word, Hebrew distinguishes *rayah* (feeling/infatuation), *ahava* (committed covenant), and *dowd* (erotic). Marty (via Rob Bell): *"The Hebrew language does similar things. There are three Hebrew words for love."*
- **Story and canon read as a unified whole with long echoes (Eastern intertext vs. Western proof-text).** The marital imagery of Song of Songs is heard against "all this Genesis language" ("a man leaves his father and mother and becomes united to his wife"), the Sinai "marriage," and the tabernacle — one continuous romance. Marty: *"All this Genesis language, it's not just trying to talk about only marriage. It's talking about a bigger picture of humanity."*
- **Intimacy/communion as the center of theology (Eastern relational frame vs. Western transactional one).** Marty leans on Collin Cornell's *God Draws Near*: the story is not *only* mission/repair but **communion** — "the co-union, the communion relationship between God and his people," grounded in the tabernacle *and* Song of Songs. Brent: *"we talk about the marriage at Sinai, God marrying his people in the desert."*
- **Humanity as embodied and relational by design (Eastern wholeness vs. Western compartmentalizing).** Sexuality is one true dimension of a whole person — "physical... emotional... relational... psychological... spiritual beings. We're also sexual beings" — so denying it fractures the person. Marty: *"Humanity is about connection because that's what God did when he made us."*
- **Wisdom as a lived toolbox for the whole community, not abstract theory (Eastern practice vs. Western system).** Judaism "doesn't typically have a category for wisdom literature" even with a rich wisdom tradition; the books function as tools — songs, wise sayings, relationships/intimacy — "to live well in the midst of our story." Marty: *"wisdom is like a toolbox that we're going to take with us."*
- **Power read as two-handed (Eastern "right-handed vs. left-handed power").** In Marty's two-lovers reading, Solomon's imperial "right-handed power" — wine, palace, perfume — *cannot* win a heart; the shepherd can. Marty: *"Song of Songs could be Solomon riffing on the limitations of imperial right-handed power when it comes to romantic love."*

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Framing the taboo.** Brent introduces "the under-addressed book called Song of Songs" and "the ramifications of avoiding the topic of sexuality"; the episode is titled "The Song of Sexuality." Marty: "We don't have a presentation today."
2. **Where this sits — the Ketuvim and the "five scrolls."** Marty locates Song of Songs in the *Ketuvim*, in the *Hamesh Megillot* (the five scrolls); notes Judaism doesn't really use the "wisdom literature" label though it has a rich wisdom tradition. Wisdom is a **toolbox** (Psalms = songs; Proverbs = wise sayings generally true; now **relationships/intimacy**) for living well while "empire is spinning out of control."
3. **Reboot disclosure.** Marty flags this as a 2017 reboot: he was 2017 Marty with a different "level of angst" (kids then 7 and 5, now 17 and 15, a son with a girlfriend); he aims to "clean up... some of the rough edges" and be "more grounded."
4. **The thesis — the wisdom is intimacy, not the act of sex.** "The act of sex is not the wisdom. It's everything that lies behind that... the place of intimacy."
5. **Correcting his earlier over-correction — allegory *and* erotic poetry.** The immature dodge ("it's just a metaphor") is wrong; so is his old flat "it's only about sex." The mature move holds both: see the sex, then "go beyond that" to the rabbinic allegory of intimacy — "God's relationship between himself and his people."
6. **Brent: the marriage at Sinai.** Brent connects to "God marrying his people in the desert"; Marty confirms the tabernacle as the Session-1 "honeymoon suite."
7. **Collin Cornell's *God Draws Near*.** Marty's grad-school project book: a critique of "theology of mission" arguing the story is also **communion** (co-union, intimacy) — grounded in the tabernacle *and*, "wouldn't you know it," Song of Songs. (Brent supplies Cornell's degrees — Emory PhD, Princeton MDiv, Columbia BA.)
8. **Not neglecting the erotica.** "This is very much erotic poetry." "There is a place to discuss and appreciate the beauty of sexuality in its appropriate context" — a book of erotic poetry inspired in the canon.
9. **How the church fails the topic.** We engage sexuality almost only as a *moral code* and *purity culture* (the "wilted rose" that "done so much damage"); we rarely teach healthy marital intimacy, self-sacrifice, serving in that context.
10. **"An untouched oasis of misunderstanding."** Marty reprises his OG phrase: silence leaves kids/youth/young adults to assemble scraps from social media, the internet, peers, and media — "it's not good." He names the career risk of getting this "wrong" in Christian subculture.
11. **A sexually charged book — young boys weren't allowed to read it.** "Her... opening the gate and letting her love into her garden" would "make most anybody blush." "Young Jewish boys were not allowed to read this book... Yes, that is what it means."
12. **Hypersexualized → hypersensitive → shame.** Our culture's hypersexualization makes us hypersensitive, breeding shame; even the "don't talk about mom and dad" joke reveals we can't discuss sexuality in healthy ways — including into one's 60s and 70s.
13. **We are sexual beings.** Alongside physical/emotional/relational/psychological/spiritual, "we're also sexual beings"; denying it "is to set us up for dysfunction."
14. **Recommended resources (updated for the reboot).** *Shame-Free Sex Talks* (Kelly Edmiston, NavPress) — the book he was asked to endorse and now plans to read with his teens; *The Great Sex Rescue* (Sheila Wray Gregoire, secondhand); *Sex God* and *The Zimzum of Love* (Rob Bell, the latter with Kristen Bell); and Rob Bell's **NOOMA** video — Brent's "entry point to Rob Bell and to the Hebrew language."
15. **The three Hebrew loves (NOOMA).** *rayah* (neighbor/friend; in romance, electric infatuation — "you hang up first"); *ahava* (committed covenant love); *dowd* ("uncle," but in erotic sense the sexual love of Song of Songs — "your *dowd* is sweeter than wine," 1:2). The bonfire: three small flames meant to go together into one blaze "seen miles away."
16. **The loves across cultures.** Ancient arranged marriage: *ahava* → *dowd* → *rayah*. Modern West: *rayah* → *ahava* → (meant to wait for) *dowd*. Hookup culture: *dowd* + *rayah*, no *ahava* — empty/destructive. The wedding "screams *rayah*" but should be "rooted in... *ahava*."
17. **Brent on compressed vs. multi-day weddings.** Spreading a wedding across days (as an ancient week-long ceremony did) makes it "more celebratory and more powerful" — even if impractical today.
18. **Reading Song of Songs 1:1-2.** Brent reads the opening two verses ("Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth, for your love is more delightful than wine"). Brent notes the "She" subtitle — the ascriptions "are debatable."
19. **The two-lovers theory (new to the reboot).** From a book sent to Marty: the woman is torn between **Solomon/the king** (imperial power — wine, palace, perfume) and a poor **shepherd** she actually loves — Solomon "riffing on the limitations of imperial right-handed power" to win a heart. Brent adds the NET's extensive footnote questioning whether it's even "the same young woman throughout."
20. **1 Corinthians 7:8-9.** Brent reads. Paul affirms the "beauty of celibacy / singleness" as "a cool calling" *and* that the loves belong together — "better to marry than to burn" / "if you lack self-control."
21. **1 Timothy 4:1-5.** Brent reads the "teachings of demons" passage: forbidding marriage and certain foods is demonic because "everything God created is good." So Song of Songs' wisdom "is not just for the married."
22. **Closing — grow the ethic, disciple the "why," end the shame.** Culture pushed marriage later (Industrial/educational/economic shifts) and "I don't think our honesty has moved with us." Our ethic must *grow* (human flourishing/dignity), not merely police "don't." Marty names his own teenage struggle and purity-culture shame, and why it matters for his 15-year-old son and how he treats the women in his life. "Song of Songs tells us that sexuality is good and beautiful and something to be celebrated... a tool in our toolbox."

## Terminology

- **Ketuvim / Hamesh Megillot** (כְּתוּבִים / חָמֵשׁ מְגִלּוֹת, "Writings" / "the Five Scrolls") — the third section of the Tanakh and the set of five scrolls within it where Song of Songs sits. Judaism "doesn't typically have a category for wisdom literature" even with a rich wisdom tradition. Marty: *"This shows up in what's called the Hamesh Megillot, the five scrolls."*
- **wisdom literature as "toolbox"** — the Psalms (songs of orientation/disorientation), Proverbs ("wise sayings that are generally true"), and Song of Songs (relationships/intimacy) are tools for living well amid the chaotic narrative. Marty: *"wisdom is like a toolbox that we're going to take with us that we know how to live well in the midst of our story."*
- **rayah** (רֵעָה, "neighbor / friend") — in a romantic context, the electric *infatuation* love — feeling, poetry, electricity. Marty: *"It's the infatuation love. It's the love that says: you hang up first... that's the rayah."*
- **ahava** (אַהֲבָה) — *committed* covenant love: "I don't care what happens and I don't care how I feel. I'm going to be here for you." Marty: *"ahava is about commitment, but rayah is about feeling."*
- **dowd** (דּוֹד, also "uncle") — the *erotic* love of Song of Songs; the same word means the act of sexualized love. Marty: *"dowd, that's the erotic love of Song of Songs... Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth for your dowd is greater than wine."* (Song of Songs 1:2.)
- **the three loves working together** — a healthy romance needs all three (*rayah*, *ahava*, *dowd*); cultures order them differently (ancient *ahava→dowd→rayah*; modern *rayah→ahava→dowd*; hookup *dowd+rayah*, no *ahava*). Marty: *"They're designed to work together."*
- **"right-handed power"** — imperial, coercive power (wealth, palace, perfume) that, in Marty's two-lovers reading, *cannot* win a heart. Marty: *"Song of Songs could be Solomon riffing on the limitations of imperial right-handed power when it comes to romantic love."*
- **"honeymoon suite" / the marriage at Sinai** — the Session-1 reading of the tabernacle as the place God "chooses to dwell... to draw near, and have intimate relationship." Marty: *"We even called it in Session 1, the honeymoon suite."*
- **communion (co-union)** — Collin Cornell's term (not the Eucharist): the intimacy/relationship between God and his people as the "center of gravity" of theology. Marty: *"he means the co-union, the communion relationship between God and his people."*
- **"an untouched oasis of misunderstanding"** — Marty's own phrase for what the church's silence on sexuality produces. Marty: *"Because we don't deal with it, we are left... It is truly an untouched oasis of misunderstanding."*

## Illustrations & Stories

- **Rob Bell's bonfire and lighter (NOOMA).** Bell flips a lighter for each of the three loves (*rayah*, *ahava*, *dowd*), pours gasoline on a huge pile of timber, and at the end drops the lighter so the whole thing "goes up in a pretty big fire... seen by miles and miles away" — the picture of romantic love when all three loves come together. Marty narrates it as the episode's governing image.
- **The "wilted rose" of purity culture.** Marty's recurring frustration: a youth-group lesson where "somebody brought in a wilted rose and said, that's what happens if you lose your virginity" — "it's just done so much damage to so many people because of the way we chose to talk about sexuality."
- **"You hang up first" (rayah).** The infatuation love dramatized: *"You hang up first. No, you hang up first... I love you more. No, I love you more"* — the electricity and romance of *rayah*.
- **The "don't talk about mom and dad" joke.** The cultural reflex ("I don't want to hear my mom and dad talk about that") exposes how we can't discuss sexuality in healthy ways — even the hope that aging parents still enjoy "a healthy marital, including sexual, relationship."
- **Compressed vs. multi-day weddings (Brent).** Our Western wedding crams everything into one *rayah*-soaked day; Brent, a wedding photographer, has seen multi-day ceremonies — like an "ancient wedding ceremony [that] would last like a week" — that are "more celebratory and how powerful."
- **"Shame-Free Sex Talks" vs. "Sex-Free Shame Talks" (slip of the tongue).** Marty's self-deprecating slip while recommending Kelly Edmiston's book — a light moment inside a heavy topic, and the book he now plans to read with his own teenagers.

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty says the wisdom of Song of Songs "is not the act of sex... It's everything that lies behind that... the place of intimacy." Where in your own relationships (not only romantic) has intimacy taught you something you couldn't have learned any other way?
2. Marty names two failure modes: the immature "la-la-la, I don't want to talk about it" dodge, and a flat "it's only about sex" reduction. Which one have you tended toward, and what would holding *both* the erotic poetry and the allegory ask of you?
3. The three Hebrew loves — *rayah* (feeling), *ahava* (commitment), *dowd* (erotic) — are "designed to work together." Looking at a relationship you care about (or a cultural pattern you've watched), which love is over-weighted and which is thin?
4. "The wedding isn't rooted in rayah... the wedding is about ahava." If you've been to or planned a wedding, where did the celebration's center of gravity actually sit — and does it matter?
5. Marty calls the church's silence "an untouched oasis of misunderstanding" that leaves people to assemble scraps from media and peers. What would it look like, in your community, to disciple *the "why" of the struggle* rather than only repeat "don't"?
6. 1 Corinthians 7 and 1 Timothy 4 affirm *both* singleness and marriage, and call forbidding either "teachings of demons." How does hearing Song of Songs' wisdom as "not just for the married" change the way you'd teach or receive this book?
7. In Session 1 the question was whether the partner would say *yes* to the covenant romance; here, in the age of the failing partner, the rabbis still hear Song of Songs as the allegory of God's intimacy with his people. What does it mean to you that God frames the relationship as a *marriage He won't abandon* rather than a contract?

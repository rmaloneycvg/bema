# Six or Seven?

BEMA 127 · Session 3
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/3-127-six-or-seven.md`
Book: Matthew (Matthew 23:1-39) with Isaiah (Isaiah 5:8-23)
Host: Marty Solomon · Co-host: Brent Billings

> **Session 3 overarching arc: the king/Messiah embodying the story.** We are "in the middle of the last week of Jesus's life" (Marty), and the plot against Jesus is tightening — the leaders "tried to set a trap for Jesus... in his own words," and now "No one dared to ask him any more questions." Instead of defending himself, Jesus goes "on the offensive" with the seven woes of Matthew 23. This episode advances the one story by showing the Messiah *reading Israel's own Scripture back to its teachers* as both indictment and invitation: he takes the **six** woes of Isaiah 5 (which describe a person of **greed**) and deliberately gives **seven** (describing a person of **self-righteousness**), using the Hebraic number for completion to say they have "completed/perfected the art of sin." Where Session 1 established the **partnership** (God seeking a people to "be the message") and Session 2 chronicled **the partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit** (Isaiah's vineyard and its six woes of greed), Session 3's king now stands in the temple in his final week and names the deadliest form of that same unfaithfulness — a counterfeit God put on display by those who "claim to speak for God." The hen longing to gather her chicks is the embodied grief of a God who still "would have none of it" refused.

## Key Lessons

- **Jesus affirms the Pharisees before he critiques them — "do what they say, just don't do what they do."** The woes are not a wholesale rejection of Pharisaic devotion. Marty: *"Jesus does affirm... the place of these Pharisaic teachers within Jewish society... he affirms their devotion to obedience... the soundness of their teaching along with their commitment to the Text."* The problem is their **halakhah** — how they *walk out* the Text — and how they load burdens on people while displaying their righteousness for approval.
- **"Moses' seat" means: listen to the Text they read, not the way they live it.** Because the Moses' seat is where the one reading Scripture sits, "do what they say" = *"Do what the Text says"* (Brent). Marty: *"listen to their teaching because they teach on the Text... but do not interpret the Text with your life in the same way that they are interpreting the Text with their life."*
- **Biblical "hypocrisy" (*hupocritos*) means *actor*, not merely two-faced — you can do exactly what you preach and still be a hypocrite.** Marty: *"This word, hupocritos, hypocrite, literally translates 'actor'... You can be saying one thing and doing exactly... what you say, and you can still be a hypocrite... because you're being an actor."* The sin is performance for an audience, not inconsistency.
- **The woes are weighted toward *people*, not ritual — "justice, mercy and faithfulness" are the weightier matters.** Jesus's complaint is their "interaction, expectation, and dealings with other people" (Marty): shutting the door of the kingdom, making converts "twice as much a child of hell," swearing-loopholes, tithing mint while neglecting justice.
- **Don't throw out the good with the bad — "do the latter without neglecting the former."** Marty: *"It's not that you shouldn't do the other. You should actually do the latter without neglecting the former. You should do both... we have a tendency to reject what is good. Throw the baby out with the bathwater. That's not right either."*
- **Jesus deliberately echoes Isaiah 5 — six woes of greed — and adds a seventh.** The Jews listening would "count things when they hear repetition" (Brent) and recognize the Isaiah pattern. Isaiah's **six** woes describe **greed**; six is the number of "a sinful man." Marty: *"Jesus takes the six woes of Isaiah and he makes them seven... What is he saying rabbinically? You have perfected — you have completed the art of evil and sin."*
- **Self-righteousness is worse than greed, because it puts a *counterfeit God* on display.** Marty: *"Do you know what is even worse than a greedy person?... a self-righteous person... These people claim to speak for God, yet they put a completely counterfeit version of God on display... at least people know greed when they see it."*
- **"Go ahead then and complete what your ancestors started" is the point of the seventh woe.** The better translation of "fill up the measure of your fathers" is a command: *"Complete what your ancestors have started"* (Marty/Brent). By building the prophets' tombs with the same mentality that killed them, this generation finishes the family work of murdering the righteous — "from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah."
- **The warning turns on us: the justice-minded generation can commit an even greater sin.** Marty: *"I belong to a generation bent on restoring justice... we can be in danger of an even greater sin... we must be very careful not to miss the pride that lurks just under the surface of our self-righteousness."*

## East/West Lens

- **Counting and gematria as meaning-bearing (Hebraic).** The whole episode turns on *how many* woes — a Jewish audience "count[s] things when they hear repetition" (Brent), and six vs. seven carries theological weight (sinful man vs. completion). Meaning rides on the number, not a propositional statement. Marty: *"he gets done with six and adds another one... 'Oh, he just added a woe.'"*
- **Knowing/obedience as *walk* — halakhah over abstract belief (Hebraic).** The critique is aimed at how the Pharisees *walk out* the Text with their lives, not at their doctrine; righteousness is a lived path, not a creed. Marty: *"it's that halakhah, it's that it is the way that you walk out the Text."*
- **Word-study anchored in the ancient sense, against the modern colloquial sense (Hebraic vs. Western).** The hosts explicitly correct the modern English use of "hypocrite" (two-faced) with the biblical *hupocritos* (actor). Marty: *"that's not what the word hypocrite means biblically at all."*
- **Reading Jesus intertextually — the Gospel woes only land when heard *through* Isaiah (Hebraic).** Jesus is not inventing a genre; he is quoting Israel's prophetic Scripture and extending it. Marty: *"This could be in the Text. In fact, it is, because somebody has had a list of woes already. Isaiah, all the way back in Isaiah 5."*
- **Avoiding presentism while letting the text indict the present (lens discipline).** Marty applies the woe to his own tradition and his own "generation bent on restoring justice," refusing to make it only a first-century problem: *"Christianity, evangelicalism as a whole is full of Pharisaical hypocrites."*

## Recurring Through-lines

- **The anti-story / empire of greed (Session 2 → Session 3).** The six woes of Isaiah describe the people who "bought into the wrong narrative... trying to build their own empires... getting wealthy and rich on the backs of the marginalized, the oppressed, the outsider, the foreigner" (Marty). Brent: *"they're pushing out the outsider and the foreigner — 'Add house to house and join field to field till no space is left and you live alone in the land.'"* The episode carries Session 2's unfaithfulness motif straight into the Messiah's final-week confrontation.
- **The vineyard (callback to Session 2).** Jesus's woes are deliberately mounted on Isaiah 5, "right after the vineyard passage" (Marty) — the song of the vineyard that defined Israel's failure in Session 2 is the exact Scripture the king now completes.
- **"Be the message, not just bring it" / counterfeit vs. true witness.** The deadly thing about self-righteousness is that teachers who "claim to speak for God" put "a completely counterfeit version of God on display to the world around them" (Marty) — the dark mirror of Session 1's vocation to *be* a people who show the world what God is like.
- **Servanthood as the shape of greatness (kingdom ethic).** Against titles and the chief seats, "The greatest among you will be your servant... this is about people being the servant of all" (Marty) — the king embodying and commanding the upside-down kingdom.
- **Completion / sevenfold fullness (Hebraic number-theology).** Seven as completion is the hinge of the episode: by adding the seventh woe Jesus declares the "art of sin" perfected — the same symbolic grammar the series uses for divine fullness, here turned to name a finished evil.
- **The prophets killed and still being killed (prophetic through-line).** "Some of them you will kill and crucify... from the blood of righteous Abel to the blood of Zechariah" (Brent, reading Jesus) — Israel's long history of rejecting the sent ones, now about to culminate in the rejection of the sent Son during this very week.

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Cold open & book recommendation.** Brent frames the episode (the list of woes; "what kind of person Jesus is describing"); Marty places it "in the middle of the last week of Jesus's life" and recommends *The Last Week* by Borg and Crossan — praising the scholarship while flagging his discomfort with the Jesus Seminar's textual criticism.
2. **Set-up from the previous episode.** The leaders "tried to set a trap for Jesus... in his own words"; Jesus's final brain-teaser left them unable to ask more questions, so he goes "on the offensive" in Matthew 23.
3. **Reading Matthew 23:1-12 (Moses' seat, burdens, titles).** Brent reads the opening; the Pharisees sit in Moses' seat, tie up heavy loads, do deeds "to be seen," love the best seats and the title "Rabbi."
4. **Jesus affirms the Pharisees first.** Marty: Jesus affirms their devotion to obedience, soundness of teaching, and commitment to the Text; the problem is their *dealings with people* and their display of righteousness for approval.
5. **"Moses' seat" unpacked via the synagogue episode.** Callback to the "seven elements" of the synagogue; the Moses' seat is where the Text-reader sits, so "do what they say" = *do what the Text says*; "don't do what they do" = don't follow their halakhah.
6. **Brent's Catholic-background aside on "call no one Father."** A pastoral detour: the point is not a blanket ban on titles like Reverend or Father, but servanthood over status — "Rabz" vs. "Rabbi with a capital R." Marty and Brent agree most priests aren't "angling for a position."
7. **Reading the woes (Matthew 23:13-31).** Brent reads the woes: shutting the kingdom, converts "twice as much a child of hell," swearing by gold/temple/altar, tithing mint-dill-cumin while neglecting justice-mercy-faithfulness, clean-outside-of-the-cup, whitewashed tombs, building the prophets' tombs.
8. **Defining *hupocritos* = "actor."** Marty corrects the modern sense; you can do exactly what you preach and still be an actor; "evangelicalism as a whole is full of Pharisaical hypocrites" with a tidy bow on their theology, all "done for a show."
9. **Commentary on each woe.** Shutting the door; doubling down on converts' evil; the vow-loophole system (swearing by the gold to escape a prior vow); tithe perfectionism that neglects the weightier matters ("do both"); the Talmudic cleaning-the-inside point; whitewashed tombs (nothing more unclean than a corpse); building tombs for prophets with the very mentality that killed them.
10. **"What will a Jew hear?" — the Isaiah pattern.** Marty leads Brent to recognize they've "heard a list of woes before" — Isaiah 5, "right after the vineyard passage."
11. **Reading Isaiah 5:8-23 (the six woes).** Brent reads Isaiah's woes; the listener is told to "compare the content" to Jesus's woes.
12. **The stick-figure exercise — Isaiah = greed.** Marty's classroom exercise: without fail students describe "a greedy person"; Session 2 context — the anti-story, empire-building "on the backs of the marginalized."
13. **Working through Isaiah's woes as greed.** House-to-house land accumulation; rising early "to run after strong drink" = wealthy privilege (not alcoholism); drawing iniquity with cart-ropes = "I want to make more money"; calling evil good; champions at mixing drinks = throwing wealth-parties.
14. **The stick-figure exercise — Matthew = self-righteousness.** The second whiteboard figure: Brent moves from "corruption" to "legalism" to the diagnosis — a person focused on tithing mint while unable to judge whether they "love mercy." Marty names it: **self-righteousness**.
15. **The count: six vs. seven.** Isaiah has **six** woes (6 = "a sinful man"); Jesus has **seven** (7 = completion). The Pharisees are counting; Jesus adds the seventh deliberately — "You have completed the art of evil and sin."
16. **"Go ahead then and complete what your ancestors started."** Brent flags the line he'd left off; the old NIV "fill up the measure of your fathers" is better rendered as a command. The testimony against themselves (descendants of prophet-murderers) seals it.
17. **Why self-righteousness is worse than greed.** It puts a counterfeit God on display; "at least people know greed when they see it." The seventh woe says the fathers' sin is now completed.
18. **Reading Matthew 23:33-36 (snakes, brood of vipers, Abel to Zechariah).** Brent reads the sending of prophets/sages/teachers who will be killed and crucified; the righteous blood from Abel to Zechariah comes on "this generation."
19. **Marty's self-application.** The justice-minded generation can commit "an even greater sin"; repentant of greed, we must watch "the pride that lurks just under the surface of our self-righteousness"; "see all people, help and serve all people, and pursue humility."
20. **Reading Matthew 23:37-39 (Jerusalem, the hen and her chicks).** Brent reads the lament: "how often I have longed to gather your children together as a hen gathers her chicks... and you were not willing... your house is left to you desolate."
21. **Marty's closing testimony & sign-off.** Raised in committed fundamentalist evangelicalism that "taught me how to love the Bible... love Jesus," Marty resolves "not to perfect the art of sin. I do not want to complete what my fathers have started." "Seven woes of Matthew."

## Terminology

- **Moses' seat** — the synagogue chair where the one reading the Text that day sits (one of the "seven elements" of the synagogue). Hence "do what they say" means "do what the *Text* says." Brent: *"It's the place where whoever is reading the Text that day sits."* Marty: *"Do what the Text says, because they're reading Text."*
- **halakhah** — "the way that you walk out the Text." Jesus affirms the Pharisees' teaching but critiques their halakhah — their lived interpretation. Marty: *"it's that halakhah, it's that it is the way that you walk out the Text... do not interpret the Text with your life in the same way that they are."*
- **hupocritos** (ὑποκριτής) — literally "actor"; a hypocrite performs righteousness "to be seen," so one can do exactly what one preaches and still be a hypocrite. Marty: *"This word, hupocritos, hypocrite, literally translates 'actor'... you're doing it to put on a show."*
- **phylacteries / tassels** — items worn conspicuously "wide" and "long" for display; the physical form of "everything they do is done for people to see." (Matthew 23:5.)
- **Rabbi / Father / Instructor (Messiah)** — titles not to be sought for status; "you have one Teacher," "one Father," "one instructor the Messiah." Marty's "Rabbi with a capital R" vs. "rabbi with a little r / Rabz" illustrates seeking vs. refusing honor.
- **six woes vs. seven woes** — Isaiah 5 lists **six** woes describing a person of **greed** (6 = "a sinful man"); Jesus gives **seven** describing a person of **self-righteousness** (7 = completion). By adding the seventh, Jesus says the "art of sin" is completed. Brent: *"Isaiah has six woes."* ... *"Seven."* Marty: *"he makes them seven... you have completed the art of evil and sin."*
- **justice, mercy, faithfulness** — "the more important matters of the law" neglected while tithing mint, dill, and cumin. Brent: *"how do you judge whether you're acting justly, loving mercy."* (Matthew 23:23.)
- **self-righteousness vs. greed** — the two "kinds of person" named by the stick-figure exercises: Isaiah's woeful person is greedy; Matthew's woeful person is self-righteous. Marty: *"If the woeful person in Isaiah is a person of greed, the woeful person in Matthew is a person of self-righteousness."*
- **"complete what your ancestors started"** — the preferred rendering of "fill up the measure of your fathers" (Matthew 23:32); read as a command sealing the seventh woe. Marty: *"Complete what your ancestors have started."*

## Illustrations & Stories

- **The two whiteboard stick figures (Marty's classroom exercise).** For Isaiah 5, "I draw a stick figure on the board... what person is being described... without fail, everybody articulates the same kind of person, and that is a greedy person." Then "we draw another stick figure on the other side of the whiteboard" for Matthew 23 — and the person described "has virtually nothing to do with greed and everything to do with" self-righteousness.
- **Counting the woes in real time (Marty).** The dramatized moment of a first-century Pharisee doing gematria on the fly: *"Some Pharisee was sitting there going, 'Oh dude.' They're expecting six... He gets done with six and adds another one... 'Oh, he just added a woe.'"*
- **"Rabbi with a capital R" vs. "Rabz" (Marty).** Marty tells his students not to introduce him as "Rabbi with a capital R," but "Rabz" is fine — a lived illustration of titles-as-servanthood vs. titles-as-status.
- **Brent's Catholic background and "call no one Father" (Brent).** A personal story of once using this verse "to step away from my prior Catholic faith," reconsidered: the priests he knew weren't "angling for a position, a title, or anything" — illustrating that the sin is sought status, not the title itself.
- **Cleaning the inside of the cup first (Marty, citing Jewish teaching).** "There's actually Jewish teaching that exists, I believe, in the Talmud... If you actually start by cleaning the inside, you will clean the outside in the process" — an image for interior-first righteousness.
- **Rising early "to run after strong drink" as a marker of wealth (Marty).** Not a lesson about alcoholism — "any peasant family could drink wine" — but a portrait of "a life of privilege and a life of wealth and a life of influence"; the greedy person of Isaiah 5.
- **Marty's own fundamentalist upbringing (closing testimony).** "I was raised by a very committed fundamentalist Christianity... They taught me how to love the Bible. They taught me how to love Jesus" — and precisely because of that gift, he resolves "not to perfect the art of sin... I do not want to complete what my fathers have started."

## Discussion Questions

1. Jesus affirms the Pharisees' devotion to Scripture *before* he condemns them — "do what they say, just don't do what they do." Where in your own faith community do you need to separate *good teaching of the Text* from a *way of walking it out* that burdens or displays? What would honoring the first while refusing the second look like?
2. The episode redefines "hypocrite" as *actor* — you can do exactly what you preach and still be performing for an audience. Where in your own practice of faith are you most tempted to do the right thing "to be seen"? How would the same act change if literally no one could ever know you did it?
3. Marty insists Jesus is saying "do both" — don't drop the tithe of mint *or* the justice, mercy, and faithfulness. In reaction against legalistic religion, where have you been tempted to "throw the baby out with the bathwater," rejecting something good because of its abuse?
4. The hinge of the episode is that **self-righteousness is worse than greed** because it "puts a counterfeit God on display." Marty applies this to a "generation bent on restoring justice." If you care deeply about justice, where might pride be "lurking just under the surface" of your own rightness — and who would be honest enough to tell you?
5. "Go ahead then and complete what your ancestors started." Marty names his own fundamentalist upbringing and resolves not to "complete what my fathers have started." What did your spiritual (or family) heritage hand you that is genuinely good — and what pattern are you in danger of *completing* if you're not careful?
6. The episode ends not with a verdict but with a hen longing to gather her chicks who "were not willing." How does picturing the Messiah's confrontation as *grief* rather than mere condemnation change the way you hear the seven woes — and the way you'd speak hard truth to someone you love?

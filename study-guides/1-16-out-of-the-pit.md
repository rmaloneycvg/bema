# Out of the Pit

BEMA 16 · Session 1
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/1-16-out-of-the-pit.md`
Book: Genesis (Genesis 41–50 — the conclusion of the Joseph narrative; read passages: 41:1–45; 43:1–10; 44:30–34; 45:1–15; 50:15–26)
Co-host: Brent Billings · Host: Marty Solomon · Guest: Elle Grover Fricks (BEMA teaching team; Hebrew / ancient Near East)

> **Session 1 overarching arc: partnership.** This is **part two of the two-part conversation** on the life of Joseph, and the closing beat of the whole book of Genesis — the "preface and introduction" to Torah. The question left open in part one ("does history simply repeat itself, or can the cycle break?") gets its answer here. Joseph's once-grating self-belief is **redeemed into usable chutzpah** that saves a nation; Pharaoh becomes a "new father figure" who reverses the gifts→dream→stripped→pit template of Joseph's earlier life (now: pulled *out* of the pit, given a coat). But the hinge of the whole story is **Judah**: a transformed, repentant man who offers *his own life* for Benjamin. Marty, following Fohrman, insists "the hero of the Joseph story is not Joseph… the hero of the Joseph story is Judah." Judah's humility catalyzes reconciliation and forgiveness, and Joseph — grounded in "what you meant for evil, God meant for good" — refuses to replay Cain and Abel. Jacob's last heroic act keeps the partnership pointed at the mission (bones carried back to Canaan, not comfort in Egypt). The arc's forward edge: which father's story will Joseph cling to — Pharaoh's Egypt or God's mission?

## Key Lessons

- **Joseph's self-belief is redeemed: the same chutzpah that was "grating" becomes the thing God uses to save lives.** Marty frames Joseph's response to catastrophic news as the opposite of despair: *"Joseph decides to fight back, not against God, but against the circumstances… He's got this chutzpah that doesn't just give the bad news. He actually has a response to the bad news."* Elle names the redemption directly: *"that self-belief that before was so grating is now something redemptive that God can use… if he had… promoted really abrasive humility the whole time of like, 'Oh, who am I?' Then he wouldn't be there in the first place."*
- **Pharaoh becomes a "new father figure" who reverses the template of Joseph's life.** Following Fohrman's "Goats and Coats," Marty traces a repeated pattern (gifts → a dream / "listen to me" → stripped of coat → thrown into a pit) through the Joseph, Potiphar, and cupbearer/baker stories — and then its inversion: *"all of a sudden in Pharaoh's story, it's reversed. So Pharaoh now pulls him out of the pit. He's given a coat. Pharaoh has dreams, says, 'Listen to me.' And then Pharaoh gives gifts. And so now it's like there's this new father figure in the story."*
- **The hero of the Joseph story is Judah, not Joseph.** Marty (crediting Fohrman) makes this the central claim: *"The hero of the story, the Joseph story is not Joseph… The hero of the Joseph story is Judah. This is why Judah will be the tribe of… the Messiah… because Judah at a critical moment… chose to be humble enough to engage in the work of confession, to go through the work of repentance."* Judah's offer of his own life for Benjamin — "please let your servant remain here as my lord's slave in place of the boy" — is the catalyzing act.
- **Repentance catalyzes forgiveness; it is Judah's humility, not Joseph's benevolence, that kicks the reconciliation in motion.** Marty: *"It's Judah's willingness to humble himself. It's Judah's willingness to repent that inspires the family to reconciliation. That's the catalyzing moment. Not just Joseph's benevolent willingness… It's his repentance that catalyzes forgiveness."* Reuben's earlier offer ("you can have my sons") did not move Jacob; Judah's personal responsibility did.
- **Forgiveness is grounded in the all-sufficiency of God, not in excusing the wrong.** Elle gives the episode's defining teaching on forgiveness: *"this forgiveness isn't centered on the acceptability of what his brothers did to him… He centers himself in the goodness and the sufficiency, the all-sufficiency of God, that God is able to take this evil and alchemy-like, turn it into something good."* Marty ties it to the arc: *"forgiveness is the ultimate act of trusting the story… It is saying, 'I believe that God's up to something in the world, and I'm going to let God do what God's doing.'"*
- **We get to write a new story — Joseph will not replay Cain and Abel.** The whole Genesis introduction lands on human agency inside partnership. Marty: *"Joseph will not replay the story of Cain and Abel… Do we have the ability to write new stories? Is hope in play?… We get to choose whether or not we're just passive members of a cycling narrative, or whether or not we're going to tell something new in our own stories."*
- **Jacob's closing act keeps the partnership aimed at the mission, not at Egyptian comfort.** Marty: *"Jacob's heroic act is going to be like, 'No, God's promises were not about Egypt… God's promises were about a land through which he would bless the rest of the world. Don't forget the story of God.'"* Hence the insistence: "promise me you'll take my bones out of here and bury them in Canaan."

## East/West Lens

- **Chutzpah as covenant fire — determination God wants to use, not a vice to be suppressed.** The Eastern reading prizes the family's stubborn fire over Western "abrasive humility." Marty (in review): *"these guys might be a dysfunctional mess… but they've got some fire. They've got some chutzpah… it's that intention… that determination, commitment that God sees as unbelievably full of potential. He wants to use that."*
- **Words/names as loaded word-pictures carrying the plot.** The dream is decoded through Hebrew sound: the cows come up *out of the reeds* (*akhu*), heard as *out of the brothers* (*akh*). Elle: *"in Hebrew it says akhu and the word for brother is akh. So you could even say it says his brothers, their brothers."*
- **Text as treasure excavated through literary device (the "Goats and Coats" template).** Meaning is carried by a repeated narrative structure placed deliberately across stories, not by surface chronology. Marty: *"Fohrman said in his 'Goats and Coats' teaching, there's a template here that we keep seeing. There are three particular repeats."*
- **Formation inside the *beit av* — administration as a trained vocation, not a sudden gift.** Elle reads Joseph's leisure garment as a sign he was being trained to *run the household*, now scaled up to a nation: *"I think he's being trained in the tent of his father… how to run the beit av… the house of the father as the bechor… now he has a chance to do that on a much bigger level with the systems brain that he was trained in."*
- **Reading in the character's own interior world (vs. flat omniscient narration).** The lens lingers on what Joseph *doesn't* know — that his brothers lied with the bloody robe, that Jacob thinks him dead — so that Pharaoh's fatherly reversal lands as it would for Joseph. Marty: *"Joseph doesn't know he was thrown into a pit… he has to assume dad's going to come look for him… and yet dad never comes."*
- **Forgiveness as *trusting the story* — a theological posture, not a transaction that excuses evil.** Marty: *"It's not saying that injustice isn't injustice, that evil isn't evil… It is saying, 'I believe that God's up to something in the world.'"* Elle's "groundedness" in God's sufficiency is the Eastern alternative to a Western "the offender must earn it" frame.
- **Cyclical history interrogated and broken — the covenant family can write something new.** The ancient assumption that history merely repeats (set up in part one) is answered: *"Joseph will not replay the story of Cain and Abel… Or do we have the ability to write new stories?"* (Marty).
- **Mission over empire — belonging and blessing-the-nations above imperial comfort.** Jacob's bones-in-Canaan demand reasserts the communal mission against "the story of imperial comfort" (Marty), and names Joseph's coming choice between "two father figures."

## Recurring Through-lines

- **Partnership (Session 1 arc) — God partners with a fiery, imperfect, chosen family and that family can change.** The episode resolves the partnership question positively: God "partners with Jacob" in "the midst of his dysfunctional mess" (Marty, review), and the chutzpah God saw as "full of potential" becomes life-saving in Joseph. The arc's forward edge is Joseph's coming loyalty test between Pharaoh's story and God's mission.
- **Trust the story / "trusting the story."** Named explicitly as the frame for forgiveness. Marty: *"forgiveness is the ultimate act of trusting the story."* Elle's groundedness "in the goodness and the sufficiency… of God" is the same thread. (Anchor motif of Session 1; flag for cross-reference, do not re-explain.)
- **chutzpah — covenant fire carried down the family line.** From Jacob wanting the *bechor* blessing to Joseph's confident "God *will* give Pharaoh the answer" to Judah/Reuben confronting their father, the family's fire recurs and is now read redemptively. Marty: *"he's got some chutzpah there"*; Elle (of Judah to Jacob): *"There is some chutzpah."*
- **bechor — chosen/firstborn as training and responsibility.** The *bechor* motif returns, now read as *vocation*: Joseph "trained in the tent of his father… as the bechor, as the chosen" (Elle), applying household administration to all Egypt. (Term anchored earlier in the patriarch arc.)
- **Repentance and transformation as the crack that lets redemption in (Judah).** The thread of hope planted by Judah/Tamar in part one pays off: *"this new, humble, repented, transformed person… softens Jacob's heart"* (Marty). Confession → repentance → transformation → reconciliation.
- **Inheriting the parents' story — footsteps of the father, redeemed.** Joseph "following in the footsteps of his father" is reread from negative to beautiful: *"the footsteps of his father aren't all negative… I'm seeing some of those same characteristics in Joseph"* (Marty). (Callback to the Jacob episodes.)
- **Benjamin laying down his life for Judah — a template that runs through all Tanakh.** Judah offers his life for Benjamin here; Fohrman's reversal (Benjaminites later dying for the line of Judah) is flagged: Jonathan & David, Mordecai & Esther. Marty: *"all throughout Tanakh, you're going to see Benjamin keep laying his… life on the line for Judah."*
- **Forgiveness as a central, deferred theme.** Explicitly flagged forward to a Session 2 episode "where we circle back around and talk about forgiveness… all the things that forgiveness is not." (Forward pointer.)
- **Mission vs. comfort / "don't forget the story of God."** Jacob's bones-in-Canaan insistence keeps the covenant pointed at blessing the nations rather than Egyptian ease. (Forward pointer to Session 6 and the coming episodes.)

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat in the episode, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Framing & review (Genesis as preface).** Marty recaps the Session 1 story: God is "a benevolent, compassionate, gracious, loving Creator" and "trust the story"; Avram known by "his best moments, not his worst moments"; Isaac realizing "the fruit of that mission… to bless all nations"; Jacob's "chutzpah" and dysfunction that God partners with. Flags that Fohrman's teaching should be linked (the "Goats and Coats" TorahAnytime lecture; *Genesis: A Parsha Companion*). Reviews the Joseph story so far — dreams told "against better wisdom," thrown in a pit, Judah/Tamar, Potiphar, the dungeon, interpreting the cupbearer's and baker's dreams, "remember me" (told to the cupbearer, not the doomed baker).
2. **Reading Genesis 41 (first portion) — Pharaoh's dreams.** Brent reads the seven fat/lean cows and seven full/scorched heads of grain, the magicians' failure, the cupbearer's confession, Joseph brought from the dungeon, and "I cannot do it… but God will give Pharaoh the answer he desires," followed by the interpretation (seven years of abundance, seven of famine; "firmly decided by God").
3. **The reeds, the brothers, the mothers (Fohrman + Akiva).** Marty unpacks the dream via Fohrman: Akiva's wordplay that "reeds" shares a root with "brothers" (Elle: *akhu* / *akh*); reeds as the Egyptian mythological paradise (Field of Reeds) — a possible echo of Joseph's lost childhood "paradise." Elle notes the cows' description shifts in Pharaoh's *retelling* (v. 18) to the exact Hebrew used of **Rachel** ("healthy of flesh and beautiful of lines" / "sleek"). The point: Joseph may hear his *own* family story inside Pharaoh's dream, which explains how he instantly ties the sevens to *years*.
4. **What kind of person is Joseph? Chutzpah redeemed.** Marty contrasts his own likely despair ("I'd fall… into despair") and Elle's "pull an Elijah, head to a cave" with Joseph's fight-back response. Elle argues Joseph was *trained* in the *beit av* for administration (the leisure garment), now scaled to a nation; his once-grating self-belief becomes "redemptive." The Rivka/Jacob parallel from *Parsha Companion* (Rebekah calling out "who Jacob is") is cited.
5. **Reading Genesis 41 (second portion) — Joseph exalted.** Brent reads Joseph's plan (store one-fifth), Pharaoh's "Can we find anyone like this man, one in whom is the spirit of God?", the signet ring, linen robes, gold chain, the chariot, "Make way!", the name Zaphnath-Paneah, and Asenath as wife.
6. **The "Goats and Coats" template and its reversal.** Marty lays out Fohrman's repeated structure — gifts → dream / "listen to me" → stripped of coat → thrown into a *pit* (Hebrew "pit," not "dungeon") — across Genesis 37 (dad), the Potiphar story, and (loosely) the cupbearer/baker — then its inversion in Pharaoh's story: pulled *out* of the pit, given a coat, dreams, gifts. Pharaoh = "new father figure." Marty + Brent list what Joseph *doesn't know* (Reuben's rescue attempt, the bloody robe, that Jacob thinks him dead).
7. **The family follows him (Genesis 42, summarized).** The new-father-figure respite "doesn't last long": the brothers arrive; Benjamin (Joseph's full brother, Rachel's son) is kept home; Joseph demands they not return without Benjamin.
8. **Reading Genesis 43:1–10 — Judah steps up.** Brent reads the severe famine, "you will not see my face again unless your brother is with you," Jacob's complaint, and **Judah's** personal guarantee for Benjamin. Elle: "There is some chutzpah" — this is "not how you talk to the patriarch"; Reuben's disappearing passivity, now stepping up. Marty contrasts Reuben's earlier offer ("you can have my sons," end of ch. 42) with Judah's offer of *his own life*; the Judah/Tamar transformation (confession, repentance) is why Judah's plea moves Jacob where Reuben's did not.
9. **The silver-cup setup (Genesis 44, summarized).** Joseph hides his diviner's cup in Benjamin's pack — a setup to keep Benjamin while sending the others back; Joseph "still doesn't know… the heart of his father," he just wants his true brother back.
10. **Brent's "see my face" question.** Brent flags the twice-repeated phrase "you will not see my face again unless your brother is with you" and asks whether it's a euphemism; Marty leaves it open ("who knows the kind of things that could be there").
11. **Reading Genesis 44:30–34 — Judah offers himself.** Brent reads Judah's plea: if the boy is not with them Jacob "will die"; "please let your servant remain here as my lord's slave in place of the boy." Joseph now hears the full backstory and how deeply Jacob cares for Benjamin.
12. **Judah as hero; the catalyst of reconciliation.** Marty states the thesis: the hero is Judah; his repentance (Tamar story), not Joseph's benevolence, is the catalyzing moment that gets Jacob's *and* Joseph's attention and "catalyzes forgiveness." Joseph goes away to weep (ch. 45) and reveals himself.
13. **Forgiveness grounded in God's sufficiency (Genesis 45; 50:20).** Elle teaches "what you meant for evil, God meant for good": forgiveness not centered on the acceptability of the brothers' act but on the "all-sufficiency of God," who "alchemy-like" turns evil into something redemptive — Joseph's "groundedness" when facing his family (with "some theatrics"). Marty: "forgiveness is the ultimate act of trusting the story," and flags a Session 2 forgiveness episode (what forgiveness is *not*).
14. **Genesis comes full circle — a new story, not Cain and Abel.** Marty: the family now "understand[s] justice… repentance… the power of humility"; Joseph "will not replay the story of Cain and Abel." The challenge: do we believe we can write new stories, playing "the role of Judah"? Fohrman's Benjamin-for-Judah pattern across Tanakh (Jonathan & David; Mordecai & Esther) is cited — "remembering where we come from and taking care of each other."
15. **"So what about Jacob?" — the mission over Egypt (Genesis 50; forward to Session 6).** Jacob's story doesn't "peter out": his closing heroic act is to insist the promise "was not about Egypt" but about the land of blessing — "promise me you'll take my bones out of here and bury them in Canaan." Joseph will face "two father figures" (Pharaoh/Egypt vs. Jacob/God's mission) and must choose which story to be loyal to — set up for the coming episodes.

## Terminology

- **akhu / akh** (אחו / אח) — "reeds" vs. "brother"; Rabbi Akiva's wordplay. The cows come up "out of the reeds," heard as "out of the brothers," tying Pharaoh's dream to Joseph's family. Elle: *"in Hebrew it says akhu and the word for brother is akh. So you could even say it says his brothers, their brothers."*
- **Field of Reeds** — in Egyptian mythology the name of the paradise/afterlife; a possible echo of Joseph's lost childhood "paradise" with his father. Elle: *"in Egyptian mythology, Reeds is the central feature… the name of their mythological paradise or heaven place in the afterlife."* Marty: *"maybe the Reeds-Paradise connection has to do with the way that he viewed his childhood."*
- **"beautiful of lines / sleek" (the Rachel cue)** — the cows in Pharaoh's *retelling* (v. 18) are described with the Hebrew used of **Rachel** when we first meet her, linking the dream to Joseph's mother. Elle: *"it switched to 'healthy of flesh and beautiful of lines.' That's sleek… exactly what it says about Rachel."*
- **bechor** (בכור) — "chosen / firstborn"; here read as a *trained vocation*. Elle: Joseph "is the bechor, as the chosen," trained in the tent "not just like how to take care of the sheep, but how to run the beit av."
- **beit av** (בית אב) — "house of the father"; the household/community Joseph was trained to administer, a skill now scaled to all Egypt. Elle: *"how to run the beit av, right? The house of his father."*
- **chutzpah** — audacious, stubborn determination/fire; the family trait God wants to use, redeemed in Joseph and visible in Judah. Marty: *"It's absolutely got some—got some chutzpah there."* Elle (of Judah): *"There is some chutzpah."*
- **Zaphenath-Paneah** (צפנת פענח) — the Egyptian name Pharaoh gives Joseph upon his exaltation. (Read in Gen 41:45.)
- **"you meant it for evil, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20)** — the key verse on forgiveness; grounded in the all-sufficiency of God, not in excusing the brothers. Elle: *"'What you meant for evil, God meant for good'… God is able to take this evil and alchemy-like, turn it into something good."*
- **"pit" (vs. "dungeon")** — Fohrman's note that the Hebrew repeatedly reads *pit*, so Pharaoh pulling Joseph "out of the pit" mirrors and reverses his being thrown *into* a pit by his brothers. Marty: *"the word dungeon there… more literally… literally pit in the Hebrew… Pharaoh now pulls him out of the pit."*

## Illustrations & Stories

- **"Let him cook" (Elle & Brent).** The playful riff as Brent reads 32 verses uninterrupted — Elle: *"Woo! Let him cook."* A light moment framing the long Genesis 41 reading.
- **"Pull an Elijah, head to a cave" (Elle).** Elle's image for how most of us would react to a divine warning of famine — retreat and wait "until it's all over" — set against Joseph's fight-back chutzpah.
- **The hurricane / natural-disaster contrast (Marty).** Marty pictures getting word of a coming disaster: *"I would be like, 'Oh no, we should probably leave.' Joseph is like, 'Oh no, let's get ready. Let's start preparing.'"* — dramatizing chutzpah as active response to bad news.
- **"Dates and hummus" in the tent (Elle).** Elle's picture of what Joseph was *not* doing in his father's tent — "I don't think it was just that he was like laying around eating dates and hummus" — making the case that he was being trained in administration.
- **The "cool, cool, cool" exchange with Pharaoh (Elle & Brent).** The comic beat on Pharaoh's unquestioning acceptance — Elle: *"It's like, cool, cool, cool"*; Brent: *"Uh, okay, dude, whatever. Here's my dream."* — illustrating Joseph's disarming confidence.
- **"We could have finished this episode twice by now!" (Brent).** Brent reuses Judah's line ("we could have traveled there and back twice") as a running gag about the episode's length — a memorable tie between the text's "delay" and the podcast's pacing.

## Discussion Questions

1. Elle says Joseph's self-belief "that before was so grating is now something redemptive that God can use." Where is a trait in yourself (or someone you find difficult) that reads as a flaw in one setting but becomes a gift in another? What would it take to let God *use* it rather than suppress it with "abrasive humility"?
2. Marty contrasts despair ("I'd fall into despair") and Elle's "pull an Elijah, head to a cave" with Joseph's choice to "fight back… against the circumstances." When you've received genuinely bad news, which instinct is yours — retreat or preparation — and what would "chutzpah that has a response to the bad news" look like in a situation you're facing now?
3. The whole episode insists the hero is **Judah**, not Joseph — because Judah repented and offered his own life. Whose quiet, costly repentance has actually been the hinge of a reconciliation you've witnessed? Why do we so often credit the "benevolent" figure (Joseph) instead of the "transformed" one (Judah)?
4. Elle grounds forgiveness not in "the acceptability of what his brothers did" but in "the all-sufficiency of God," and Marty calls forgiveness "the ultimate act of trusting the story." Where are you tempted to think forgiveness requires *excusing* the wrong? How does Joseph's groundedness reframe a hurt you're carrying?
5. Marty asks whether we are "just passive members of a cycling narrative" or can "tell something new" — Joseph "will not replay the story of Cain and Abel." What inherited family pattern are you most at risk of simply repeating, and what would the first "new story" choice look like this week?
6. Jacob's last act refuses to let the promise collapse into Egyptian comfort — "take my bones… bury them in Canaan." Where in your life is the "comfortable Egypt" option quietly replacing the harder "mission" God actually gave? Which "father figure's story" are you clinging to?

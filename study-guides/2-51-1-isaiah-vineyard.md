## Where This Sits in the One Story

Session 1 built the picture of **partnership**: God rescues a people out of Egypt and invites them to become a kingdom of priests — a shelter and shade in the desert, a people who notice the cry of the oppressed. Session 2 turns to the **Prophets**, and the arc of this stage is the partner's (Israel's/Judah's) **unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** 1 Isaiah advances that arc precisely: the people are still "doing worship rightly" — Levitical offerings, incense, Sabbaths, festivals, prayers — yet they have abandoned the actual terms of the partnership by failing to care for the foreigner, orphan, and widow. The vineyard God planted and lavished with care has yielded *be'ushim* (bad fruit) instead of a harvest of justice. And yet — even here — God does not quit: Isaiah 4 is six whole verses of hope reusing Session 1's desert imagery, and the warning explicitly ends with the promise of becoming a "restorer of broken walls." This episode is the fourth and final pre-Assyrian voice (after Amos, Hosea, Micah), and it drives home the diagnosis: the covenant partner has "forgotten the plot of the story," and God keeps calling them back.

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **Four voices of Isaiah** — framing the book as four historical voices (Proto/1, 2, redaction, Deutero/3, and a fourth/Trito), following Brueggemann; a packaging aid, not a claim the book should be torn apart.
2. **Locating 1 Isaiah** — chapters 1–11(ish), the pre-Assyrian "Isaiah Isaiah," writing during the reign of King Uzziah, with a message of warning.
3. **Isaiah 1 — the opening indictment** — rebellious children, a body covered in wounds, a land desolate; the Sodom/Gomorrah parallel.
4. **Isaiah 1 — the worship God hates** — right Levitical worship rejected because "your hands are full of blood"; the call to "seek justice, defend the oppressed."
5. **The Source A / Source B test** — is this idolatry/immorality (A) or empire/injustice (B)? Verdict: clearly Source B.
6. **Isaiah 3 — Marty reads the warning** — the removal of every support, social collapse, leaders who mislead, "grind the faces of the poor."
7. **Isaiah 4 — the chapter of hope** — six verses reusing Exodus desert imagery (branch, cloud, fire, canopy, shelter, shade, refuge).
8. **The review images** — Amos = plumb line, Hosea = garden, Micah = judge, 1 Isaiah = vineyard.
9. **The terraced vineyard** — family land, Jubilee, interdependent terraces, neighbors watching one another's walls as a picture of community.
10. **Isaiah 5:1-7 — the Song of the Vineyard** — soreq planted, watchtower and winepress built in confidence, be'ushim produced, the hedge torn down; the tzedakah/za'aqah wordplay.
11. **The six woes** — greed (house to house, field to field), drunkenness, hubris, calling evil good; "you built this world and left my vineyard untended."

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 2 arc — the covenant partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **Right worship cannot substitute for justice.** The people's problem is not that they got worship wrong; it is that they offered correct worship while oppressing the vulnerable. The partnership of Session 1 required a people who care for the cry; abandoning that empties the worship.
   - *Marty:* "they're showing up in religious space, they're giving their offerings, they're singing their songs, they're going through all the religious motions, they're doing worship rightly... So why is the picture so grim?"
   - *Brent (reading Isaiah 1):* "Your hands are full of blood... Seek justice, defend the oppressed, take up the cause of the fatherless, plead the case of the widow."

2. **The sin named is Source B — injustice, not idolatry.** This episode sharpens the diagnosis of the whole Prophets arc: the breakdown of partnership here is about how people are being treated, reinforced by the repeated Sodom parallel.
   - *Marty:* "Is this Source A or Source B, Brent?" / *Brent:* "Source B." / *Marty:* "Absolutely, Source B. Very clearly."
   - *Marty:* "What's the sin of Sodom?" / *Brent:* "They did not care for the oppressed, the aliens, the orphans and widows."

3. **God's refusal to quit shows up as hope planted inside judgment.** Even as warning dominates, God insists it is not hopeless and reaches back to the Exodus rescue — the clearest sign of the arc's "refusal to quit."
   - *Marty:* "in the midst of all of this warning, God's like: Hey, this isn't hopeless. I need you to remember what's true underneath it all. So don't forget the hope."
   - *Marty:* "God knows how to rescue his people from Egypts, from empires. He's done it before. He'll do it again."

4. **God gave the vineyard everything; the failure is the partner's.** The Song of the Vineyard makes God's lavish, confident investment explicit, so the bad fruit cannot be blamed on neglect from God's side — advancing the arc's theme that the unfaithfulness is Israel's, not God's.
   - *Marty:* "He's so confident... I am so confident that this is going to give me the crop that I want that I'm just going to build a watchtower and a winepress. I'm ready for a harvest."
   - *Brent (reading Isaiah 5):* "What more could have been done for my vineyard than I have done for it?"

5. **Covenant life is interdependent — you must care for your neighbor and let your neighbor care for you.** The terraced hillside reframes accountability as community health rather than judgment, recovering what the partnership was always meant to be.
   - *Marty:* "if Brent doesn't take care of his wall, if Brent doesn't take care of his terrace, I lose my terrace... it's not about judgment. It's about cooperation."
   - *Marty:* "not only do I have to care about my neighbor, I have to let my neighbor care about me."

## East/West Lens

Only categories genuinely engaged by this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Block logic / holistic indictment over step-by-step argument (East).** Isaiah does not build a linear case; he piles image on image — wounds, siege-hut, Sodom, worthless worship — to produce a total picture rather than a syllogism.
  - *Marty:* "I'm not sure Isaiah's supposed to be read from beginning to end, like one endless, seamless stream of consciousness. I think these are bits and pieces that are probably given by Isaiah... over time."

- **Right action over right ritual / orthopraxy (East).** The Hebraic concern is what the "works of our hands" actually do to real people, not whether the ceremonial boxes were checked.
  - *Marty:* "It's not that we've gotten our worship wrong. The works of our hands is... that we're not taking care of each other. It's that we're oppressing."

- **Communal identity over individual autonomy (East).** The terraced-vineyard image assumes a self understood only within an interdependent community, in contrast to a Western "that's his wall, his problem" individualism.
  - *Marty:* "in our culture, we don't like this kind of interdependence. We don't like this kind of accountability... But it's not about judgment. It's about the community being healthy."

- **God who feels — ethos/pathos (East).** Drawing on Heschel, the prophet is portrayed as entering God's emotional experience, not merely relaying propositions.
  - *Marty:* "I have experienced the ethos, Abraham Heschel might say, of God, and this is not good."

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension states the problem, tags its type, cites the transcript, links the passage, and gives a Hebraic resolution (or marks it open).

1. **Why does God hate worship he himself commanded?**
   - *Type:* Apparent contradiction (theological).
   - *Problem:* God prescribed the offerings, incense, Sabbaths, and festivals in Torah, yet declares them detestable and says he will not listen.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "it was God that asked them to do this, to bring the offerings. It's Leviticus. They're following Levitical instruction, and yet God's pretty upset about it."
   - *Passage:* [Isaiah 1:11-15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+1%3A11-15&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The worship is not rejected as ritual but as hypocrisy — "your hands are covered with blood." Orthopraxy toward the vulnerable is the condition that makes worship coherent; detached from justice, the ritual becomes offensive.

2. **"Judge not" vs. watching your neighbor's wall.**
   - *Type:* Cultural assumption / value clash.
   - *Problem:* A Western reflex ("judge not lest you be judged") treats speaking into a neighbor's maintenance as intrusive judgment.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "We're like: Hey, judge not lest you be judged. Don't talk about my wall. But it's not about judgment. It's about cooperation."
   - *Passage:* [Isaiah 5:1-2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+5%3A1-2&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* In a terraced-community economy, mutual oversight is survival and love, not condemnation — one wall's collapse ruins the terraces below, so accountability is reframed as collaboration.

3. **One book of Isaiah or four voices?**
   - *Type:* Authorship / translation-tradition disagreement.
   - *Problem:* The Bible presents a single book, yet scholarship hears multiple historical voices spanning centuries.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "It's one book of Isaiah in your Bible. We're not trying to suggest it should be anything else. But as you look at that book you're going to find four different portions of history represented."
   - *Passage:* [Isaiah 1:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+1%3A1&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* A disciple writing in a master prophet's voice was a normal, non-deceptive ancient practice; honoring the book as one while hearing distinct historical settings is a both/and, not a tearing-apart. *Marty:* "it would be very common in their day for a disciple or a student of a prophet to write in that prophet's voice."

4. **Could the "divination/Philistine" language mean this is Source A after all?**
   - *Type:* Ambiguity / open question.
   - *Problem:* A few phrases (e.g., practicing divination like the Philistines) carry an idolatry flavor that complicates the clean Source B reading.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "there's a couple references that you could be like: I don't know, that kind of feels idolatry-esque, I guess. They practice the divination like the Philistines, 2:6... I don't want to misrepresent that."
   - *Passage:* [Isaiah 2:6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+2%3A6&version=NIV)
   - *Resolution:* **Open — dance with it.** Marty acknowledges the thread "is there if you want to find it," while the dominant, repeated message remains justice/Source B.

## Terminology

- **soreq** (שורק) — the "choicest vines" of Isaiah 5:2; the best wine grape, associated with the Soreq Valley.
  - *Marty:* "that choicest of vines there is the word soreq... That's the best of the best. Like that's the best wine grape."
- **be'ushim / betushim** (באשים) — the "bad fruit" of Isaiah 5:2: stunted, sour, worthless grapes produced when a vine is left untended.
  - *Marty:* "that Hebrew phrase there, bad fruit, is the Hebrew word betushim. It refers to a stunted... they never mature and they just go bad."
- **tzedakah** (צדקה) — "righteousness"; the good grapes God came looking for.
  - *Marty:* "The good grapes he wanted to find would be tzedakah. Tzedakah is righteousness."
- **za'aqah** (זעקה) — the "cry of distress"; the scream of agony from those suffering injustice (the same cry that rose from Sodom).
  - *Marty:* "the za'aqah... it's the scream of somebody in agony. And not just any agony... but the agony of injustice, the agony of oppression."
- **ethos / pathos** — Heschel's language for the prophet experiencing God's own emotional reality.
  - *Marty:* "I have experienced the ethos, Abraham Heschel might say, of God."
- **Source A / Source B** — the running interpretive test: idolatry/immorality (A) versus empire/injustice (B).
  - *Marty:* "Is this an idolatry/morality conversation? Or is this an empire/injustice conversation?"
- **Proto- / Deutero- / Trito-Isaiah** — scholarly labels for the first, second, and third sections/voices of Isaiah.
  - *Brent:* "Proto-Isaiah is what they call it." / *Marty:* "So it's at least Proto and Deutero, but maybe Trito."

## Illustrations & Images

- **The terraced hillside garden (2008 photos near Jerusalem).** A terraced vineyard dating back (as Marty was told) possibly near Abraham's day, requiring thousands of years of continuous cultivation; a living picture of interdependent community where each family tends its terrace.
  - *Marty:* "you're looking at a terraced garden on a hillside just outside of modern day Jerusalem... there are gardens like that that would have been maintained for thousands of [years]."
- **Brent's neighbor's retaining wall.** A real-world memory: a neighbor two houses up had a failing retaining wall that caused problems three houses down — illustrating why neighbors must watch one another's walls.
  - *Marty:* "They had a retaining wall that had — and it was causing problems like three houses down." / *Brent:* "Yeah, yeah."
- **Seeing your neighbor's wall, not your own.** Standing on your terrace every day, you see your neighbor's wall, not your own — so community members naturally care for each other.
  - *Marty:* "if I spend every day on my terrace, do I see my own wall?" / *Brent:* "No." / *Marty:* "No, I do see my neighbor's wall."
- **The uncultivated hillside and the Amish rebuilding a wall.** A photo turned 90° shows where a vineyard used to be — now just stones — while Amish fellow travelers began rebuilding a small section of broken wall, enacting the "restorer of broken walls" image.
  - *Marty:* "I was on that tour with a bunch of Amish brothers and sisters. And we actually kind of started rebuilding a small little wall, because the end of Isaiah says you'll be like restorer of broken walls."
- **The confident Gardener building the winepress early.** God builds the watchtower and winepress before the grapes could possibly be ready — an image of lavish, eager confidence in the harvest.
  - *Marty:* "He's so confident. He built a winepress. He's so excited. He's been waiting for this day."

## Discussion Questions

1. The people of Judah were "doing worship rightly" — offerings, festivals, prayers — yet God found it detestable because "your hands are full of blood." Where might our communities be most tempted to let correct religious practice stand in for actual justice toward the vulnerable?
2. The terraced vineyard reframes accountability as cooperation, not judgment: "if Brent doesn't take care of his terrace, I lose my terrace." What would it look like to both *care for your neighbor's wall* and *let your neighbor care for yours* in your own context?
3. In the Song of the Vineyard, God asks, "What more could have been done for my vineyard than I have done for it?" If the failure is the partner's and not God's, how does Isaiah 4's chapter of hope keep this from collapsing into despair — and what does that reveal about God's refusal to quit?
4. The Source A / Source B test keeps returning a Source B verdict (injustice, not idolatry). Why might it matter for how we read the Prophets that God's complaint is so consistently about how people are treated rather than about ritual purity?
5. Isaiah hears za'aqah (the cry of the oppressed) where God planted for tzedakah (righteousness). Whose za'aqah might be rising around us today, and what would it mean to produce the "good grapes" of justice instead?

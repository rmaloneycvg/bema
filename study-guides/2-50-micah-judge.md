# Micah - Judge

BEMA 50 · Session 2
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/2-50-micah-judge.md`
Book: Micah 1:1-16; 2:1-13; 5:2-6; 6:1-8; 7:8-12. The transcript prints the NET; Brent reads aloud from the NIV.
Host: Marty Solomon · Co-host: Brent Billings
Core move throughout: Micah's governing image is **God as judge** — not a robed figure making abstract pronouncements, but a *shofet* like Deborah or Gideon who both declares the right verdict *and enacts* it, bringing the restoration (*mishpat*) to pass. Reading Micah, the series' running "Source A (immorality/idolatry) vs. Source B (empire/injustice)" question comes out near a tie, and Micah 6:8 is the synthesis: God wants not empty obedience, nor correct worship void of justice, but "correct worship that's full of justice."

> **Session 2 overarching arc: the prophets — the partner's (Israel's) unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Session 1 built the *partnership*: God rescuing a people out of empire, marrying them at Sinai, planting them "at the crossroads of the earth" and charging them to look out for "the foreigner... the orphan... the widow." Session 2 lives in the long collapse of that partnership — the monarchy spinning out, the people building their own little empires and exploiting the poor. This episode sits among the *pre-Assyrian prophets* (Amos the plumb line, Hosea, now Micah the judge), the voices God keeps sending to the failing partner. This guide advances the one story by showing *what God's refusal to quit looks like in a courtroom*: God files a formal case against his covenant people (Micah 6), recites the exodus-and-wilderness history of the partnership ("I brought you up out of Egypt... I sent Moses, Aaron and Miriam"), and still — after all the woe and warning — ends in "a little dusting of hope" (Micah 7). Where Session 1 asked whether the partner would say *yes* to the covenant, Micah shows the partner failing *and* the Judge choosing restoration over mere condemnation — and, remarkably for the prophets, Judah actually *listens* and is spared from Assyria for a time. God is still prosecuting the marriage not to end it but to put it right.

## Key Lessons

- **The biblical judge *enacts* restoration; he doesn't just pronounce it.** When Micah says the judge is coming, don't picture a black robe, powdered wig, and gavel — picture Deborah or Gideon. Marty: *"The judge in the biblical world is the one who makes the pronouncement, but they are also the one who enacts and brings the judgment to pass. And that focus on the judgment is of restoration, not just the abstract indictment."*
- **God is after *mishpat* (restoration), and will use *diyn* (retribution) only in service of it.** Biblical justice is primarily restorative — making the world right again — not primarily punitive. Marty: *"what God's really going after on a big level is mishpat. But sometimes in order to get to mishpat, you have to have a little bit of diyn... which one is driving the big picture? For the world of the Bible, it's mishpat because we're trying to restore Shalom."*
- **Shalom is the byproduct of righteousness and justice working together.** *Tzedakah* (being in right relationship) and *mishpat* (the act/place of restoration) together produce *shalom* — "the world put back in its proper place." Marty: *"Shalom is the byproduct of righteousness and justice."*
- **"Source A or Source B?" — the honest answer in Micah is *yes*.** The sin/idolatry lens and the empire/injustice lens are both alive and nearly even in Micah; 6:8 holds them together. Marty: *"He wants the obedience of justice. He doesn't want empty obedience. He doesn't just want correct worship that's void of obedience. What God wants is correct worship that's full of justice."*
- **Real strength in God's kingdom comes from smallness, not empire.** The "ruler from Bethlehem Ephrathah, small among the clans of Judah" teaches that God works out of lastness and littleness. Marty: *"real true strength in God's kingdom doesn't come from empires and might. Out of lastness comes firstness. Out of smallness comes bigness. Out of despair comes hope... We're supposed to be trusting the story."*
- **Micah 5's first-order meaning is a *Davidic* ruler, not (first) a Christmas prophecy.** Micah's original hearers would hear David — Bethlehem, shepherd, the eighth of seven brothers ("seven shepherds, even eight commanders") — and deliverance from *Assyria*. Marty: *"when the author of Micah wrote Micah 5, I think they had something else in mind... the immediate implication of this prophecy is about their wanting a Davidic ruler."* (He is clear it *can* be read secondarily in light of Jesus.)
- **The prophets almost always leave "a little sprinkling of hope."** Amid all the woe and warning, God keeps saying "this is not where the story ends." Marty: *"very rarely is it without at least a little bit of God saying: But don't worry, this is not where the story ends... there's something better around the corner. Hope."*
- **Sometimes God's people actually *listen* — and that's beautiful.** Against the caricature that "the people never listen," Judah heeds Micah and is spared from Assyria (though not later from Babylon). Marty: *"it's worth noting that here in Micah, the people of Judah do hear the cry of the prophets like Micah. They do repent, and they are saved from Assyria... Sometimes God's people do listen, and it's beautiful when they do."*
- **The sin of one part of the covenant family infects the whole.** Samaria's "incurable plague" spreads south to Judah and reaches "the very gate of my people, even to Jerusalem." Marty: *"whatever it is that our northern brothers are struggling with has now infected us... This compromise is going to kind of get into everything and destroy everything."*

## East/West Lens

*(Only categories the episode actually engages, each with a citation.)*

- **Hebrew words carry concrete, active meaning the English "justice/judge" flattens (Eastern concreteness vs. Western abstraction).** One English word "judge" blurs a whole active word family — *shaphat* (to judge), *shofet* (the judge/person), *mishpat* (the place/act of justice). Marty: *"Shofet does the shaphat. Shaphat is the judging; shofet is the person."*
- **Justice as restorative, not merely retributive (Eastern relational frame vs. Western punitive one).** Marty contrasts the American/retributive instinct with the biblical and many non-Western frameworks. Marty: *"The world of the Bible and a more ancient world... often will have an understanding of justice that's far more restorative... We're trying to make the world right again. We're trying to put everything back in its proper place."* (Illustrated by the Nez Perce tribal council's description of restorative vs. retributive systems.)
- **Reading Scripture as one unified, echoing story (Eastern intertext vs. Western proof-text).** Micah 5 is heard through David (Bethlehem, shepherd, eighth of seven), and Micah 6's courtroom recites the exodus and the Balak/Balaam and Shittim-to-Gilgal history. Marty: *"we've got a David connection here"*; the Judge's case recalls "what Balak, king of Moab, plotted, and... remember your journey from Shetim to Gilgal."
- **Resisting empire; strength through smallness (Eastern suspicion of imperial power).** Against might and empire, God's kingdom runs on littleness. Marty: *"We're not supposed to be building empires. We're supposed to be trusting the story."*
- **Worship and ethics are inseparable (Eastern wholeness vs. Western compartmentalizing).** The 6:6-8 plea assumes you could satisfy God with enough rams and oil; the answer fuses worship and justice. Marty: *"What God wants is correct worship that's full of justice."*

3. **Locating Micah among the prophets.** Amos = plumb line (very Source B); Hosea = (Elle's forthcoming treatment; "prostitute"/possibly more Source B than Marty once thought); Amos and Hosea aimed at the northern kingdom. Micah's image = **the judge**; Micah is now aimed at **Judah**.
4. **Unpacking justice.** Retributive/punitive (American instinct — "consequences... comeuppance") vs. restorative ("make the world right again"). The Nez Perce tribal-council illustration. Not either/or, but which drives the day — in the Bible, restoration.
5. **The Hebrew word study.** *shalom* as the byproduct of *tzedakah* (righteousness/right relationship) and *mishpat* (justice/restoration); *diyn* as retributive justice used in service of *mishpat*; the root *shaphat* (to judge) → *shofet* (judge) → *shofetim* (book of Judges) → *mishpat* ("place of"). The friendly disagreement with Elle over translating *tzedakah* and *mishpat*.
6. **The judge who enacts.** Not a gavel-and-wig figure but Deborah/Gideon — one who pronounces *and* brings the verdict to pass. Brent's *Judge Dredd* ("judge, jury, and executioner") comparison, "just going in the opposite direction."
7. **Micah 1 read & diagnosed.** Micah of Moresheth (Beit Guvrin today); the LORD coming down, mountains melting; denunciation of *both* Samaria and Jerusalem. Marty: this opening "felt pretty Source A" (idolatry, prostitute's wages). Samaria's "incurable plague" spreads to Judah/Jerusalem.
8. **Micah 2 read & the Source-B turn.** "Woe to those who plan iniquity... They covet fields and seize them, and houses and take them. 9. **The hope sprinkle (2:12-13).** "I will surely gather... the remnant of Israel... The one who breaks open the way will go up before them... the Lord at their head." Marty: a "juicy little verse" that "comes back in Session 3" (extra credit to spot it); Brent suggests memorizing it.
10. **Micah 5 — Bethlehem.** The Christmas-text ruler "from Bethlehem Ephrathah," but read first as *David* (Bethlehem, shepherd, "seven shepherds, even eight commanders" = eighth of seven brothers), with deliverance from *Assyria* in view. Theme: strength from smallness; out of lastness, firstness.
11. **Historical note — it came true, and they listened.** The Assyrian deliverance "is all going to end up being true historically"; Judah heeds Micah and is spared from Assyria (not later from Babylon). "Sometimes God's people do listen."
12. **Micah 6 — the courtroom.** God "has a case against his people," recites the partnership history (Egypt; Moses, Aaron, Miriam; Balak and Balaam; Shittim to Gilgal). The plea (6:6-7): what can I bring — burnt offerings, thousands of rams, ten thousand rivers of oil, my firstborn?
13. **Micah 6:8 — the synthesis.** "To act justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God." Source A or B? "The answer is yes" — correct worship *full* of justice. Marty scores Micah overall as "a tie."
14. **Micah 7 — misery into hope.** The closing: "Though I have fallen, I will rise. Though I sit in darkness, the Lord will be my light... he will plead my case." Ends with rebuilding walls and the nations streaming in. "A little dusting of hope."
15. **Close & homework.** Micah is "a little bit easier than the voice of Amos... beautiful, poetic judgment." Few show notes; Brent urges groups to read the whole book slowly, "one verse at a time," watching for Source A and Source B.

## Terminology

- **shaphat** (שָׁפַט, "to judge") — the verb; the three-consonant root (shin–pei–tav) underlies the whole family. Judging in the biblical world is *active*, making right what has been wronged. Marty: *"The root word shaphat is to judge."*
- **shofet** (שׁוֹפֵט, "judge") — the person who does the *shaphat*; the book of Judges is *Shofetim*. The model is Deborah or Gideon, who enact as well as pronounce. Marty: *"Shofet does the shaphat."*
- **mishpat** (מִשְׁפָּט, "justice") — same root with the *mem* ("place of") prefix; for Marty, not the mere pronouncement but "the place where everything is restored, the act of the restoration." Marty: *"the place of justice... refers to... the act of the restoration."*
- **tzedakah** (צְדָקָה, "righteousness") — "the state of being in right relationship"; together with *mishpat* it produces *shalom*. Marty: *"Righteousness is the word tzedakah... it is the state of being in right relationship."* (He notes a friendly translation disagreement with Elle Grover Fricks over *tzedakah* and *mishpat*.)
- **diyn** (דִּין) — retributive/punitive justice: "the pronouncement of condemnation, of guilt, and everything that comes with it." Sometimes needed to reach *mishpat*. Marty: *"there is a word in the Hebrew for this retributive justice idea. Diyn."*
- **shalom** (שָׁלוֹם) — the byproduct of righteousness and justice; "the world put back in its proper place." Marty: *"Shalom is the byproduct of righteousness and justice."*
- **restorative vs. retributive justice** — restorative aims to "make the world right again"; retributive/punitive aims at "consequences... comeuppance." Both exist in Scripture, but restoration drives the day. Marty: *"We're trying to make the world right again. We're trying to put everything back in its proper place."*
- **Source A / Source B** — the series' running lens: Source A (Samuel-Kings) reads the failure as *immorality and idolatry*; Source B (Chronicles) reads it as *empire and injustice*. Micah scores near-even. Marty: *"Is it immorality and idolatry? Is it Empire and injustice? Which one is it?"*
- **the prophet's "image"** — each prophet gets an encapsulating metaphor: Amos the *plumb line*, Hosea (Elle's forthcoming word), Micah the *judge*. Marty: *"Micah's image is going to be the judge."*

## Illustrations & Stories

- **The Nez Perce tribal council (restorative vs. retributive justice).** From Marty's time on the Palouse: the Nez Perce council gave an annual talk at the University of Idaho law school, pointing out that "the American legal system is a retributive system" that deters by punishment, whereas "we're working on a restorative justice system... trying to make the world right again." The governing picture of the whole justice word-study.
- **Judge Dredd — "judge, jury, and executioner" (Brent).** Brent raises the film's concept; Marty embraces it as "the same idea, just... a different kind of justice... just going in the opposite direction" — the biblical judge also *executes* the verdict, but toward shalom.
- **Deborah and Gideon as the model judge.** Not a gavel-and-wig figure: "Deborah's not the person who sits in a chair and makes decisions all day. She makes the decision, but she also enacts the decision. She brings about the shalom that she's pronouncing." (Marty jokingly warns against picturing *Samson* when you picture God.)
- **Beit Guvrin / Moresheth — Micah's hometown.** Marty: "There's a place today called Beit Guvrin... the ancient site of ancient Moresheth where Micah was from," a site he visits with groups ("I'll have to tell you about it later if you're ever there with me").
- **"Seven, even eight" = the eighth of seven brothers (the David connection).** Brent catches the number pattern in Micah 5:5; Marty links it to David as the eighth son of Jesse's seven, reinforcing the Davidic-shepherd reading of the Bethlehem ruler.

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty says the biblical judge is like Deborah or Gideon — one who both pronounces *and enacts* the verdict, "bringing about the execution of Shalom." How does picturing God as that kind of judge (rather than a gavel-and-wig figure) change the way you hear "judgment" in Scripture?
2. In the Bible "both exist" — *diyn* (retribution) and *mishpat* (restoration) — "but which one is driving the big picture?" Where in your own instincts about justice does retribution tend to crowd out restoration, and what would it look like to let *mishpat* drive?
3. On "Source A or Source B?" Marty answers "yes" — God wants "correct worship that's full of justice." Which half do you more easily neglect: the worship/obedience, or the justice? What does Micah 6:8 ask of the half you under-weight?
4. Micah 5 is read every Christmas, but Marty argues its first meaning is a *Davidic* ruler and rescue from Assyria. How do you hold a text's original meaning *and* its later resonance without collapsing one into the other?
5. "Out of lastness comes firstness. Out of smallness comes bigness." Where are you tempted to seek strength through empire and might rather than through the smallness Micah commends?
6. Against the caricature that "the people never listen," Judah actually heeds Micah and is spared from Assyria. When has heeding a hard, prophetic word actually changed an outcome for you or your community?
7. In Session 1 the question was whether the partner would say *yes* to the covenant; here, in the age of the failing partner, God files a courtroom case yet still ends in "a dusting of hope." What does it mean to you that the Judge prosecutes the marriage in order to *restore* it rather than end it?

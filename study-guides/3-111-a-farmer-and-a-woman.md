# BEMA 111 — A Farmer and a Woman — Study Guide

**Session 3 (The Gospels) · Episode 111**
**Speakers:** Marty Solomon, Brent Billings
**Primary texts:** Matthew 13:31–35; Ezekiel 17:22–24; Genesis 18; Genesis 19; Leviticus (unleavened sacrifices); 1 Corinthians (Paul on leaven)

---

## Overview

This episode puts the PaRDeS mechanics from the previous episode to work on two tiny, intimately connected parables: the mustard seed (Matthew 13:31–32) and the leaven hidden in three measures of flour (Matthew 13:33). The hosts move deliberately through p'shat, remez, and drash on each, discover that both parables teach the *same* thing, and land on Matthew's agenda — a Kingdom bent toward blessing the outsider.

Within the Session 3 arc — the king/Messiah embodying the one story — this episode shows the King *describing* the Kingdom he is bringing, and doing it in a way that is deliberately shocking to his hearers. The mustard seed is a toxic, unstoppable weed; leaven is the biblical symbol of sin. Jesus calls the Kingdom of Heaven *both* of these, and buries in each a remez that reaches back into Session 1 and Session 2: Ezekiel's vision of exiled Israel transplanted and grown into a tree where the Gentiles nest, and Sarah's sixty pounds of flour baked in radical hospitality for three foreign visitors.

> **Marty:** "This tree in Ezekiel is the fulfillment of what God told Abraham he was going to do through him... It represents the people of God finally remembering and doing what God sent them out to do."

## East/West Lens

BEMA's recurring interpretive contrast between Hebraic (Eastern, concrete, relational, story-shaped) and Greek-Western (abstract, systematized) ways of reading. Only the categories actually engaged in this episode are tagged below.

- **Awareness of the Western impulse to over-systematize (Hebraic self-critique).** Brent names the danger of over-mechanizing PaRDeS even as the hosts use it, insisting the technique is a learning scaffold, not a rigid formula.
  > **Brent:** "I am over-mechanizing it, if that's a word... because we are Western, and we like to... As long as we remember, it's not like you can definitely do too much, though you shouldn't go too far, you can hold everything too rigidly, don't do that. There is an exception to every rule."

- **Text in conversation with text (Hebraic / intertextuality).** The entire method of the episode is to read Jesus's words by their echoes — the remez — rather than at face value. Both parables are decoded by chasing the "unnecessary" detail back into the Hebrew Scriptures.
  > **Marty:** "We start looking for that and of course, we find it comes out of Ezekiel 17 which I love because I think Jesus's remez is Ezekiel."

- **The anomaly as a teaching signal (Hebraic / rabbinic device).** The moment a statement stops making sense ("a mustard seed becomes a *tree*"; the Kingdom is like *leaven*) is treated not as an error but as the rabbi's intentional flag to dig deeper.
  > **Marty:** "That moment where you go, 'That doesn't make any sense.' That is the moment where all the lights on your dashboard should be going off... the Rabbi wants me to notice something here."

- **Concrete image over abstract concept (Hebraic).** The Kingdom is carried by physical, agricultural, domestic images — a weed, a seed, a bird, yeast, sixty pounds of flour — rather than by a stated definition.
  > **Marty:** "The Kingdom of Heaven is just a p'shat principle that's small."

- **Learning as a slow, communal process (Hebraic).** Marty stresses that finding a remez can take days, weeks, or years, and often depends on a discussion group or a student catching what the teacher missed.
  > **Marty:** "I've worked on some parables and teachings for years and even had students clue me in after years of studying... It's just this beautiful process when we do these things together."

## Episode Flow

The transcript's teaching, in order. Coverage verification follows.

1. **Recap of PaRDeS.** Brent restates the four levels — p'shat (surface), remez (hint to an Old Testament text), drash (wisdom from that text), sod (wisdom only from God) — and flags the Western tendency to over-formulate the technique.
2. **Framing the two parables.** The episode will treat two very short parables and test whether they are parallel teachings.
3. **Mustard seed — p'shat (Matthew 13:31–32).** The Kingdom is not an anvil dropping in full force; it starts tiny. This triggers a note on *three-part eschatology* — the Kingdom grows rather than crashing in all at once.
4. **Mustard seed — context.** Mustard is a toxic, unstoppable weed; no farmer would deliberately sow it. Hearers would have laughed.
5. **Mustard seed — four p'shat takeaways.** The Kingdom is (1) small, (2) counterintuitive / something the world doesn't want, (3) unstoppable, and (4) a blessing to everything around it.
6. **Mustard seed — the anomaly.** A mustard plant (shrub) does not become a *tree*; the shrub and the tree are different plants. The impossibility is the signal to look for a remez.
7. **Mustard seed — remez to Ezekiel 17:22–24.** The "unnecessary" detail (birds nesting in the branches) points to Ezekiel's transplanted cedar. An aside: Jesus likely leans on Ezekiel for remezes more than any other prophet, consistent with an Essene-trained rabbi; Brent suggests memorizing Ezekiel.
8. **Ezekiel's imagery decoded.** The tree = God's exiled people transplanted and grown; the birds (and fish/beasts) = the other nations, the Gentiles. The fishermen-on-the-riverbank image connects to "fishers of men."
9. **Mustard seed — drash.** The Kingdom is a small, counterintuitive, unstoppable movement that blesses outsiders; the disciple who knows the Text deduces generosity and love toward the Gentile. Matthew's agenda (the mamzer / outsider) fits perfectly.
10. **Leaven — reading the parable (Matthew 13:33–35).** The woman hides yeast in "about 60 pounds" (three measures) of flour until it is all leavened.
11. **Leaven — p'shat tested against the mustard-seed takeaways.** Small (a little yeast), counterintuitive (leaven = sin in Scripture; removed at Passover; sacrifices must be unleavened), unstoppable (yeast can't be undone), and a blessing (60 lbs feeds far more than herself).
12. **Leaven — remez to Sarah.** The "three measures" / 60 pounds echoes Genesis 18 and Sarah baking three seahs for the three visitors (BEMA Episode 11). The hosts lament the new NIV translating "three measures" as "60 pounds," which obscures the remez.
13. **Radical hospitality and the miracle of the bread.** The rabbis treat Sarah's baking as a miracle and teach that God miraculously blesses radical efforts at hospitality.
14. **The visitors as outsiders.** The three visitors are foreigners / the alien, orphan, widow; Abraham does anything for them. This story is immediately juxtaposed (Genesis 19) with Sodom's *failure* to welcome the visitors.
15. **Leaven — drash.** Exactly parallel to the mustard seed: small, counterintuitive, unstoppable, radical acts of hospitality toward outsiders will start something the world cannot understand or stop. Matthew's Kingdom bends toward the mamzer.
16. **Why Jesus teaches in parables.** Only those with "ears to hear" who truly want the Kingdom will dig, discover, and be transformed — because understanding would require changing how they treat the Gentile, the Roman, the alien, and their own hoarded wealth.
17. **Recommendations.** A sermon on the parable of leaven by Reed Dent's wife at CCF, Truman State University; a shout-out to the CCF sermons podcast.

**Coverage verification:** All major transcript segments are represented in the flow above. No substantive teaching block was dropped. The photo-editing/saturation analogy (Brent's "push the boundaries to figure out where you should be") and the closing CCF banter are not doctrinally load-bearing; the one substantive aside not folded into a Key Lesson — the Ezekiel-memorization exchange — is parked in *Other Talking Points* below.

## Key Lessons

Framed against the Session 3 arc — the King embodying the one story — and how this episode advances it.

### 1. The King defines the Kingdom with deliberately scandalous images

Jesus calls the Kingdom of Heaven a toxic weed and then a lump of leaven — two things his hearers associate with contamination and sin. The shock is the point: the Kingdom is small, counterintuitive, unstoppable, and bent toward blessing whatever is around it.

> **Marty:** "The Kingdom of Heaven is small, starts small, it starts really tiny. The Kingdom of Heaven is counterintuitive... Another one I love to throw in here is that the Kingdom of Heaven is unstoppable."

**Arc advance:** Session 1 established a God who invites partnership to bless all nations. Here the King himself names the shape of that Kingdom — not an empire that crashes in, but something tiny, offensive to the powerful, and impossible to stop.

### 2. The remez pulls the Kingdom back into Israel's whole story

The "unnecessary" detail of birds nesting is Jesus quoting Ezekiel 17: exiled Israel transplanted and grown into a great tree where the Gentiles ("birds of the air") nest. The parable is not a new idea; it is the *fulfillment* of what God promised Abraham.

> **Marty:** "You are finally going to do the mission that I sent you out to do. The mission we saw in Session 1, to bless all nations. What is this tree in Ezekiel? This tree in Ezekiel is the fulfillment of what God told Abraham he was going to do through him."

**Arc advance:** Session 2 traced the partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit. The King now embodies the turning point Ezekiel foresaw — the people finally remembering and doing what they were sent to do, with the nations streaming in.

### 3. Both parables teach the same thing: the Kingdom blesses the outsider

The leaven parable, read through its remez to Sarah's sixty pounds of flour (Genesis 18), turns out to be exactly parallel to the mustard seed. The Kingdom is small, radical acts of hospitality to foreigners — the alien, the orphan, the widow — that the world will never understand.

> **Marty:** "The Kingdom of Heaven is small, small acts of hospitality, small acts of radical hospitality. Small, tiny acts will start something that is counterintuitive and unstoppable because the world will not understand what you're doing."

**Arc advance:** The King embodies the covenant purpose the partner kept forgetting — that the blessing was always *for the nations*. Matthew's "mamzer" agenda makes this the heartbeat of the Kingdom, not an afterthought.

### 4. God miraculously honors radical hospitality

Reading Sarah's baking as the rabbis did — a miracle — the episode draws out a teaching that God blesses extravagant, impractical generosity toward outsiders. Hospitality to the stranger is the small act that the Kingdom multiplies.

> **Marty:** "If you want to practice radical hospitality... The rabbis said, 'God will always miraculously bless your radical efforts at hospitality.'"

**Arc advance:** Where Sodom (Genesis 19) failed the visitors and was destroyed, the King's Kingdom runs in the opposite direction — toward welcome. The one story advances from the partner's self-protective hoarding toward a Kingdom that embodies Abraham-and-Sarah hospitality.

### 5. The King teaches in parables to sort the willing from the unwilling

The parable form is not decoration; it filters. Only those who want the Kingdom badly enough to dig will find it — because truly understanding it demands changing how you treat the Roman, the Gentile, the foreigner, and your own wealth.

> **Marty:** "Only those who have ears to hear who want to find the Kingdom of God are going to actually find this, discover it, and be transformed and made new by the teachings of the Kingdom of Heaven."

**Arc advance:** The King embodies and conceals the story at once, so that the hearer must *choose* to be transformed. The invitation to partnership (Session 1) is reissued as a sifting invitation that costs the hearer their status and security.

## Narrative Tensions

Surface problems the episode raises, each with a classification tag, a cited quote, the relevant text, and either a Hebraic resolution or an honest "Open — dance with it."

**Tension 1 — The Kingdom compared to a toxic weed.** *(Classification: cultural / symbolic anomaly.)* No farmer would sow mustard; it is an unstoppable, noxious weed, yet Jesus likens the Kingdom to a man planting it in his field.
> **Marty:** "Mustard seed is in their world a toxic weed, you cannot get rid of it... When Jesus says, 'the Kingdom of Heaven, it looks like a farmer that went out and planted mustard seed in his field,' Everybody was like, 'Where? Nobody would do that.'"
> **Resolution (Hebraic / symbolic):** The offense is intentional and yields the p'shat principles — the Kingdom is counterintuitive and unstoppable, exactly like the weed. See [Matthew 13:31–32](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+13%3A31-32&version=NIV).

**Tension 2 — A mustard shrub becomes a tree.** *(Classification: botanical / logic impossibility.)* The shrub and the tree are different plants; a mustard shrub does not grow into a tree.
> **Marty:** "When Jesus says the Kingdom of Heaven is like mustard seed, that is the smallest of all garden plants, and yet grows to become a tree, you're like, 'No. No, it's not.' There is something taking place there."
> **Resolution (Hebraic / intertextual):** The impossibility is the dashboard light pointing to the remez — Ezekiel 17, where the Lord "makes the low tree grow tall." The anomaly is the key, not a mistake. See [Ezekiel 17:22–24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+17%3A22-24&version=NIV).

**Tension 3 — The Kingdom compared to leaven, the symbol of sin.** *(Classification: symbolic contradiction.)* Leaven consistently represents sin in Scripture — removed at Passover, forbidden in sacrifices — so comparing the Kingdom to it is jarring.
> **Marty:** "Leaven always represents — Paul will say in Corinthians... [sin]. We have to remove — like in the Passover... remove all the leaven because leaven represents sin."
> **Resolution (Hebraic / symbolic):** Like the mustard seed, the shock is deliberate; a Jewish hearer would never expect a rabbi to pair the Kingdom with leaven, which is precisely why it signals the counterintuitive Kingdom. See [1 Corinthians 5:6–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Corinthians+5%3A6-8&version=NIV) and [Leviticus 2:11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus+2%3A11&version=NIV).

**Tension 4 — "Three measures" rendered as "60 pounds."** *(Classification: translation disagreement.)* The new NIV translates Matthew's "three measures" (three seahs) directly as "60 pounds," erasing the verbal link to Sarah's baking.
> **Marty:** "What Matthew actually says is three measures, because three measures would've made us think of three seahs... and we would've thought about Sarah connected directly in the Text because of the language."
> **Resolution (Hebraic / intertextual):** Restoring "three measures" recovers the remez to Genesis 18; the hosts flag the translation as obscuring (Brent: "that feels a little disingenuous") but recover the connection by the quantity. See [Matthew 13:33](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+13%3A33&version=NIV) and [Genesis 18:1–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+18%3A1-8&version=NIV).

**Tension 5 — Why does Jesus teach so cryptically?** *(Classification: logic / purpose gap.)* If the Kingdom is good news, why conceal it in parables that not everyone will understand?
> **Marty:** "Not everybody wants to understand this, because if people truly understood this, they'd have to change what they do... They don't really want to learn this stuff. I teach them in parables because only people that are truly trying to dig and to find... are going to actually find this."
> **Resolution (Hebraic):** Parables sort hearers by desire; understanding costs the hearer their treatment of the outsider and their hoarded wealth, so only "he who has ears" will press in. See [Matthew 13:34–35](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+13%3A34-35&version=NIV).

## Terminology

- **PaRDeS** — the four interpretive levels: **p'shat** (surface reading), **remez** (a hint pointing back to a Hebrew text), **drash** (the derived wisdom from that text), **sod** (wisdom given directly and only by God). *(Brent: "You have p'shat, remez, drash, and sod. P'shat is like a surface-level reading... Remez is the hint that points you back to an Old Testament scripture... sod is wisdom that you get directly and only from God.")*
- **Remez** — the "hint," the buried reference the teacher expects you to chase; the hosts argue Jesus leans on Ezekiel for remezes more than any other book. *(Marty: "As far as remezes, the remez buried in a teaching, I think Ezekiel is one of the most common go-tos.")*
- **Three-part eschatology** — the Jewish expectation that the Kingdom starts tiny and grows, rather than arriving all at once in a crash of fire and trumpets. *(Marty: "A very definite adherence to a three-part Jewish eschatology. This is not a two-part eschatology where the Kingdom comes with a loud crash.")*
- **Mustard seed** — the smallest of seeds and a toxic, unstoppable weed; the shock image for a counterintuitive, unstoppable Kingdom. *(Marty: "It is a tiny, tiny, tiny little thing, and the Kingdom of God starts with something incredibly tiny.")*
- **Leaven / yeast** — normally the symbol of sin (removed at Passover; sacrifices must be unleavened); applied to the Kingdom to shock the hearer. *(Marty: "Leaven has this negative connotation. When Jesus says the Kingdom of Heaven is like leaven, you have the same reaction.")*
- **Three seahs / "three measures" (~60 lbs)** — the quantity of flour linking the leaven parable to Sarah's baking in Genesis 18. *(Marty: "'Get three seahs of flour and bake it into bread.' Now what Matthew actually says... is three measures.")*
- **Birds of the air** — in Ezekiel, the Gentile nations who come to nest in the tree of God's restored people. *(Marty: "The birds represent who and what, Brent?" / Brent: "Gentile nations.")*
- **Mamzer (mumzer)** — the outsider / one of questionable birth status; Matthew's agenda that the Kingdom bends toward blessing such a one. *(Marty: "The Kingdom of Heaven is bent towards blessing who, Brent?" / Brent: "The mumzer.")*
- **Radical hospitality** — extravagant welcome of the stranger (the alien, orphan, widow), which the rabbis say God miraculously blesses. *(Marty: "If you want to practice radical hospitality... God will always miraculously bless your radical efforts at hospitality.")*

## Illustrations & Images

- **The over-saturated photo.** Brent's photo-editing analogy for learning a technique: crank the saturation all the way up, then all the way down, to find where it belongs — you push the boundaries to learn where the middle is. *(Brent: "Every time you edit, you crank the saturation all the way up, then you crank it all the way down, then you come back to the middle... You have to push the boundaries to figure out where you should be.")*
- **The anvil vs. the seed.** The Kingdom is not an anvil dropping into the world in full force; it is a tiny thing that grows. *(Brent: "The Kingdom of Heaven is not an anvil that is dropping into the world in full force. It is this tiny little thing that starts small and then grows into something great.")*
- **The unkillable weed.** Mustard (like Canadian thistle or ragweed) with a root system you cannot burn, poison, chop, or dig out — a picture of the unstoppable Kingdom. *(Marty: "The root system of these mustard plants is so immense, complicated, and intertwined, you just can't get rid of it.")*
- **Lights on the dashboard.** The "that doesn't make sense" moment as warning lights signaling a hidden remez. *(Marty: "That is the moment where all the lights on your dashboard should be going off.")*
- **Fishermen drying nets on the riverbank.** Ezekiel's end-vision of fishermen catching great fish, connecting to Jesus making "fishers of men." *(Marty: "There's going to come a day where I'm going to call many fishermen and they're going to stand on the bank of the river.")*
- **Sixty pounds of flour on the kitchen counter.** The sheer, miraculous volume of Sarah's baking, imagined domestically. *(Marty: "Imagine 60 pounds of flour on your kitchen counter and how much yeast and how much... baking would have to take place.")*
- **Two neighbors, two welcomes.** Abraham's extravagant welcome of the foreign visitors set directly against Sodom's refusal of them. *(Marty: "This story was about radical hospitality... what does Sodom do with the visitors?" / Brent: "They do not welcome them.")*

## Discussion Questions

1. Jesus calls the Kingdom a toxic weed and a lump of leaven — images his hearers associated with contamination and sin. What does it do to your picture of the Kingdom to call it *counterintuitive* and *unstoppable* rather than powerful and obvious? Where do you expect God to show up like an anvil instead of a seed? *(Marty: "The Kingdom of Heaven is unstoppable. You cannot stop mustard seed, once you get started.")*
2. The remez pulls both parables back to Israel's call to bless the nations (Ezekiel) and to welcome the stranger (Sarah). If the Kingdom "bends toward blessing the mamzer," who are the outsiders your community is least inclined to welcome — and what would a small, mustard-seed act of hospitality toward them look like?
3. Marty says the rabbis taught that "God will always miraculously bless your radical efforts at hospitality." Have you ever practiced hospitality that felt impractical or extravagant? What happened — and what keeps you from it?
4. Both parables turn out to teach the same thing. Why do you think Jesus doubled the image (a farmer *and* a woman, a seed *and* yeast)? What does the pairing — one agricultural, one domestic; one a man, one a woman — communicate that a single parable could not?
5. Jesus teaches in parables, Marty argues, because understanding the Kingdom would force hearers to change how they treat the Roman, the foreigner, and their own wealth. What is one way understanding this Kingdom would cost *you* a change you've been avoiding? *(Marty: "They'd have to change the way that they hoard all their goods in their wealth.")*

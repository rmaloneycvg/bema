# BEMA 117 — Jesus Resorts to Name Calling — Study Guide

**Session 3 (The Gospels) · Episode 117**
**Speakers:** Marty Solomon, Brent Billings, Dr. Christopher Gambino (guest)
**Primary texts:** Matthew 15:1–28; Exodus 21:15–17; Isaiah 29:13; 1 Kings 17:7–16

---

## Overview

This episode runs two scenes from Matthew 15. First, Pharisees and teachers of the law from Jerusalem confront Jesus over his disciples' failure to wash their hands — "the tradition of the elders" — and Jesus answers by going straight to Torah (Exodus 21) and the prophet Isaiah (29:13), charging them with nullifying God's command for the sake of their tradition. Then Jesus withdraws to the pagan region of Tyre and Sidon, where a Canaanite woman begs him to heal her demon-possessed daughter. Jesus ignores her, then tells her he was sent only to the lost sheep of Israel, then uses the first-century slang that likens Gentiles to "dogs." The woman answers with a line about dogs eating "the crumbs that fall from their master's table" — which Marty reads as a remez to Elijah and the widow of Zarephath (1 Kings 17), a story set in that very region — and Jesus declares her faith "great."

Within the Session 3 arc (the king/Messiah embodying the one story), this episode advances the arc on two fronts. Jesus critiques a leadership that has used halakha to "trample over people," re-centering Torah on the protection and dignity of persons — the very partnership and shalom established in Session 1. And in the encounter with the Canaanite woman, Marty entertains the possibility that Jesus himself *learns* the full scope of his mission here: that the King came not only for the lost sheep of Israel but for the Gentile outsider too. The King embodies the story — and this episode suggests he grows into the breadth of it.

> **Marty:** "He thinks this is the story where Jesus goes, 'Oh my goodness, I'm here for the Gentiles as well. I'm not just here for the Jews, but I'm here for everybody, Jew and Gentile.'"

## East/West Lens

BEMA's recurring interpretive contrast between Hebraic (Eastern, concrete, relational, story-shaped) and Greek-Western (abstract, systematized) ways of reading. Only the categories actually engaged in this episode are tagged below.

- **Text in conversation with text (Hebraic / intertextuality).** Jesus critiques the way the leaders handle the Text *by going to the Text* — Exodus 21 to expose the loophole, Isaiah 29 to name the hypocrisy. The woman's reply works the same way, as a remez to a 1 Kings story set in the same geography.
  > **Marty:** "Which I love because he then goes to the text to critique the way that they engage the Text. This is so good. I just love that, so Rabbinic of him."

- **Torah laws as story / commentary, not abstract code (Hebraic).** Gambino reads the Exodus case laws as commentary on the Ten Commandments and as *stories* that became laws — concrete rulings about protecting vulnerable people, not an abstract legal system.
  > **Chris:** "They suggest that this whole thing, all these laws are stories, so the stories of the Torah become laws."

- **Concrete image / geography carries meaning (Hebraic).** The scene is not merely "with the Gentiles"; it is in Sidonian territory, the literal setting of the Elijah story. Western readers skip geography; the Hebraic reading insists the place is the point.
  > **Marty:** "We Gentiles read our New Testaments and we don't pay attention to geography because we could care less... no, he's not just with the Gentiles; it's where he's at. He's at a place where a story already took place."

- **Holding the tension / block logic (Hebraic).** The episode refuses to resolve the "hard sells" (Jesus ignoring the woman, calling her a dog, and possibly *learning*). Marty names them as things that are difficult to buy and declines to flatten them, carrying the tension into the next episode.
  > **Marty:** "There's a lot of things that are like, 'Oh, man, I don't know if I can buy some of this stuff.' I'm going to try to pull it together here..."

- **Reader/listener drawn into the story (Hebraic).** The teaching keeps turning the question back to the hearer — "In the Text!" — and Brent models the p'shat/remez layered reading as the way in.
  > **Brent:** "Before we jump to it, though, should we maybe look at p'shat even?"

## Episode Flow

The transcript's teaching, in order. Coverage verification follows.

1. **Pharisees "from Jerusalem."** Marty wrestles with the detail — likely Judean, more "sophisticated," rather than the devoted Galilean Pharisees of Capernaum.
2. **The charge: breaking "the tradition of the elders."** Hand-washing is not in Torah; it is halakha — the fence around the 613 that becomes Mishnah and Talmud.
3. **Jesus turns the charge around (Matthew 15:3–6).** "Why do you break the command of God for the sake of your tradition?" — the Corban/devoted-to-God loophole used to avoid honoring father and mother.
4. **Marty as a Torah-observant Jesus-follower.** He interprets his Judaism through Jesus; Jesus thinks critically about halakha and won't follow a ruling that nullifies written Torah.
5. **Jesus goes to Exodus 21 (Gambino).** 21:15 and 21:17 (strike / curse father or mother → death), with the kidnapping verse (21:16) between them; the whole section is case law about protecting people.
6. **Stealing a name.** The kidnapping verse: to "steal" a person is to rob their essence and dignity — which is what cursing one's parents does.
7. **Isaiah 29:13 quoted (Matthew 15:7–9).** "These people honor me with their lips, but their hearts are far from me" — Jesus critiques their handling of the Text with the Text.
8. **"What defiles" teaching (Matthew 15:10–20).** Not what goes into the mouth but what comes out of the heart; the list of defiling things (murder, adultery, theft, false testimony, slander) maps onto "the back half of the 10 commandments" — things that hurt other people.
9. **Blind guides / the offended Pharisees / Peter's dullness.** "Every plant my Father has not planted will be pulled up"; Marty wonders about the tone of Jesus calling Peter "dull."
10. **Withdrawal to Tyre and Sidon (Matthew 15:21).** Jesus seeks R&R after John the Baptist's beheading; the only way to escape the crowds is to go to a pagan region.
11. **"Canaanite" vs. "Syrophoenician."** Matthew (Jewish audience) says Canaanite to mark the outsider of the biblical narrative; Mark (Roman audience) says Syrophoenician. There are no Canaanites anymore.
12. **Jesus ignores the woman; the disciples beg him to act.** Her persistence (chutzpah) forces the disciples to speak.
13. **"The lost sheep of Israel."** Jesus states his mission is to Israel — to tell his Pharisaic world to be on mission and love people like her — not to the Gentiles directly.
14. **"The Gentiles don't take over the covenant?"** Gambino's sarcastic prompt; Marty rejects replacement theology outright.
15. **"The children's bread... to the dogs."** The first-century Jewish slang for Gentiles (dogs/pigs; cf. Ezekiel's birds and fish for the nations) — not as harsh in that world as it sounds in English.
16. **The woman's reply — the crumbs.** "Even the dogs eat the crumbs that fall from their master's table," spoken with chutzpah.
17. **Jesus learns things (Philippians 2, John, Hebrews).** Marty's "hard sell": Jesus set aside divine advantage and functioned as a man who learned, learned obedience, learned from the Father.
18. **Ray Vander Laan's reading.** This may be the moment the lights go on for Jesus — that he is here for Gentiles too, potentially altering the trajectory of his ministry.
19. **P'shat first (Brent).** On the surface, "crumbs from the master's table" means "we have the same master / the same God."
20. **Remez to 1 Kings 17:7–16 (Gambino reads it).** Elijah sent to Zarephath "that belongs to Sidon" — the same region Jesus is in now.
21. **The widow eats crumbs.** A starvation-ration — "just enough," a Session 1 callback — literally a story about a widow eating crumbs.
22. **Elijah as the first "master" / rav.** The Hebrew for master shares the root of rabbi; Elijah and Elisha are the first master–disciple pair. The woman "knows her Text" — she knows this story happened here — and that is what blows Jesus away.
23. **Wrap-up / the hard sells.** Marty names the stretch (Jesus learning; the earlier feeding-of-the-5,000 numbers) and promises to pull it together next episode; guest and co-host plugs.

**Coverage verification:** All major transcript segments are represented in the flow above. No significant teaching block was dropped. Minor asides (the opening banter re-introducing Dr. Gambino, the "Every Verse(tm)" bit, the closing Twitter/website plug for The Shuvah Project) are not doctrinally load-bearing; the one substantive aside not folded into a Key Lesson — the question of what Greek-vs-original-language Matthew does to the "dull" line — is parked in *Other Talking Points* below.

## Key Lessons

Framed against the Session 3 arc — the King embodying the one story — and how this episode advances it.

### 1. The King re-centers Torah on people, against traditions that trample them

Jesus's whole critique of the hand-washing charge is that the leaders have used their traditions to get around "the very heart of Torah." Gambino shows the case laws Jesus cites are about protecting people — personal injury, theft, the vulnerable — while the tradition in question is "really about your own little thing, not so much how to protect people."

> **Marty:** "You've actually used your traditions to get around what is at times the very heart of Torah."

> **Chris:** "The traditions that he's calling out here are really about your own little thing, not so much how to protect people."

**Arc advance:** Session 1 established partnership and the dignity of persons; Session 2 traced leaders who kept missing it. Here the King embodies the correction in person, insisting that Torah's point is people and the heart — "even more practical than the practical."

### 2. Defilement is a matter of the heart, and the heart is measured by how you treat people

Jesus relocates purity from the hands to the heart. The list of defiling things that "come out of the mouth" — murder, adultery, theft, false testimony, slander — is, as Brent notices, a catalogue of harms done to other people: the back half of the Ten Commandments.

> **Marty:** "Out of your heart is what you actually believe about people and it drives how you treat people in the first place, and so that's where the cleanliness lies."

> **Brent:** "Everything He names is something that hurts someone else."

**Arc advance:** The King embodies a righteousness that is relational, not ritual — the shalom-shaped life Israel was always invited into, now spoken by the Messiah himself.

### 3. The King's mission widens — possibly as Jesus himself learns it

Marty's central "hard sell": in his human nature, Jesus is a learner (Philippians 2; John; Hebrews). Ray Vander Laan reads the Canaanite woman as the moment the lights go on — Jesus realizing he is here for Gentiles as well as Jews, potentially altering the trajectory of his ministry.

> **Marty:** "I'd like you to consider the idea that Jesus learns things. He can be unaware of a certain truth because Jesus is operating in his total human nature."

**Arc advance:** The one story has always strained toward "all nations." Here the King who embodies the story grows into the full breadth of it — explicitly without replacement theology; Israel's covenant is not taken over, it is widened.

### 4. Faith looks like a Gentile woman who knows the Text

What astounds Jesus is not merely the woman's persistence but her knowledge of Scripture. Marty believes she knows the story of Elijah and the widow of Zarephath happened right there, and her "crumbs from the master's table" is a remez to it — Elijah being the first "master" / rav.

> **Marty:** "This woman in the story of Jesus knows her Text and it blows him away."

**Arc advance:** The outsider embodies the covenant-shaped faith the insiders were failing to show — a reversal that advances the one story toward the nations and vindicates the King's widening mission.

### 5. Critique the Text with the Text

Jesus answers an accusation about his handling of tradition by appealing to Torah (Exodus 21) and prophet (Isaiah 29). The leaders are judged not by Jesus's opinion but by Scripture turned back on their own practice.

> **Marty:** "He then goes to the text to critique the way that they engage the Text... so Rabbinic of him."

**Arc advance:** The King embodies the story by living inside its Text, modeling the Hebraic reading that lets Scripture interpret Scripture — the method the whole session is teaching.

## Narrative Tensions

Surface problems the episode raises, each with a classification tag, a cited quote, the relevant text, and either a Hebraic resolution or an honest "Open — dance with it."

**Tension 1 — Jesus ignores a desperate mother and then calls her a "dog."** *(Classification: apparent contradiction with Jesus's character.)* Jesus does not answer her a word, then says it is not right to throw the children's bread to the dogs.
> **Marty:** "Just when you thought the story couldn't get any worse — Jesus ignoring the woman, Jesus telling her I'm not here for you — did Jesus just call her a dog?"
> **Resolution (Hebraic / cultural):** "Dogs" (and "pigs") was standard first-century Jewish slang for Gentiles — cf. Ezekiel's birds and fish for the nations — and does not carry the derogatory punch English readers hear. Marty notes it "doesn't have that connotation that we might read into it," though he concedes it "still doesn't get rid of the actual problem here." See [Matthew 15:21–28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+15%3A21-28&version=NIV).

**Tension 2 — Can Jesus actually learn something he did not know?** *(Classification: theological / Christological tension.)* Marty asks the listener to consider that Jesus was unaware of the full scope of his mission until this encounter.
> **Marty:** "He can be unaware of a certain truth because Jesus is operating in his total human nature. I would never take away from Jesus's divinity... but I also believe that Philippians 2 teaches us very clearly that Jesus set his God nature aside and came to function as a man."
> **Resolution (Hebraic / block logic):** Held in tension rather than flattened: fully divine *and* a genuine human learner, grounded in Philippians 2 (did not grasp equality with God), John ("everything I have learned from my Father"), and Hebrews ("learned obedience"). Marty affirms "historic Christian Orthodoxy" while holding both. See [Philippians 2:5–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Philippians+2%3A5-8&version=NIV) and [Hebrews 5:8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews+5%3A8&version=NIV).

**Tension 3 — Does the mission belong to Israel or to the Gentiles?** *(Classification: covenant/mission tension.)* Jesus says he was sent "only to the lost sheep of Israel," yet he has been preaching about the Gentiles throughout Matthew and ends up healing a Gentile.
> **Chris:** "So the Gentiles don't take over the covenant?"
> **Resolution (Hebraic):** No replacement theology. Jesus's job is to turn his Pharisaic world back to its mission to love outsiders like her; Brent reads the Gentiles as a *prompt* to Israel — "I'm seeing more of what you're supposed to be doing in the Gentiles than I am in you." The covenant is widened, not transferred. See [Matthew 15:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+15%3A24&version=NIV).

**Tension 4 — The kidnapping verse wedged between two commands about parents.** *(Classification: textual-ordering puzzle.)* Exodus 21:16 (kidnapping) sits oddly between 21:15 and 21:17 (striking/cursing father and mother), both of which Jesus quotes.
> **Marty:** "Is there anything particularly important about the kidnapping verse that lies in the middle of those two references that Jesus quotes?"
> **Resolution (Hebraic / semantic):** To "kidnap" / steal a person is, in the Hebrew idiom, to rob their essence and identity — "stealing one's name" — exactly what cursing one's parents does by robbing their dignity. The ordering is thematic, not accidental. See [Exodus 21:15–17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus+21%3A15-17&version=NIV).

**Tension 5 — What exactly turned Jesus's "no" into a "yes" in one sentence?** *(Classification: logic/motivation gap.)* The woman's single reply flips Jesus's position entirely.
> **Marty:** "What was it about that statement that gets Jesus to turn His opinion on its head in one single sentence?"
> **Resolution (Hebraic / remez):** Her "crumbs from the master's table" is read as a remez to 1 Kings 17 — Elijah, the first "master"/rav, and the widow of Zarephath in Sidonian territory, who survived on starvation-ration crumbs in the very region where Jesus now stands. She knows the Text, and that is what astounds him. See [1 Kings 17:7–16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Kings+17%3A7-16&version=NIV).

**Tension 6 — Jesus calling Peter "dull" / "foolish."** *(Classification: translation / tone uncertainty.)* The line reads harshly in English, and Marty is unsure of the original tone — or even the original language.
> **Marty:** "I've done word studies and I'm not sure that Matthew is written in the Greek, to begin with... I want to know more about the heart and the tone with which Jesus said that to Peter."
> **Open — dance with it:** The underlying language and tone are left genuinely unresolved; Marty flags the uncertainty rather than settling it. See [Matthew 15:15–16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+15%3A15-16&version=NIV).

## Terminology

- **Halakha** (from *halakh*, "to walk") — the rabbinic rulings on how to live out Torah; "the tradition of the elders"; the fence around the 613 that becomes Mishnah, then Talmud. *(Marty: "Halakha is the walk — that's what it means. It's the way that you walk, and they use that term to reference the Rabbinic rulings.")*
- **The tradition of the elders** — the named charge against the disciples; not written Torah. *(Brent: "The tradition of the elders.")*
- **The fence around the 613** — the protective rulings surrounding the 613 commands, becoming Mishnah and Talmud. *(Marty: "That fence, which ends up becoming the Mishnah, which ends up becoming the Talmud.")*
- **Corban / devoted to God** — the loophole: declaring resources "devoted to God" to avoid honoring one's parents. *(Brent: "What might have been used to help their father or mother is devoted to God.")*
- **Stealing a name / kidnapping** — the Hebrew idiom in Exodus 21:16: robbing a person's essence and dignity, not merely their body. *(Marty: "To kidnap isn't just taking the physicality of their person and their body. The idea is that you're robbing their actual essence and identity.")*
- **Dogs / pigs** — first-century Jewish slang for Gentiles; cf. Ezekiel's birds and fish for the nations. *(Marty: "It was cultural slang in the first century in the Jewish world to refer to the Gentiles as dogs or even pigs.")*
- **Chutzpah** — bold audacity; the Canaanite/Syrophoenician woman's persistence. *(Marty: "This girl's got some chutzpah, I like it.")*
- **Master / rav** — the Hebrew for "master" shares the root of *rabbi*; Elijah is the first "master" with a disciple (Elisha). *(Marty: "The word master in the Hebrew is the same root word that rabbi comes from.")*
- **P'shat** — the plain/surface reading of a text. *(Brent: "Should we maybe look at p'shat even?")*
- **Remez** — the "hint": a reading that points to another text by allusion. *(Marty: "You started with p'shat, let's go remez.")*
- **Jesus as learner** — in his human nature Jesus learns, per Philippians 2, John, and Hebrews. *(Marty: "Jesus is a learner... He learned things. He came to realize things.")*

## Illustrations & Images

- **Backwoods Galileans vs. sophisticated Judeans.** Marty's (self-qualified) picture of the Jerusalem Pharisees as a "different, sophisticated Judaism" versus "backwoods, charismatic, hillbilly Galileans" — while insisting the Galilean Pharisees of Capernaum were "unbelievably devoted and unbelievably educated." *(Marty: "Instead of like backwoods, charismatic, hillbilly Galileans... these are a different, sophisticated Judaism, perhaps.")*
- **Loopholes in the church system.** Marty's prod that modern religion still invents loopholes to get around the heart of the command. *(Marty: "Of course, I'm sure we don't do this much today... We never come up with loopholes. I'm prodding right now.")*
- **"Even more practical than the practical."** Jesus pushing past ritual cleanliness to the heart. *(Marty: "Just Jesus getting even more practical than the practical, if you will.")*
- **Going off with Misha / escaping to pagan territory.** The only way to lose the Jewish crowds is to flee to a pagan nation they won't follow him into. *(Marty: "The only way he's going to get away from these crowds of Jewish observers is to go to a pagan nation.")*
- **R&R that lasts two verses.** Jesus's attempt at rest interrupted almost immediately by a woman in need. *(Marty: "It takes a mere two verses, Mr. Brent Billings, for him to encounter somebody who needs his attention.")*
- **"Packing" — reading context into the derogatory.** Ezekiel's birds and fish and animals as symbols for the nations, framing "dogs" as ordinary slang. *(Marty: "What comes to nest in the trees? The birds... What are we catching in a net? Fish.")*
- **Starvation-ration crumbs.** The widow of Zarephath surviving on "just enough" bread, literally eating crumbs — the image behind the woman's reply. *(Marty: "This story is literally about a widow who eats crumbs — literally. Eats crumbs.")*
- **"In the Text!"** The recurring punchline that the answer is always found by returning to Scripture. *(Brent: "In the Text!")*

## Discussion Questions

1. Jesus charges the leaders with using tradition to get around "the very heart of Torah," and Gambino notes the traditions were "really about your own little thing, not so much how to protect people." Where do our own religious practices risk becoming loopholes that let us trample over actual people?
2. Jesus relocates defilement from the hands to the heart, and Brent observes that everything on Jesus's list "is something that hurts someone else." If cleanliness is measured by how you treat people, what does that change about how you'd assess your own "purity"?
3. Marty asks us to consider that Jesus, in his human nature, *learned* — that the Canaanite woman may be where the scope of his mission clicks. How does the idea of a learning Jesus sit with you, and does holding it alongside his full divinity (Philippians 2) enrich or unsettle your reading?
4. The outsider — a Gentile woman — turns out to know the Text well enough to answer Jesus with a remez to Elijah and the widow of Zarephath. What does it mean that the one with "great faith" here is the outsider, and where might the people we write off be reading the story better than we are?
5. Jesus critiques the way the leaders handle Scripture *by going back to Scripture*. What would it look like for us to let the Text, rather than our opinions, do the critiquing in our own disputes?

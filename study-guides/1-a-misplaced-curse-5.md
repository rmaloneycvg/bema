# A Misplaced Curse

BEMA 5 · Session 1
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/1-a-misplaced-curse-5.md`
Book: Genesis (Genesis 9:18–29; with Deuteronomy 22:30)
Guest: Reed Dent (BEMA teaching team; campus minister, Campus Christian Fellowship, Truman State University)

> The Noah story "that nobody usually talks about" — not the ark but the vineyard, the drunkenness, and Noah's curse landing on the wrong person. The hosts read it as a deliberate parallel to the Garden of Eden and let the Midrash's startling castration reading expose the real subject: the cancerous spread of **vengeance**.

## Key Lessons

- **The real failure here is Noah's, not Ham's — and the lesson is the danger of vengeance.** Marty: *"if the lesson isn't about Ham's wrong, but about Noah's wrong, then obviously the offender just became... Noah."* And: *"vengeance never truly stays where it ought to... that becomes the lesson for the story."* God's constant invitation — stop the cycle of destruction — is refused again. Trusting the story means trusting that God's priorities (mercy over vengeance) do not change even when the hero fails.
- **This is one more "will you trust the invitation to stop?" story in a chain.** Marty threads the arc explicitly: creation = learn to stop *creating*; Adam & Eve = will you trust that invitation (no); flood = learn to stop *destroying*; and now Noah = *"Can you stop destroying? And the answer... ends up tragically being no."* Same God, same invitation, repeated.
- **The Hebrew Scriptures' capacity for self-criticism is a feature, not a bug.** Reed: *"nobody is just clean all the way through... the Hebrew scriptures decide to actually look at the problems that maybe we have created."* He cites Richard Rohr: *"any nation that loses the capacity for self-criticism will become idolatrous."* The story even hints the Canaanites' origin lies partly in *Noah's* fault, not only in disobedience — "what about me?" is the posture the text invites.
- **Noah is cast as the God-character — and plays the part badly.** Marty: *"Noah's the only human character in the entire scripture to be given that word for curse... Only God engages in it."* Reed's contrast: in Eden God curses the ground and the serpent, never the humans directly; Noah instead *"lays it on a person where it's not supposed to be... he puts it on the wrong person."* Being made in God's image is not the same as living out of it.

## East/West Lens

- **Truth (how/what vs who/why)** — Marty warns against getting snagged on mechanics: *"a lot of Westerners will get hung up in the details. How did he not wake up in the midst of a castration?... we might be messing with the Midrash is trying to get us to see."* The point is who/why (vengeance, our complicity), not forensic how.
- **Words (abstract definition vs word-picture/idiom)** — "To see / uncover nakedness" is an idiom, not literal looking; parallel to **shema** ("hear" meaning hear-perceive-obey). Brent's familiar example: *"Adam knew his wife Eve and she became pregnant."* Words carry loaded images, not flat definitions.
- **Numbers (quantity vs quality/symbol)** — Brent: *"the number four is often used to refer to the Gentiles... that's supposed to be these people who scatter over the whole earth, the four corners of the earth, but there's only three sons."* Four rivers in Eden vs four expected sons — numbers signal meaning.
- **Truth over time (static vs dynamic/discovery)** — Midrash is framed as discovery, not information delivery: *"the Midrash takes you all the way around the block to go right next door... It's a way of burying treasure."* Meaning unfolds through wrestling.

## Recurring Through-lines

- **Trust the story (the arc itself).** This episode is an explicit link in the "will you trust the invitation to stop?" chain the hosts have been building since Genesis 1. Marty: creation→stop creating, flood→stop destroying, *"the next story is now the invitation for Noah to trust. Can you stop destroying?"* God's desire is constant; the human answer keeps coming back "no." Recurs across the session; condensed at the Session 1 capstone (episode 32).
- **Sin as failing to master the animal instinct ("master the beast").** Strongly present, though not named by that phrase here: Noah's failure *is* a failure to master a base impulse — vengeance — once provoked. Marty: *"If only Noah could have... decided to know when to stop destroying, to say the cycle's going to stop with me. But vengeance is so dangerous."* This is the same "fail to master the beast" dynamic anchored in `1-master-the-beast-3.md`. Flag for cross-reference; do not re-explain there.

## Eden Parallels (structure)

Not a chiasm, but the hosts build a deliberate **parallel panel** between the vineyard story and the Garden of Eden — and insist the "treasure" is where the two *diverge*. Reed: *"I often find that the meatiest part of the treasure... is in what then is different."*

| Element | Garden of Eden | Noah's vineyard |
|---|---|---|
| A garden/planting | the Garden | a vineyard |
| Fruit → trouble | eat the fruit, fall | drink the wine, drunkenness |
| Nakedness | naked and unashamed, *then* a problem | a problem "from the get-go" |
| Covering the nakedness | God clothes them | Shem & Japheth cover Noah |
| A curse | God curses *ground* and *serpent*, not the people | Noah curses a *person* — and the wrong one |
| "What have you done?" | God's question to Adam | Noah emerges demanding the same |
| Rivers / sons | **four** rivers | **three** sons (the expected 4th lost to castration) |

Prose: the parallels mark Noah as the God-figure of this panel; the *divergences* (curse mislaid on a person/grandson; four-becomes-three) are where the lesson about vengeance surfaces.

## Terminology

- **Canaan** (כנען) — Ham's son, conspicuously named; foreshadows Israel's fraught relationship with the Canaanites. Marty notes the following genealogy is thick with nation-names (**Mitzrayim** = Egypt, **Kush**, Canaan). Reed: *"is it weird or conspicuous that the name is Canaan?"*
- **shema** (שמע) — "to hear," but far deeper: to listen, perceive, understand, obey. Marty: *"the word 'shema' is so much deeper than just 'hear.'"* Used as the parallel for how "to see nakedness" exceeds mere looking.
- **"to see / uncover nakedness"** — Hebrew idioms pointing beyond literal sight — sexual union, abuse, or "seeing as taking." The Midrash lands on **castration**, explaining the curse on Ham's progeny.
- **Midrash** — ancient Jewish commentary that teaches by discovery, "burying treasure." Marty: *"the Midrash takes you all the way around the block to go right next door."* Not treated as inspired — "as inspired as the commentary that sits on my shelf from a Christian theologian."
- **Principle (Law) of First Mention** — the habit of asking where an image first appears, letting that first use frame later ones. Marty: *"whenever you see a vineyard in Scripture, it's possible that it might bring you back to this very story."* (First *love* appears in the Akedah.)

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty says vengeance "never truly stays where it ought to" — it reaches "our kids and our grandkids... even nations." Where have you seen a cycle of retaliation outlive its original offense? What would it take to be the one who says "the cycle stops with me"?
2. The castration reading strikes many listeners as "crazy," yet it makes the misplaced curse and the missing fourth son cohere. When has an uncomfortable interpretation actually explained a text *better* than the comfortable one for you?
3. Reed quotes Richard Rohr: "any nation that loses the capacity for self-criticism will become idolatrous." How does a story that implicates its own hero (Noah) model that posture — and where might your own community need it?
4. Noah is handed the God-character's word for "curse" and uses it to wound rather than to bless. What's the difference between being *made* in God's image and *living out of* it?
5. This is the third "will you trust the invitation to stop?" story in a row (stop creating, stop destroying...). Why might God keep issuing the same invitation across different characters, and what does its repeated refusal reveal about the story God is still telling?
6. The text spends the rivers/sons detail and the shifting birth order without fully explaining them. Which unresolved detail are you most tempted to force shut — and can you "dance with it" instead?

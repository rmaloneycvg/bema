**Session:** 2 (The Prophets)

**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings

## Where This Sits in the One Story

Session 1 built the picture of **partnership**: God rescued a people out of Egypt and summed up Torah in one word — *partner* — inviting Israel to be his servant in the world. Session 2 turns to the **Prophets**, and the arc of this stage is the partner's (Israel's) **unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** 3 Isaiah — the *exilic* voice, chapters 40–54 — advances that arc past warning into the heart of the consequence: Babylon has come, the partner sits in exile, and the tone shifts from the vineyard-indictment of 1 Isaiah and the woes of 2 Isaiah to **comfort**. God's refusal to quit here takes its most startling form yet: he reaches *back* to Torah's one-word summary and re-names the exiled, failed partner "my servant, whom I have chosen and have not rejected." The suffering servant of Isaiah 53 is, in its first-order meaning, Israel itself — crushed in exile, yet promised that perseverance will bring healing to its children. This episode shows the arc at its sharpest: the partner has failed and is suffering the exile its unfaithfulness invited, and God, instead of discarding the servant, assigns the suffering a redemptive purpose and keeps the partnership alive.

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **Four voices of Isaiah, now distinguishing a fourth** — Marty, following Walter Brueggemann's *Out of Babylon*, now separates 3 Isaiah (chs. 40–54) from a forthcoming 4 Isaiah; today wraps up the exilic prophets.
2. **The famous chapter** — Isaiah 53, "the suffering servant passage," immediately recognized and conventionally connected to Jesus; Brent reads 53:1-5.
3. **"Absolutely, we find Jesus in Isaiah 53" — but that is not its first point** — Marty affirms seeing Jesus here while insisting it was not the primary meaning when written.
4. **The crucial correction: Jesus points to the Tanakh, not the reverse** — Brent reframes ("Jesus lived his life to portray what God had been doing"), Marty affirms and names the usual backwards (and offensive) Christian framing.
5. **Isaiah 40 — the tone turns to comfort** — "Comfort, comfort my people... a voice crying in the wilderness, prepare the way of the LORD."
6. **Review of the Isaiah images** — 1 Isaiah = vineyard, 2 Isaiah = woe, and now 3 Isaiah = servant; locating the episode in the exile.
7. **The servant named explicitly before 53** — Isaiah 41:8-10 (Israel/Jacob my servant), 43:10 (my servant whom I have chosen), 44:1-2 (Jacob my servant, Yeshurun) — the servant is God's whole people.
8. **God reaches back to Torah's "partner"** — "I was looking for a partner and I found you; you are my servant."
9. **Reading Isaiah 53 as the climax of the servant discourse** — the singular "he" is the metaphor for the people; the exile is why they suffer, and perseverance brings healing to the generations after them.
10. **The communal reading of Scripture** — Brent: Jews read "*we* were in Egypt, *we* did it"; the "he" and the "our" are the same people across generations; the out-of-body observer grammar and the later-date argument.
11. **Jumping back to Jesus at the appropriate point** — Jesus shows us *how* to live out Isaiah 53; the sharpest point: it is an invitation, not an exemption ("not so that we wouldn't have to").
12. **Pastoral close** — the Greek individualism that keeps us outside the story as observers; it takes years to stop seeing only Jesus in Isaiah 53.

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 2 arc — the covenant partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **God re-names the failed, exiled partner "my servant" — he refuses to quit.** The clearest advance on the arc: the people whose unfaithfulness landed them in Babylon are not discarded; God reaches back to Torah's "partner" and reclaims them.
   - *Marty:* "He says you are my servant. I chose you, I was looking for partners... God goes back to Torah and I was looking for a partner and I found you. You are my servant."
   - *Brent (reading Isaiah 41):* "I said, you are my servant. I have chosen you and have not rejected you. Do not fear for I am with you."

2. **The suffering servant of Isaiah 53 is, first, Israel in exile — not initially a prediction of Jesus.** Read in context as the climax of the 40–54 discourse, the chapter explains *why* the partner suffers, which is exactly the stage of the arc this episode occupies.
   - *Marty:* "When Isaiah 53 was written, Isaiah was not writing about Jesus... Isaiah 53 is a part of a much larger discourse... Jews see it as such."
   - *Marty:* "the servant was then taken to Babylon and the servant was then punished, pierced, striped, crushed... because the servant perseveres... we are healed."

3. **Suffering, borne faithfully, is given redemptive purpose for the generations to come.** God does not merely preserve the partner; he assigns the exile meaning — endure, and your children are healed.
   - *Marty:* "You have to endure this exile... Because if you suffer, your children and the generations that come after you will be healed."

4. **Jesus points back to the Tanakh and validates it; the Tanakh does not merely point forward to Jesus.** Correcting the common framing matters for the whole one-story reading — the partnership narrative was never pointless filler awaiting a replacement.
   - *Brent:* "Jesus lived his life in a way that portrayed what Scripture and what God has been doing for all of eternity."
   - *Marty:* "it's not that Tanakh points toward Jesus, it's that Jesus points toward Tanakh and says, 'I'm going to live this out perfectly.'"

5. **Isaiah 53 is an invitation to the partnership, not an exemption from it.** The servant vocation is not something Jesus did *instead* of us; it is what God's people before and after Jesus are called to live.
   - *Marty:* "That's the exact opposite point of Isaiah 53... not so that we wouldn't have to... Jesus suffered so that I could experience healing and know how to suffer as well, to know how to suffer on behalf of others."
   - *Marty:* "Sometimes, you're called to be a servant, even a suffering servant. We do that so that other people can find the hope."

## East/West Lens

Only categories genuinely engaged by this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Communal identity over individual autonomy (East).** The Jewish reading places the reader inside the story — "we were in Egypt, we did it" — so the servant and the "we" are one people across generations.
  - *Brent:* "when the Jews read Scripture, they consider themselves as part of the story... It's 'We were in Egypt.'... even if it was our parents or grandparents... we did it."

- **Entering the story as participant over holding it as abstract observer (East vs. West).** The episode explicitly names Greek individualism as what keeps us outside the text, and the servant vocation as something to partake in.
  - *Brent:* "The Greek-like individualistic thinking that we all have makes it really hard to put ourselves into the story."
  - *Marty:* "we always like to look at it as an observer, as abstract. We hold it as an abstract thing rather than entering into it as a participant."

- **Reading in context / whole-discourse over proof-text (East).** The Hebraic reader refuses to let Isaiah 53 stand alone in a vacuum, hearing it only as the peak of the 40–54 servant discourse.
  - *Marty:* "They're not going to let Isaiah 53 stand on its own in a vacuum. Isaiah 53 is a part of a much larger conversation, and Jews see it as such."

- **Lived word (orthopraxy) over abstract doctrine (East).** Jesus validates Scripture by *living* its word (logos) rather than by functioning as a proposition the text was pointing toward.
  - *Marty:* "pick whatever you want and I'm going to live out the message, the heart, the word — logos... I'm going to live that word out, as purely and as perfectly... as any interpretation that you could engage in."

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension states the problem, tags its type, cites the transcript, links the passage, and gives a Hebraic resolution (or marks it open).

1. **Does Isaiah 53 point to Jesus, or not?**
   - *Type:* Apparent contradiction (interpretive / Christological).
   - *Problem:* Marty both affirms "we find Jesus in Isaiah 53" and insists "Isaiah was not writing about Jesus" — seemingly contradictory claims about the same chapter.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "we, of course, see Jesus in Isaiah 53... But without a doubt, I find Jesus in Isaiah 53." and "When Isaiah 53 was written, Isaiah was not writing about Jesus."
   - *Passage:* [Isaiah 53:1-5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+53%3A1-5&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The directionality is the key. The chapter's first-order referent is Israel the servant in exile; Jesus is its *fulfillment* not because Isaiah foresaw a particular rabbi, but because Jesus lived the servant vocation perfectly and so pointed back to validate the whole Tanakh. Both are true once you stop making the text a mere prediction.

2. **If the servant is Israel (plural), why is "he" singular?**
   - *Type:* Grammatical / logic gap.
   - *Problem:* The discourse names a corporate servant (Israel, Jacob) yet speaks of a singular "he" who is pierced for "our" transgressions — the pronouns seem to split one group into two.
   - *Cited:* *Brent:* "it's like he was pierced for our transgressions, but the he and the our are both referring to the same group in some various ways."
   - *Passage:* [Isaiah 41:8-10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+41%3A8-10&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The singular "he/servant" is a deliberate metaphor for the whole people. *Marty:* "'You are my servant, Jacob, my people, Israel, who I have chosen.' You are my servant — singular. This is the image that gets played over and over." The out-of-body "we looking at the servant" grammar is the community reflecting on its own corporate suffering.

3. **How many authors / voices are in Isaiah, and when was 3 Isaiah written?**
   - *Type:* Authorship / dating disagreement.
   - *Problem:* The grammar of Isaiah 53 reads like people looking back long after the exile, prompting debate over the number of voices and a possible later date for this one.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "some have even argued for a later date to the third voice because it's almost like people are looking back not in the exile but long after the exile... is that really just four voices, are there voices upon voices upon voices?"
   - *Passage:* [Isaiah 53:1-5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+53%3A1-5&version=NIV)
   - *Resolution:* **Open — dance with it.** Marty declines to settle the date ("I won't try to figure out where to date it"); the retrospective, communal grammar is acknowledged as real evidence without forcing a conclusion.

4. **"Jesus did it so I don't have to" vs. the servant invitation.**
   - *Type:* Cultural assumption / theological value clash.
   - *Problem:* The familiar reading treats Isaiah 53 (and the cross) as an exemption from suffering, which the episode calls the exact opposite of the text's point.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "How many times have we read Isaiah 53... and thought, 'Oh, yes... I'm so glad that Jesus did it so that I don't have to.' That's the exact opposite point of Isaiah 53."
   - *Passage:* [Isaiah 53:1-5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+53%3A1-5&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The servant discourse is participatory. Jesus shows *how* to suffer on behalf of others so that others find healing; the people of God are invited to partake in the servant vocation, not to be excused from it (Marty points ahead to Hebrews in Session 4).

## Terminology

- **servant** (the image for 3 Isaiah) — the exilic voice (chs. 40–54) is the servant discourse; "servant" is the one-word image, as vineyard was for 1 Isaiah and woe for 2 Isaiah.
  - *Marty:* "'servant' is going to be the image when we review 3 Isaiah. We're going to say 3 Isaiah, and our listeners are going to think servant."
- **partner** — Torah's one-word summary, which God reaches back to in naming Israel his servant.
  - *Marty:* "the word for Torah if we were to sum up Torah in one word... Partner, right? God goes back to Torah and I was looking for a partner and I found you."
- **Jeshurun / Yeshurun** — an affectionate name for Israel used in Isaiah 44:2 ("my servant Jacob, Jeshurun, whom I have chosen").
  - *Brent (reading Isaiah 44):* "Do not be afraid Jacob, my servant. Yeshurun whom I have chosen."
- **logos / word** — the word God's people are to *live out*; Jesus validates the Tanakh by living its logos perfectly (Marty flags it as a Session 3 topic).
  - *Marty:* "I'm going to live out the message, the heart, the word — logos — we're not in session three yet, but I'm going to live that word out."
- **suffering servant passage** — the conventional name for Isaiah 53, famous and recognized even by those who don't know the reference.
  - *Marty:* "It is the famous — what we call 'the suffering servant passage' and we connect it obviously to Jesus."
- **exilic** — the period of 3 Isaiah: Babylon has conquered and carried the people into exile, changing the prophet's tone to comfort.
  - *Brent:* "Exilic." / *Marty:* "Exilic. Right? We're in the exile. Babylon has come."

## Illustrations & Images

- **"Lowly slaves in Egypt" as the servant's unimpressive beginnings.** When Isaiah 53 says the servant "grew up like a tender shoot... out of dry ground," Brent names Israel's origin — nobody would look at lowly Egyptian slaves and expect something awesome.
  - *Marty:* "Nobody would've looked at our beginnings and said, well, this is the making of something awesome." / *Brent:* "Lowly slaves in Egypt."
- **The out-of-body observer hovering over the servant.** Marty pictures the voice of 53 stepping outside itself — "almost like an out-of-body experience, this hovering observer" — to explain to the community why it had to suffer.
  - *Marty:* "This voice is stepping back as the servant discourse comes to a close and says, if we are his servant let us look at this servant. Almost like an out-of-body experience, this hovering observer."
- **"We were in Egypt" — the communal reading.** Brent's image of Jewish identity: not "those Jews back then" but "we were in Egypt," "we did it," collapsing the distance between generations.
  - *Brent:* "Not 'Those Jews were back in Egypt and we're here now.' It's 'We were in Egypt.'"
- **The rut of how you've always thought.** Brent's picture of the difficulty of change — after years, it is hard to step back from Isaiah 53 and "not just see Jesus and see nothing else."
  - *Brent:* "It's really hard to get out of the rut of how you've always thought... it's so hard to step back from Isaiah 53 and not just see Jesus and see nothing else."

## Discussion Questions

1. God re-names the exiled, failed partner "my servant... whom I have chosen and have not rejected." Where in your own life or community is it hardest to believe that God refuses to quit on a partner who has clearly failed?
2. The episode insists the suffering servant of Isaiah 53 first means *Israel in exile*, and only then is fulfilled in Jesus. How does reading the chapter in the context of the whole 40–54 discourse change what it asks of you?
3. Marty calls Isaiah 53 "an invitation, not an exemption" — Jesus shows us *how* to suffer on behalf of others rather than suffering so we wouldn't have to. What would it look like to accept the servant vocation instead of only receiving its benefits?
4. Brent names "the rut of how you've always thought" and how hard it is to step back from Isaiah 53 and "not just see Jesus and see nothing else." What is one long-held reading you are being invited to hold more spaciously — and what makes that hard?
5. The Jewish communal reading says "*we* were in Egypt, *we* did it." How might entering the story as a participant rather than an abstract observer reshape the way your community reads Scripture together?

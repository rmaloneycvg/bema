# Jeremiah - Weeping

BEMA 56 · Session 2
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/2-56-jeremiah-weeping.md`
Book: Jeremiah 2:1-3, 2:13, 2:20-37; 3:14-18; 7:1-11; 7:30–8:3; 19:10-15; 25:1-14 (with 2 Chronicles 36:20-21). The transcript prints the NET; Brent reads aloud from the NIV.
Host: Marty Solomon · Co-host: Brent Billings
Core move throughout: Jeremiah opens the **Babylonian prophets** with the image of **weeping**. The time for repentance has passed — there will be "no Hezekiah moment" — so Jeremiah must carry a message no one wants to hear: God's discipline (Babylon) is here, it cannot be turned back, and the wisest, least bloody response is to lay down weapons and go peacefully into captivity. More than that, Jeremiah reads the whole crisis through the lens of **broken covenant relationship** — God the husband, Israel the once-devoted bride — and finally retires the series' running "Source A (idolatry) vs. Source B (injustice)" game by refusing to let the two be separated: *idolatry feeds injustice and injustice feeds idolatry.*

> **Session 2 overarching arc: the prophets — the partner's (Israel's) unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Session 1 built the *partnership*: God rescuing a people out of empire, marrying them at Sinai, taking them on a desert honeymoon in Numbers, and charging them to look out for "the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow." Session 2 lives in the long collapse of that partnership. This episode sits at the hinge where warning turns to verdict: Jeremiah opens the *Babylonian* prophets (the third of five periods — Pre-Assyrian, Assyrian, **Babylonian**, Exilic, Remnant), after the earlier prophets (Amos, Hosea, Micah, 1 Isaiah; then Jonah, Nahum, Zephaniah, 2 Isaiah) have already cried out. This guide advances the one story by showing *what God's refusal to quit looks like once the time for repentance is gone*: God still names the covenant tenderly ("I remember the devotion of your youth, how as a bride you loved me"), still calls "Return, faithless people... for I am your husband," and still slips in "a little bit of hope" even inside the sentence of seventy years' exile. Where Session 1 asked whether the partner would say *yes*, Jeremiah shows the partner's *no* reaching its consequence — and God grieving (weeping with his prophet) rather than walking away, disciplining the marriage toward restoration rather than ending it.

## Key Lessons

- **The image is weeping because the call is brutal — Jeremiah feels "seduced" into it.** No single image can hold so vast a book, so the review word is *weeping*: the "weeping prophet" given a message that wins him no friends. Marty: *"he's crying out to God and he says, 'You seduced me, God, and I was seduced.' That's the word he uses in the Hebrew. 'You wooed me. You fooled me. I thought I was signing up for this glorious calling of being a prophet...' Instead, I found out it was this unbelievable, hard call."*
- **The time for repentance has passed — no second Hezekiah moment.** Earlier prophets offered a chance to turn; Jeremiah's word is that the window is closed and Babylon is inevitable. Marty: *"The time for repentance has passed. That was Pre-Assyrian, that was Assyrian days, but those days are over. Babylon is here... We're going to have no Hezekiah moment here in Jeremiah."*
- **The least-bloody faithfulness is surrender — Jeremiah is so "pro-Babylon" they leave him behind.** Accepting the discipline, not resisting it, is the humble path. Marty: *"The best move for all of you would be to lay down your weapons and head into captivity. There will be much less bloodshed that way... that message is so pro-Babylon that when Babylon gets there, they actually leave Jeremiah behind."*
- **Jeremiah reads everything through broken covenant *relationship*, not abstract theology.** The sin is a marriage betrayed, not a doctrine misfiled. Marty: *"Jeremiah sees everything through the lens of covenant relationship. The key problem for Jeremiah is that you have broken relationship with God. It's not about abstract theological concepts or just pure idolatry as an abstract idea or even injustice as a concept. It's about a relationship. You are God's bride and you've broken relationship."*
- **The "two sins" image: forsaking the fountain to dig cracked cisterns.** The betrayal is both desertion and a futile self-reliance that holds nothing. Marty (reading 2:13): *"My people have committed two sins. They have forsaken me, the Spring of Living Water, and have dug their own cisterns, broken cisterns, that cannot hold water."*
- **Source A and Source B are not a choice in Jeremiah — idolatry *drives* injustice.** Marty retires his tongue-in-cheek game: Jeremiah fuses the two deliberately. Marty: *"you don't pick, it's not source A or source B. It's really the two together... in Jeremiah 7, he's really drumming on false religion and the fact that their religious devotion isn't producing a care for others. It's not producing mishpat... but then he connects it right there — quite deliberately in verse 9 — to their idolatry, because their idolatry is what's driving their injustice."*
- **Our idols still work the same way — they come at other people's expense.** The gods of consumerism, security, and comfort cannot invite care for others. Marty: *"If you bow down to the god of your own self-security, it's going to come at the expense of the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow... Our idolatry is directly connected to our injustice, and injustice feeds our idolatry. It becomes this catch 22, which is why God tried to teach us in Deuteronomy to reverse that."*
- **"Hell" in Jeremiah is a real valley, not (yet) an eternal destination.** The Valley of Ben Hinnom / Tophet — later Jerusalem's trash dump — is the concrete root of Jesus' *gehenna*. Marty: *"Hell was an actual place. The word 'hell' — gehenna — actually comes from the Hebrew phrase Gei Hinnom... Hell is the utter desolation of choosing to not be a part of what God's doing. It's going to be a very present image, a very present tense image."*
- **Chronicles, writing later "with perspective," reframes the exile as the land's Sabbath rest.** The deeper diagnosis is injustice — unobserved *shmitah* and Jubilee. Marty: *"the key issue was Sabbath rest. Injustice. We had disrupted shalom... They weren't observing shmitah. They weren't observing the Year of Jubilee... some of the things that really pushed for economic justice, looking out for those on the bottom."*
- **Even the sentence of exile carries "a little bit of hope."** The prophets' pattern holds: seventy years, then judgment on Babylon too. Brent: *"Hope."* Marty: *"A little bit of hope."*

## East/West Lens

*(Only categories the episode actually engages, each with a citation.)*

- **Covenant as marriage — relational/concrete, not abstract-propositional (Eastern relational frame vs. Western theological abstraction).** Jeremiah frames sin as a betrayed marriage, God as husband, Israel as bride. Marty: *"It's not about abstract theological concepts... It's about a relationship. You are God's bride and you've broken relationship."*
- **Street/guerrilla theater as prophecy — embodied, enacted word (Eastern concreteness vs. Western words-on-a-page).** The prophetic word is *performed*, not merely spoken. Marty: *"God tells Jeremiah, 'I need you to do some gorilla theater... I want you to take a big jar... you're going to break the jar... as a symbol, as an image of what I'm going to do.'"*
- **"Hell" (gehenna) as a concrete place, not a disembodied abstraction (Eastern this-worldly concreteness vs. Western otherworldly abstraction).** The word points first to dirt and geography outside Jerusalem. Marty: *"Hell was an actual place... Gei Hinnom would be 'valley of Hinnom.' It was an actual valley outside of Jerusalem."*
- **Idolatry and ethics are one fabric (Eastern wholeness vs. Western compartmentalizing of worship and justice).** False religion and injustice are welded together. Marty: *"their religious devotion isn't producing a care for others. It's not producing mishpat... their idolatry is what's driving their injustice."*
- **Reading Scripture as one echoing story (Eastern intertext vs. Western proof-text).** Jeremiah reuses the Exodus/Numbers honeymoon imagery, and 2 Chronicles later re-reads Jeremiah's own prophecy. Marty: *"Jeremiah employs this image that we talked about in session one with Torah and the book of Exodus and this marriage, and then a desert honeymoon in the book of Numbers."*

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Cold open & the five periods.** Brent frames "our journey through the Babylonian prophets... the famous weeping prophet, Jeremiah." Marty reviews the prophetic-history diagram: five sections — Pre-Assyrian, Assyrian, Babylonian, Exilic, Remnant — noting labels vary ("some people call it Pre-Babylonian... we call it Babylonian").
2. **Review of the earlier prophets and their images.** Pre-Assyrian (four): Amos and Hosea to Israel; Micah and 1 Isaiah to Judah. Assyrian (four): Jonah and Nahum to Israel; Zephaniah and 2 Isaiah to Judah. Images rehearsed: Amos = plumb line / ripe fruit; Hosea = prostitute; Micah = judge; 1 Isaiah = vineyard; Jonah = potential; Nahum = *diyn*; Zephaniah = *t'shuvah* (return/repent); 2 Isaiah = woe.
3. **Jeremiah the nature prophet (P.S.).** A tidbit: if a prophecy involves a tree or an animal, "there's a good chance that Jeremiah would be a really good guess" (with Habakkuk's "feet of a deer" as a counter-example).
4. **The image: weeping, and the "seduced" call.** Impossible to pick one image for so huge a book; the review word is *weeping*. Jeremiah protests, "You seduced me, God, and I was seduced" — a glorious-sounding calling that turned out brutal.
5. **Jeremiah's message boiled down.** The time for repentance has passed; no Hezekiah moment; Babylon is inevitable; lay down weapons and go into captivity for less bloodshed. He prophesies before, during, and after Babylon's arrival — so "pro-Babylon" they leave him behind.
6. **Baruch and the burned-and-rewritten scroll.** The book is a *re-collection* of many small pieces ("the word of the LORD came to me"), dictated to the scribe Baruch after the first scroll was burned.
7. **Book recommendation.** Alan Rabinowitz, *The Disciple Scroll* — ~300-page historical fiction, ebook ~$4 — recommended for grasping the period.
8. **Jeremiah 2 — the bride and the two sins.** "I remember the devotion of your youth, how as a bride you loved me and followed me through the desert" (the Session-1 Exodus/Numbers honeymoon). The covenant-relationship lens. 2:13: forsaking "the Spring of Living Water" to dig "broken cisterns that cannot hold water."
9. **Jeremiah 2:20-37 — idolatry front and center.** "On every high hill and under every spreading tree you lay down as a prostitute"; "to wood, 'You are my father,' and to stone, 'You gave me birth.'" Marty throws "Source A some love," then notes an out-of-nowhere Source-B line: "On your clothes, men find the lifeblood of the innocent poor"; and the futile political allegiances ("disappointed by Egypt as you were by Assyria").
10. **Jeremiah 3:14-18 — "Return... for I am your husband."** The gather-to-Zion hope, shepherds after God's own heart, Judah and Israel reunited — the marriage image carried forward.
11. **Jeremiah 7:1-11 — the temple-gate sermon.** Brent reads it; Marty imagines Jeremiah (and trembling Baruch) at the gate before corrupt priests. "The temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD" false security; the demand to deal justly, not oppress the foreigner/fatherless/widow; "a den of robbers" flagged as extra-credit Jesus foreshadowing.
12. **Source A + Source B fused; the game retired.** Jeremiah combines both; v. 9 welds idolatry to injustice. The gods — Baal (fertility, commerce, profit) and Asherah — care nothing for others. Modern parallels: consumerism, security, comfort ("you buy a bag of chips and nobody else gets chips"); the Deuteronomy "reverse catch-22" of caring for the alien, orphan, widow (James's "pure and undefiled religion").
13. **Jeremiah 7:30–8:3 — Tophet and the Valley of Slaughter.** High places of Tophet in the Valley of Ben Hinnom, burning sons and daughters "something I did not command, nor did it enter my mind"; the valley renamed, bodies as refuse, bones exposed. Setup for *gehenna* / hell (Rob Bell's *Love Wins*, Francis Chan's *Erasing Hell*; the valley "full of houses" today explains the thin archaeology). Fuller treatment deferred to Session 3.
14. **Jeremiah 19:10-15 — the broken jar.** Guerrilla/street theater: shatter a potter's jar before the people — "I will smash this nation and this city just as this potter's jar is smashed and cannot be repaired." The same Tophet reference; the people "stiff-necked and would not listen."
15. **Jeremiah 25:1-14 — seventy years, then judgment on Babylon, then hope.** Judah's captivity announced in Jehoiakim's fourth year (= Nebuchadnezzar's first); "again and again... you have not listened"; seventy years of Babylonian service; then Babylon itself punished. Brent names the ever-present "Hope."
16. **2 Chronicles 36:20-21 — the land's Sabbath rest.** The later chronicler cites Jeremiah's prophecy from near the very end of the Tanakh: the land "enjoyed its Sabbath rest... until the seventy years were completed." Chronicles' "perspective" reframes the exile as *injustice* — unobserved *shmitah* and Jubilee — fusing idolatry and injustice once more.
17. **Close.** No one ever enjoys a Jeremiah message; confronting God's people with "impending, unchangeable, inevitable, imminent doom" was his hard job. "There we have it."

## Terminology

- **weeping (the prophet's image)** — the review word for Jeremiah, "the weeping prophet," since no single image can hold so vast a book; it names the anguish of a brutal, friendless calling. Marty: *"Jeremiah's image is an image of weeping... he was given a daunting task."*
- **"seduced" / wooed (the call lament)** — the Hebrew word behind Jeremiah's complaint that God lured him into prophecy. Marty: *"he says, 'You seduced me, God, and I was seduced.' That's the word he uses in the Hebrew. 'You wooed me. You fooled me.'"*
- **Source A / Source B** — the series' running lens: Source A (Samuel-Kings) reads the failure as *idolatry/immorality*; Source B (Chronicles) reads it as *empire/injustice*. In Jeremiah the game is retired because the two fuse. Marty: *"it's not source A or source B. It's really the two together."*
- **mishpat** (מִשְׁפָּט, "justice") — the just, restored ordering the people's "religious devotion isn't producing." Marty: *"It's not producing mishpat. It's not producing justice."*
- **shalom** (שָׁלוֹם) — the right-ordering of the world their injustice disrupted. Marty: *"We had disrupted shalom."*
- **Baal** — the Canaanite god of agricultural fertility, commerce, and economy; by nature unconcerned with the vulnerable. Marty: *"Baal's concerned about profit and well-being. Baal is the god of agricultural fertility, commerce, and economy. It's definitely not concerned with other people."*
- **Asherah** — the goddess (and the "Asherah poles") the people "spread themselves before"; likewise uninterested in others. Marty: *"they spread themselves before all the Asherah poles. Is Asherah going to be concerned about other people? Probably not."*
- **Molech** — the god to whom children were burned in the Valley of Hinnom. Marty: *"It was this valley that you used to burn your children to Moloch."*
- **Tophet / Valley of Ben Hinnom** — the valley where the "high places of Tophet" stood; renamed the "Valley of Slaughter" and later Jerusalem's trash dump. Marty: *"Tophet was the ancient word for the valley of... the valley of Ben Hinnom it's going to be called."*
- **gehenna / Gei Hinnom** — the Greek *gehenna* (Jesus' word for "hell") derives from the Hebrew *Gei Hinnom*, "valley of Hinnom," an actual place; the image is present-tense desolation, not an eternal fiery destination. Marty: *"The word 'hell' — gehenna — actually comes from the Hebrew phrase Gei Hinnom."*
- **shmitah** — the sabbatical ("Sabbath") year the land was denied; its neglect is the injustice Chronicles names. Marty: *"They weren't observing shmitah. They weren't observing the Year of Jubilee."*
- **Jubilee** — the economic-justice reset ("looking out for those on the bottom") the people failed to keep. Marty: *"some of the things that really pushed for economic justice, looking out for those on the bottom. They weren't doing those things."*
- **the prophet's "image"** — the series convention that each prophet gets an encapsulating metaphor or word. Reviewed here: Amos = plumb line / ripe fruit, Hosea = prostitute, Micah = judge, 1 Isaiah = vineyard, Jonah = potential, Nahum = *diyn*, Zephaniah = *t'shuvah*, 2 Isaiah = woe, Jeremiah = weeping. Marty: *"every prophet has an image."*
- **diyn / t'shuvah** (reviewed words) — the images for two earlier prophets: Nahum's *diyn* (retributive justice) and Zephaniah's *t'shuvah* ("return/repent"). Marty: *"T'shuvah, which means... Return? Repent or return; it's that word we usually translate repent."*

## Illustrations & Stories

- **"You seduced me, God, and I was seduced" — the weeping prophet's anguish.** Jeremiah thought he was signing up for a "glorious calling" and found "this unbelievable, hard call." The cry anchors why *weeping* is his image.
- **Babylon leaves Jeremiah behind.** His "lay down your arms and go to captivity" message is so pro-surrender that when Babylon arrives, the conquerors leave him with the remnant — "Hey, this guy is on our side." A vivid proof of how costly and contrarian his message was.
- **Baruch the scribe and the burned, re-dictated scroll.** Marty pictures Baruch as "a little pet journalist, a note-taker," trailing Jeremiah and cataloging every "the word of the LORD came to me" — and the book as a *re-collection* after the first scroll was burned.
- **Jeremiah (and trembling Baruch) at the temple gate.** "Imagine Jeremiah having to go, and Baruch having to stand next to him, shaking as he writes this down, as he stands in front of the gate, in front of corrupt priests and false prophets." Dramatizes the cost of the 7:1-11 sermon.
- **The bag of chips at Walmart (consumerism's self-interest).** Brent: "You go to Walmart, you buy a bag of chips and nobody else is getting chips." Marty: "I'm usually buying chips quite cheaply at the expense of other people." The modern face of idolatry-driving-injustice.
- **Toms / Warby Parker (buy-one-give-one).** Brent's example of "a little bit of conscience" trying to "infiltrate this consumeristic world" — the exception that proves consumerism generally doesn't care for others.
- **The broken potter's jar — guerrilla/street theater (ch. 19).** God sends Jeremiah to shatter a jar before the people as an enacted sign that the nation will be "smashed... and cannot be repaired."
- **Driving through the house-filled Valley of Hinnom.** "We drove through the valley of Hinnom last summer... It was full of houses" — why there's no "sexy archaeology" of the ancient trash dump, answering the no-evidence objection.

## Discussion Questions

1. Jeremiah's image is *weeping*, and he accuses God, "You seduced me... and I was seduced." Where in your own life has a genuine calling turned out to be a far harder, lonelier thing than you signed up for — and what does it mean that God weeps alongside the one who carries it?
2. Marty says Jeremiah brings a word with "no Hezekiah moment" — the time for repentance has passed and the discipline is now inevitable. How do you tell the difference between a season to *resist and reverse* and a season to *accept and humble yourself*? What makes the latter so hard to hear?
3. Jeremiah reads the whole crisis through *broken covenant relationship* — "You are God's bride and you've broken relationship" — rather than through abstract theology. How would your sense of sin change if you framed it primarily as a betrayed relationship instead of a broken rule?
4. Marty retires the Source A / Source B game because "idolatry is what's driving their injustice." Name one modern idol (consumerism, security, comfort) and trace honestly how serving it comes "at the expense of the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow." What would reversing that catch-22 cost you?
5. The "two sins" are forsaking "the Spring of Living Water" *and* digging "broken cisterns that cannot hold water." What cracked cisterns have you dug — self-reliant substitutes that promise security but hold nothing?
6. *Gehenna* in Jeremiah is a real valley of desolation, and Marty frames hell as "the utter desolation of choosing to not be a part of what God's doing" — "a very present-tense image." How does reading hell as present-tense desolation (rather than only a future destination) change how you hear Jeremiah's warning?
7. In Session 1 the question was whether the partner would say *yes* to the covenant. In Jeremiah the partner's *no* has reached its consequence — yet God still says "Return, faithless people... for I am your husband" and tucks "a little bit of hope" inside the exile. Where do you most need to trust that God's refusal to quit survives even his discipline?

# With All Your Soul & "Very"

BEMA 21 · Session 1
Source transcript: `transcripts/1-21-with-all-your-soul-very.md`

## Overview

Marty Solomon, Brent Billings, and returning guest Reed Dent continue the three desert tests on the road to Sinai (Exodus 17), covering the **second and third tests** — the **test of the soul (*nephesh*)** at Massah and Meribah, and the **test of the "very" / might (*meod*)** against the Amalekites at Rephidim. The episode reads the water-from-the-rock scene as a picture of a self-giving God who stands *before* the rock and takes the blow, and reads the battle against Amalek as the formation of a people who use their strength to protect the weak and marginalized. Both tests are gathered up into the **Jesus Shema** — love God with all your heart, soul, and might, and love your neighbor as yourself — and sit squarely inside the **Session 1 arc of partnership**: God forming a *bechor* people who can become what he intends to make them at Sinai.

## Key Lessons

- **The test of the *nephesh* (soul) asks: does God *have* you, or only when he meets your demands?** Marty, relaying Ray Vander Laan: *"what they were doing was sitting in the desert... 'But he's shown you.' 'I know. Make him do it again.'... 'We're not going to go one more step unless God does what we say.' That is testing."* The soul-test exposes a conditional allegiance — *"Yeah, I'll follow God if He... as long as He...blank"* — and asks whether God has all of you.

- **Testing God is not the same as asking God for reassurance.** Marty distinguishes the Israelites' demand from Gideon's fleece: *"Gideon's not saying unless you do X, I won't move. Gideon was saying, I'm having a hard time believing. Help me believe... He wasn't making a demand of God. He was asking for some kind of reassurance."* The sin is the ultimatum, not the honest struggle.

- **God answers Moses' legitimate cry by first commanding him into vulnerability — "go out in front of the people."** When Moses raises a genuine *tsa'aqah* ("they are almost ready to stone me"), God's very first word is to walk out ahead of the crowd that wants to kill him. Marty: *"God's like, yeah, step number one, I want you to go out in front of the people, which in a sense is his own test of the nephesh. 'Moses, do I have you? Okay, go. I got you. Go.'"*

- **The water-from-the-rock scene pictures a God who takes the blow on our behalf.** With God standing *paniym* (face-to-face) before the rock and Moses commanded to *nakah* (strike-to-kill) it, the elders watch "Moses trying to kill the Holy One of Israel" (Reed). Marty: *"Moses is at God's command, striking the mountain of God, in picture, striking God. This whole thing just screams Jesus... It's the God that will put his bow in the clouds... that will walk the blood path on our behalf... a God that will take the blow on your behalf."*

- **The test of the *meod* (might) asks whether you will spend your strength to protect the weak.** The Amalekites preyed on the weak at the back of the camp; the battle teaches Israel to use their "very" for the vulnerable. Marty: *"this is the message of the story of how God wants us to learn how to use our might, our meod, our very... use it for the larger community, use it for those that have need of it... Use your strength to fight for others."*

- **Shalom communities put the weak and marginalized in the *middle*; Empire leaves them on the fringes or the bottom.** Marty, from his rabbi: *"you will always know whether or not you're looking at a community of Shalom or a community of Empire based on where you find the weak and the marginalized... Are they on the fringes? Then I know... Empire. Are they on the bottom?... That's Empire. Or are they in the middle? Are the wagons circled around them?... then I know I have the community of God's Shalom."*

- **"Choose men who will fight *for us*" — strength is validated by self-forgetful care for others.** The rabbis move the Hebrew phrase "for us," and connect it to Gideon's 300 who drank from their hands to make way for others. Marty: *"'Those are men who will fight for us.' They're not just thinking about themselves... Let me get my water and get out of the way."* Moses' lifted arms, held up by Aaron and Hur, make the same point: strength is sustained communally, for others.

- **All three tests are finally one test — what the community does with those who cannot do for themselves.** Reed: *"all three of these tests... are in some way kind of the same test, looking for the same thing... which is what the community is doing with those who can't do for themselves... Are you the kind of people that can be this thing that I'm going to make you?"* The Shema's two halves are inseparable — Marty: *"It's loving God and it's loving others."*

## East/West Lens

- **Soul (*nephesh*) as the whole, integrated self over a detachable "spiritual" part.** The Western instinct splits soul from body (and often equates soul with spirit); the Hebraic *nephesh* is all of you at once. Marty: *"your soul is all of it. Your soul is you... soul and spirit are not the same thing. Your soul is all of you, body and spirit, and your seat of emotions... the sum total of that is your soul."* A test of the soul is therefore a claim on the entire person, not an inner compartment.

- **Faith measured relationally (does God *have* you?) over faith as intellectual assent to God's existence.** Reed on Psalm 78: *"'they did not believe in God'... means something very different for them than it means for us. We talk about not believing in God as believing in the existence of God... what really makes God angry here... is that they doubt... God's generosity."* Belief is covenant trust, not propositional agreement.

- **God revealed as self-giving and blow-absorbing over a primarily wrathful, demand-enforcing deity.** Faced with a people putting him to the test, the expected Western/"Empire" response would be judgment and fire; the Text shows restraint and substitution. Marty: *"He should probably respond with a whole bunch of judgment and... burning everybody up. But it's a God that instead says... 'I'm a God that will take the blow on your behalf.'"*

- **Strength (*meod*) as resource for the community over might as personal power or self-advancement.** *Meod* means "with all your resources, whatever you have to offer," and the lesson bends it outward. Marty: *"Are they going to use their resources... everything they have to protect the weak?"* The measure of might is who it serves.

- **Where you locate the weak reveals the kingdom you belong to — a corporate/communal diagnostic over an individual ethic.** The fringe-vs-bottom-vs-middle test is about the *shape of the whole community*, not private virtue. The tribe of Dan guarding the rear so "the people that are sick, that are weak" are "in the middle of the pack" embodies this.

- **Truth carried by image and embodied practice over abstract doctrine.** The banner (*Adonai Nissi*) teaches by pointing beyond itself; the Jesus Shema is learned by *standing and reciting it together* at the start of every class. Marty: *"every class from here on out... we'd start by standing together and reciting Shema together, and it just became a part of our practice."*

## Recurring Through-lines

- **Trust the story (the arc itself).** The episode opens with an explicit "flyby review" of the whole arc and keeps reading each test as part of one unfolding narrative toward Sinai. Marty: *"We got the book of Genesis... the goodness of creation, invitation to trust... this tale of two Kingdoms... the Exodus... now are in the middle of the story where they're on their way to Mount Sinai, this 40-day journey and a time of testing."* The story runs on, and the tests only make sense inside it.

- **Partnership / Israel as *bechor* (Session 1 overarching arc).** The three tests are God's formation of a people fit for what he will make them at Sinai. Reed names it directly: *"if you think about what... they're going to be made into at Sinai... God is testing to see what is in their heart. 'Are you the kind of people that can be this thing that I'm going to make you?'"* The soul- and might-tests are partnership being forged — reciprocal life, each test "an opportunity to show God where they were at" and "to learn something about who God was."

- **The two kingdoms: Empire vs. Shalom.** Carried forward and sharpened into a concrete diagnostic: where the weak and marginalized sit. Marty: *"Are they on the fringes?... Are they on the bottom?... That's Empire. Or are they in the middle?... then I know I have the community of God's Shalom."* The Amalekites (who attack the weak at the back) are Empire; the tribe of Dan guarding the rear is Shalom.

- **A God who hears the cry (continuing the Episode 17 thread).** Moses' *tsa'aqah* is explicitly named as the oppressed cry of those whose lives are threatened, and God answers it. Marty: *"The tsa'aqah is... the cry of the oppressed... their lives are being threatened. This is a tsa'aqah... Moses cries out before God. And so watch what God says."* The God who heard Israel in Egypt keeps hearing in the desert.

- **His bow in the clouds / walking the blood path — the consistent, self-giving character of God (callback to Episodes 4 and 10).** The rock scene is explicitly tied to those earlier images of a God who absorbs the cost. Marty: *"It's the God that will put his bow in the clouds. It's the God that will walk the blood path on our behalf. This is who God has always been through Genesis and Exodus. This is not some angry God."*

- *(Note: the "master the beast" / sin-as-failing-to-master-the-animal motif from `1-3-master-the-beast.md` is not engaged this episode — the struggle is framed through the soul/might tests and communal generosity, not the beast/instinct lens, so the motif is not claimed. The El Shaddai "God who knows when to say enough" motif from Episode 2 is likewise not invoked here.)*

## Chiasm / Structure

The hosts do not identify a formal chiasm in this episode; none is asserted here. The episode's own organizing structure is the **three-tests-as-the-three-faculties-of-the-Shema** — heart (*vav*/*levav*), soul (*nephesh*), and might (*meod*) — of which this episode covers the **second (soul)** and **third (might)** tests, with the first (heart) having been covered in Episode 20. *(Marty: "love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul and with all your very... the three tests map onto the three faculties of the Shema"; Reed: "all three of these tests... are in some way kind of the same test.")*

## Hebrew / Terms

- **nephesh** (נֶפֶשׁ) — the soul; in the Hebraic mind the whole essence of a person — body and spirit together, emotions, consciousness, "the sum total of that is your soul." Marty: *"your soul is all of you, body and spirit, and your seat of emotions... everything you can think of that makes 'you' you."* The second test is a test of the *nephesh*: *"does God have you or does God only have you when He meets your demands?"*
- **meod** (מְאֹד) — "very"; translated "might" or "strength." Marty: *"It simply means with all your resources, with whatever you have to offer."* The third test is the test of the *meod* — will you use your strength to protect the weak? (cf. *tov meod*, "very good," Genesis 1 — Brent: "Very good. Tov meod.")
- **vav / levav** (לֵב / לֵבָב) — the heart, the seat of the will (the first test, from Episode 20). Marty lists it first in the Shema triad: *"Their vav, their nephesh, their meod."*
- **tsa'aqah** (צְעָקָה) — the cry of the oppressed, of those whose lives are threatened. Marty: *"the cry of those that are... their lives are being threatened. This is a tsa'aqah. I do not think Moses is being dramatic here."*
- **paniym** (פָּנִים) — "faces," the word behind "before." Marty: *"The word for 'before' is the word paniym, and paniym is the Hebrew word for faces... He is between Moses and the mountain."*
- **nakah** (נָכָה) — to strike, to smite in order to kill. Marty: *"That word nakah means to smite, to strike in order to kill."* The same word used when Moses struck down the Egyptian and when God struck the gods of Egypt; now Moses is told to *nakah* the rock — in picture, striking God, who stands *paniym* before it.
- **Adonai Nissi** (יְהוָה נִסִּי) — "The LORD is my Banner," the name Moses gives the altar. Marty: *"the banner is not the point. It's what lies right behind the banner... The banner always points the way to where the god is."* The raised staff points past Moses to God.
- **Shema / Ve'ahavta** (שְׁמַע / וְאָהַבְתָּ) — "Hear, O Israel, the LORD is our God, the LORD alone. Love the LORD your God with all your heart (*levav*), with all your soul (*nephesh*), and with all your might (*meod*)." Marty: *"Shema Yisrael Adonai Eloheinu Adonai Echad... the three tests map onto the three faculties of the Shema."*
- **Jesus Shema** — Marty's shortened Shema joining Deuteronomy 6 (love God with heart/soul/might) and Leviticus 19 (love your neighbor as yourself). Marty: *"Why do we call it the Jesus Shema? Because that was the two greatest commands that Jesus gave in the Gospels."*

## Discussion Questions

1. The test of the *nephesh* asks whether God *has* you, or only when he meets your demands. Where do you catch yourself saying, in Marty's words, "Yeah, I'll follow God *as long as He ___*"? What would it look like for God to "have" that part of you unconditionally? *(Ex 17:1-7.)*
2. Marty distinguishes *testing God* (an ultimatum — "unless you do X, I won't move") from *asking for reassurance* (Gideon — "help me in my unbelief"). Think of a recent prayer: were you making a demand or honestly asking for help to trust? How can you tell the difference in yourself? *(Ex 17:2; Judges 6:36-40.)*
3. At the rock, the elders watch "Moses trying to kill the Holy One of Israel," and the scene "screams Jesus" — a God who steps between us and the blow. How does seeing God as the one who *takes* the strike (rather than delivering it) reshape your picture of God's character? *(Ex 17:6; compare the bow in the clouds and the blood path.)*
4. Marty's rabbi says you can diagnose Empire vs. Shalom by *where the weak and marginalized are* — on the fringes, on the bottom, or protected in the middle. Where are the weak located in the communities you belong to (church, workplace, family), and what would "circling the wagons" around them require of your *meod*? *(Ex 17:8-13; Deut 25:17-19.)*
5. The rabbis read "choose men who will fight *for us*" alongside Gideon's soldiers who drank from their hands to make way for others. Where is your strength currently spent mostly on yourself, and where could it be spent "for us" — for those who cannot do for themselves? *(Ex 17:9.)*
6. Reed argues all three tests are finally one test: "what the community is doing with those who can't do for themselves." If God were measuring your community by that single question right now, what would the "test strip" read — and what one change would move it? *(Ex 17; the Jesus Shema.)*
7. Moses names the altar *Adonai Nissi* — "The LORD is my Banner" — because the banner points past itself to God. What "banners" in your life are meant to point beyond themselves, and are people looking at the banner or at the God behind it? *(Ex 17:15-16.)*

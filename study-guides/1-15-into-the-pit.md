# Into the Pit

BEMA 15 · Session 1
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/1-15-into-the-pit.md`
Book: Genesis (Genesis 37; 38; 39; 40 — the opening of the Joseph narrative, with the Judah/Tamar story embedded)
Co-host: Brent Billings · Host: Marty Solomon · Guest: Elle Grover Fricks (BEMA teaching team; Hebrew / ancient Near East)

> **Session 1 overarching arc: partnership.** This is **part one of a two-part conversation** opening the life of Joseph (Genesis 37–40). Jacob's unresolved dysfunction follows the story straight into his son: Joseph brings "evil, creeping, whispered rumors," wears a *second* coat (a bechor marker), and keeps saying "notice me." The brothers' murderous jealousy, the sale into Egypt, and the strange interruption of the Judah/Tamar story are all stitched together by one Hebrew word — *nakar* ("scrutinize / recognize"). Tracing *nakar* backward (Jacob deceiving Isaac, Jacob deceiving Laban, the brothers deceiving Jacob, Judah and Tamar) exposes a family trapped in a **cycle of deception**. The episode's driving, deliberately-unresolved question is the partnership question in another key: *does history simply repeat itself, or can a family — with God — break the cycle and change the trajectory toward redemption?*

## Key Lessons

- **Joseph is read as a flawed, self-focused character following in Jacob's footsteps — not a flawless hero.** Marty resists the youth-group "superhero" Joseph and sees inherited dysfunction. Marty: *"I feel like what I'm seeing is a son that in a lot of ways is following in the footsteps of his Jacob father... he seems very self-focused."* Elle backs it from the Hebrew of the "bad report": *"in the Hebrew, it's more specific... he brings evil. Evil is a big word. Creeping, whispered rumors to his father... there's more darkness, more resentment, bitterness, getting passed down."*
- **The "ornate robe" is a *second coat* — a bechor (chosen/firstborn) marker that detonates the family.** The point isn't the fabric; every son got a coat from the *beit av*. Marty (crediting Fohrman): *"the point here isn't even necessarily the kind of garment. It's... the second one... by giving him a second coat... Dad's made a very clear statement, 'Yosef is my bechor. He gets the two coats, he gets the double portion.'"* Elle sharpens the drama: Joseph is "the bechor of Rachel," so Jacob "is ignoring Leah's supremacy as the first wife."
- **God's favor does not look like a gold-paved road — it looks like light and hope in the darkest places.** On "the LORD was with Joseph" in prison, Elle gives the episode's most quoted takeaway: *"Sometimes what it looks like is you go to prison because you're falsely accused of something, but when you're there, the prison warden is nice to you... It's not the yellow brick road being paved with gold... It's finding light and finding hope in the darkest of places."* Marty: *"God loves to hang on to imperfect characters."*
- **The embedded Judah/Tamar story is the moral hinge: a deceiver, caught, chooses confession over destroying his victim.** When Tamar sends back the pledge and says "recognize these," Judah could crush, defame, or blackmail her. Marty: *"what we see is Judah publicly confess, fess up, acknowledge what's happened, call her righteous. He goes through this very appropriate process of repentance."* It models the possibility that a family of deceivers *can* turn.
- **The real question the episode plants is whether the cycle of deception can be broken.** Marty closes by tracing *nakar* across the family's deceptions and names the stakes directly: *"does history simply repeat itself? Or are we able to break these cycles?... Could this actually end up being a story about hope? We will see in next week's episode."* The partnership arc depends on the answer: redemption and reconciliation would mean God is "up to something new and not the same old thing."

## East/West Lens

- **Community / belonging over individual provision (the *beit av* and *mishpachah*).** The hosts explicitly correct a Western "the man provides everything" reading. Marty: *"when we say provision, we're not talking about the way that it's the man who provides everything... it's a family of belonging... the community that enables our survival."* Elle: *"it's the whole community, it's your whole extended family, everyone is working together... still under this central beit av, the house of the father."*
- **Words / names as loaded word-pictures (vs. flat labels).** Meaning rides on Hebrew sound and root. The "bad report" is "evil, creeping, whispered rumors"; the dream's grain-word *aduma* plays on "to be dumb" ("extra shade he's throwing on his brother's face" — Elle); **Tamar** = palm tree → "upright / righteous."
- **Avoiding presentism and primitivism (truth read in its own world).** Marty names the twin errors by name: *"primitivism... where we just assume that it was way different back then, or we assume it's just like it is today... Presentism."* The lens insists on reading the family's world on its own terms — e.g., women "very active in the daily provision of what happens in the beit av."
- **Descriptive, not prescriptive — and justice-oriented institutions.** Levirate marriage (*yibbum*) is "fairly normative for the time," not a biblical weirdness, and its impulse "should be one oriented around justice" — belonging, a future, the memory of the dead brother (Marty/Elle on Deuteronomy 25).
- **Text as treasure excavated through literary device (the *nakar* link).** The two "whiplash" stories (Gen 37 and 38) are placed side by side on purpose; the repeated "scrutinize/recognize these" is the buried key. Marty: *"whenever we see two stories that maybe we wouldn't expect to be next to each other... we might want to start looking for parallels."*
- **God named by concrete presence-in-relationship (vs. abstract attribute).** "The LORD was with Joseph" is cashed out concretely — favor with the warden inside a false imprisonment, not abstract blessedness (Elle's "light and hope in the darkest places").
- **History as cyclical — the Eastern/ancient assumption the story interrogates.** Marty: *"these ancient Sumerian tales were built off of this assumption that history was cyclical... this is in the spiritual DNA of this family. They're deceivers."* The lens sets up whether the covenant family can transcend the cycle.

## Recurring Through-lines

- **Partnership (Session 1 arc) — God travels with an imperfect, chosen family and asks whether it can change.** The episode keeps the partnership question open: God "loves to hang on to imperfect characters" (Marty) and "loves every character of all of these stories and... longs to be with them," even while the family spirals. The arc's forward edge is whether the partners can break their inherited cycle.
- **The cycle of deception (the *nakar* thread).** The anchor motif of this episode: *nakar* traces deception from Jacob/Isaac (Gen 27) and Jacob/Laban (Gen 31) to the brothers deceiving Jacob with the bloody robe (Gen 37) and Judah/Tamar (Gen 38). Marty: *"This word seems to be pointing out this cycle of deception that keeps showing up in this family story."*
- **Inheriting the parents' story (Joseph "giving Jacob").** Joseph's self-focus and deception are read as Jacob's dysfunction passed down. Marty: *"It feels more Jacob than Avram. I'm getting Jacob."* (Callback to the Jacob episodes, 1-13/1-14; flag for cross-reference, do not re-explain there.)
- **bechor — chosen/firstborn as the seat of conflict.** The double-portion/double-responsibility motif from the Jacob material returns as the "second coat." Elle: Joseph "is the bechor, but he's the bechor of Rachel" — reopening the Rachel/Leah rivalry. (Term anchored earlier in the patriarch arc.)
- **Shechem as a loaded place — mission, sacrifice, and violence.** Mentioned three times "on purpose." Shechem is where God first gave the family its mission and where Abraham built an altar/sacrificed, *and* the site of the Dinah violence — all hovering over the brothers' errand. Elle/Marty tie the Joseph story's "sacrifice language... blood and the covering and the ripping" to that place.
- **Repentance as the crack in the cycle (Judah).** Judah's public confession ("she is more righteous than I") is the thread of hope that redemption might be possible — set against the brothers' and his own earlier deceptions.
- **Foreshadowing redemption / "a story about hope."** The explicit cliffhanger: whether the trajectory can "change radically" and God "may in fact be up to something new." Points ahead to part two.

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat in the episode, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Framing & review.** Marty recaps the Session 1 arc ("creation is good, and trust Me"), the patriarch chain Avram → Yitzhak (mission realized) → Jacob (the "slimy," developing, dysfunctional central character), and notes Jacob's unresolved theme "follows us right into the Joseph story." Elle is welcomed back; a slide or two is promised. *(Marty: "the Jacob story really doesn't resolve itself because the Jacob story follows us right into the Joseph story.")*
2. **Reading Gen 37:1–2.** Brent reads the two verses; the old problem resurfaces — why only the sons of Bilhah and Zilpah (not Leah)? Elle reads the *toldot* ("this is the history of the family of Yaakov") as the Text pointing the finger at Jacob's unresolved split-family dynamics. Brent settles that all four (Dan, Naphtali, Gad, Asher) are present; "put a pin in" the missing Reuben, Judah, Benjamin.
3. **The "bad report."** Marty flags Joseph lacking practical wisdom and his self-focus; Elle's Hebrew note that it is "evil, creeping, whispered rumors," not mere tattling.
4. **Reading Gen 37:3–4 — the robe.** Elle debunks "technicolor dreamcoat": it is a *ketonet passim*, a long-sleeved undergarment "to his palms," a leisure (not work) garment. Marty/Fohrman: the real point is it's a *second* coat — a bechor marker from the *beit av*. The provision/*beit av* correction (community, not solo male provision; women active in provision) is inserted here. Brent imagines the "backroom conversations" among the wives; Elle: Joseph is "the bechor of Rachel," bypassing Leah.
5. **Reading Gen 37:5–11 — the dreams.** The sheaves and the sun/moon/stars; brothers hate him more; even Jacob rebukes him. Elle notes the wheat-sheaf fertility symbolism and "something euphemistic / pointed." Brent plays defense for Joseph ("Dad, you were right"). The wheat-bows-at-harvest image; Elle corrects that the Hebrew doesn't say wheat but uses *aduma* (play on "to be dumb") — "extra shade."
6. **Reading Gen 37:12–20 — sent to Shechem; the plot to kill.** Real "conspiracy for murder" hatred. The thrice-mentioned Shechem: a student's old observation; Elle on Shechem as a violent major trade center with warlords/mercenaries, *and* the leaving of "the valley of Hebron" (friendship) toward a place of prior violence (Dinah). Marty adds Shechem = where the mission was first given and where Abraham sacrificed; the Joseph story's "sacrifice language." Brent wonders if the coat is how they spot him "from a distance."
7. **Reading Gen 37:21–36 — Reuben, Judah, the sale, the bloody robe.** Reuben tries to act the firstborn and save Joseph; while he's gone, Judah devises plan B (sell him); Reuben returns to find him gone. Marty speculates this seeds a Reuben/Judah rift that explains Judah "not with the family" in the next chapter. The brothers dip the robe in goat's blood and send it to Jacob; Jacob mourns inconsolably; Midianites sell Joseph to Potiphar.
8. **Reading Gen 38:1–11 — Judah, Tamar, and *yibbum*.** Judah leaves, marries Shua's daughter, has Er, Onan, Shelah. **Tamar** introduced; her name = palm tree ("upright/righteous"); Elle on why women are named for trees (cultural fruitfulness ideal; cf. Hadassah/Esther = myrtle). Levirate marriage / *yibbum* explained (Deuteronomy 25; cross-cultural; protective but chiefly about the dead brother's legacy; the shoe-throwing; the rabbinic guard against a too-eager brother). Er and Onan die; Judah withholds Shelah and sends Tamar back to her father's house (an injustice).
9. **Reading Gen 38:12–26 — Tamar takes action; the pledge; "recognize these."** Tamar, slighted, disguises herself; Judah's pledge of seal, cord, and staff; the "shrine prostitute" that cannot be found; "bring her out and have her burned"; Tamar sends the pledge with "recognize whose these are." The *nakar* link is introduced via the slide: the brothers' "examine/recognize" the bloody robe (37:32) mirrors Tamar's "recognize these" (38:25). *nakar* defined (Elle: outsider / to scrutinize); both Joseph and Tamar are being treated as outsiders/interlopers. Marty frames Judah's crossroads — crush, defame, blackmail (Elle), or confess — and Judah confesses: "she is more righteous than I."
10. **Reading Gen 39:1–20 — Potiphar's house and the accusation.** Marty subverts the "valor of Joseph" youth-group reading: Joseph's refusal speech is heavy on "look at this position I have," light on sinning against God. Elle adds practical-wisdom/gratitude and empathy for Potiphar's wife — Potiphar is a *saris* (eunuch). The grabbed cloak; the false accusation; prison.
11. **Reading Gen 39:21–23 — "the LORD was with Joseph" in prison.** Elle's key teaching: God's favor isn't a golden road but "light and hope in the darkest of places" — the warden is kind inside a false imprisonment. Marty: God "loves to hang on to imperfect characters."
12. **Reading Gen 40 — the cupbearer and the baker.** Joseph again says "remember me... mention me to Pharaoh" — Marty hears "Jacob, not Avram" ("I'm getting Jacob"). The two dreams and interpretations ("interpretations belong to God"); the cupbearer restored, the baker impaled; "the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph. He forgot him" — the cliffhanger.
13. **Closing reflection — the *nakar* cycle and the hope question.** Marty traces *nakar* back through Gen 27 (Jacob deceiving Isaac) and Gen 31 (Jacob deceiving Laban), then the brothers deceiving Jacob and Judah/Tamar — a family cycle of deception. The ancient Sumerian assumption that history is cyclical frames the question: does history repeat, or can the cycle break into redemption? "We will see in next week's episode."

## Terminology

- **toldot** (תולדות) — "generations / account / legacy"; the heading "this is the account of Jacob" frames the chapter's dysfunction as *Jacob's* unresolved legacy. Elle: *"these are the toldot, the generations, which is a way that you usually start off talking about somebody's legacy."*
- **"bad report" (evil, whispered rumors)** — the Hebrew behind Joseph's report of his brothers; more than tattling. Elle: *"it says that he brings evil... Creeping, whispered rumors to his father."*
- **ketonet passim** (כתנת פסים) — the "ornate robe": a long-sleeved undergarment reaching "to his palms," a leisure (not work) garment. Elle: *"it's not the coat with long sleeves that a priest would get... It's a garment for more leisure... going to his palms."* The teaching point is that it is a **second** coat.
- **bechor** (בכור) — "firstborn / chosen"; the double-portion/double-responsibility role. Elle: *"bechor means 'chosen' and we use it as firstborn because so often the firstborn was the chosen... he's the bechor of Rachel."*
- **beit av** (בית אב) — "house of the father"; the household/community responsible for provision and belonging. Marty: *"it's the beit av's job... everybody, every son would have had a coat."*
- **mishpachah** (משפחה) — the larger extended family/community of belonging ("the community that enables our survival"). Used alongside *beit av* in the provision correction.
- **aduma** — the word used in Joseph's first dream (hosts note it is *not* "wheat"), playing on the root "to be dumb." Elle: *"it does use the word aduma, which comes from the word 'to be dumb.' So there is still some extra shade."*
- **Shechem** (שכם) — a major, violent Canaanite trade center (warlords, mercenaries; site of the Dinah violence) *and* the place of the family's mission and Abraham's altar/sacrifice. Elle: *"Shechem is a major trade center... known for having this wild warlord of the king."*
- **Tamar** (תמר) — "palm tree" (the upright Middle-Eastern palm), evoking uprightness/righteousness. Marty: *"Tamar is the Hebrew reference to a palm tree... a way that you could talk about righteousness. She's straight."* Elle: *"Upright."*
- **yibbum** (יבום) — levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25): the brother-in-law's duty to raise up offspring for a dead brother and keep the widow within the *beit av*. Elle: *"in the Hebrew it's yibbum... the emphasis in the Text is really about preserving the legacy and the honor of the dead first husband."*
- **nakar** (נכר) — "to scrutinize / recognize" (noun: an outsider, one scrutinized because they don't belong). The key word linking Genesis 37 and 38 and tracing the family's cycle of deception. Elle: *"Nakar, in its noun form, means to be an outsider... Its verb form means to scrutinize."* Marty: *"'Nakar this and see if you nakar it.' The same exact repetition... shows up in both stories."*
- **saris** (סריס) — "eunuch / castrated official"; Potiphar is a *saris*, which recontextualizes his wife's situation. Elle: *"saris means 'castrated.' So, he's a eunuch."*

## Illustrations & Stories

- **The "bedazzled jacket" / swagger (Marty & Brent).** The running riff on how the brothers spot Joseph "from a distance" — Marty: *"The bedazzled jacket... It's a swagger"*; Elle keeps correcting, "it's fancy undergarment... It's the sparkles." A playful image of Joseph flaunting his status.
- **Wheat bowing at harvest (Marty).** On the dream of the bowing sheaves: *"at the point of harvest, wheat will bow over... the tares will stand straight up and wheat bows over at harvest"* (cf. the wheat-and-tares parable) — a self-undercutting image for Joseph if he is the "immature wheat."
- **The widow's shoe (Elle).** On the social penalty for a brother refusing *yibbum*: *"Deuteronomy says she gets to throw a shoe at him, which is pretty great"* — a vivid picture of levirate obligation enforced by shame.
- **"It's giving Jacob" (Marty & Elle).** The generational-echo gag — Marty: *"I'm getting Jacob, I think is what the kids would say"*; Elle: *"It's giving Jacob."* The joke carries the lesson that Joseph's "remember me" self-focus mirrors his father.
- **Not the "yellow brick road paved with gold" (Elle).** The signature image for God's favor in hardship: *"It's not the yellow brick road being paved with gold ahead of your ascension to heaven. It's finding light and finding hope in the darkest of places."*
- **The "ludicrous" slide of parallel verses.** Brent/Marty pull up a presentation to show the *nakar* "recognize these" matching between the bloody robe (37:32) and Tamar's pledge (38:25) — the visual proof that the two stories are stitched together.

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty insists Joseph is no flawless superhero but a self-focused son "giving Jacob." Where do you most easily cast yourself (or someone you admire) as the hero of a story the text is actually telling more honestly — and what changes if you let the character stay flawed?
2. The "second coat" is a public signal that detonates a family. Where have you seen favoritism — or being favored — quietly wreck relationships, and how does naming it as *inherited* dysfunction (not just one bad decision) change how you'd address it?
3. Elle reframes God's favor as "light and hope in the darkest of places," not a "yellow brick road paved with gold." Where have you been waiting for the gold-paved road, and what would it mean to look instead for the kindness of the "prison warden" right where you are?
4. Judah, caught, could have crushed, defamed, or blackmailed Tamar — and instead confessed, "she is more righteous than I." Think of a moment you were "caught." What would Judah's kind of public, costly honesty have cost you, and what might it have opened?
5. The whole episode hangs on one question: *does history simply repeat itself, or can the cycle be broken?* What inherited "cycle of deception" (or fear, or grasping) runs in your own family or community — and what would the first *nakar*-breaking act of honesty actually look like this week?
6. The hosts read Shechem as a loaded place — mission, sacrifice, *and* violence all at once. Is there a place or situation in your life that is simultaneously your calling and your danger zone? How do you "check on the welfare of your brothers" there without pretending the danger isn't real?

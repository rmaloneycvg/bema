# BEMA 115: 5 Loaves, 2 Fish, and 1 Big Lesson — Study Guide

**Session 3 (The Gospels) · Episode 115**
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings · **Guest:** Dr. Christopher Gambino
**Primary texts:** Matthew 13:53–58; Luke 4:14–30; Matthew 14:1–21; John 6:5–6

---

## Overview

This episode threads three stories — Jesus's reading in the Nazareth synagogue, the beheading of John the Baptist, and the feeding of the 5,000 — and lands its "one big lesson" on the last of them. With returning-family guest Dr. Christopher Gambino (who writes a parashah/haftarah blog), the hosts read Nazareth through the synagogue reading-cycle, hear Jesus pointedly choose an Isaiah passage that matches no known lectionary, and watch him provoke his hometown by naming Elijah's widow of Zarephath and Naaman the Syrian — a mission to outsiders. After a brief, humanizing pass through John's execution and Jesus's withdrawal to grieve, the episode decodes the feeding of the 5,000 through Eastern number-symbolism: 5 loaves (the books of Moses) + 2 fish (the tablets) = the complete Law, handed from Jesus to his disciples to his people, with 12 baskets left over. Jesus is read as **the second Moses**.

**Where this sits in the one story:** Session 1 established the partnership — God calling a people to be a blessing to all nations. Session 2 traced that partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit. Session 3 shows **the King/Messiah embodying the story in person**. This episode advances the arc on two fronts: at Nazareth, Jesus *re-voices* Israel's calling toward the outsider (the Gentile widow, the Syrian leper), re-opening the Abrahamic blessing-to-the-nations that Israel's partner had curved inward; and at the feeding, Jesus *performs* the role of a new Moses — taking the whole Law, giving it to disciples, and demonstrating that when the Messiah is trusted to interpret Torah, there is "more than enough to go around for all of God's people."

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 3 arc — the Messiah embodying and re-issuing the one story — and backed by a transcript citation.

### 1. Jesus's hometown "yes" curdles the moment the mission turns outward
Nazareth loves Jesus right up until he says the year of the Lord's favor is for the outsider, not for them.
> **Marty:** "He goes to his hometown, he basically says, 'I'm the guy. I'm ushering in the Messianic age.' ... and everybody speaks well of him."
> **Marty:** "You want the hometown, but you don't get it. You don't understand why I am here. ... I'm here to pronounce it for them and now things change."

In the arc, this is the Messiah re-opening Israel's original vocation — a blessing to the nations — against a hometown that wants the blessing hoarded.

### 2. Jesus may have chosen his own reading — pointedly
The Isaiah passage matches no surviving lectionary, which turns a liturgical reading into a deliberate, provocative act.
> **Marty:** "None of them is the passage that Jesus reads a part of the haftarah reading, which is so juicy to me. ... did Jesus read his portion and when he was supposed to stop, did he just keep going? Or did he totally ignore the portion that was given to him?"

The King does not merely fulfill the text passively; he selects and declares it ("Today this scripture is fulfilled in your hearing").

### 3. The mission, not the hometown miracles, is the point
Jesus refuses the "special hometown treatment" because the outsiders — the widow of Zarephath, Naaman the Syrian — are the mission.
> **Chris:** "It seems as though what Jesus is talking about is an invitation to the Gentiles. It's a Gentile woman he's speaking of."
> **Marty:** "You know your Bible, you just don't know the mission."

### 4. Jesus models human grief, and that is instructive
Learning John the Baptist has been beheaded, Jesus withdraws — the text pauses to show his humanity.
> **Marty:** "'I've got to get away. I got to create space for some grieving and some mourning.' I just find that verse to be instructive."
> **Marty:** "We sometimes overlook how many places we actually see the humanity of Jesus and Jesus modeling for us what it means to engage emotion, and struggle."

### 5. The feeding of the 5,000 is a rabbi's lesson, not just a mercy
Everything a rabbi does is teaching; John 6:6 says Jesus "knew what he was going to do" — he was testing his disciples.
> **Marty:** "Everything that Jesus does is with intention and with purpose. ... This isn't just Jesus feeling sad because 5,000 people don't have dinner. This is Jesus wanting to do something again with his disciples. ... On some level, it's about Philip, particularly."

### 6. The "one big lesson": Jesus is the second Moses, and his Torah is enough for everyone
Read through Eastern number-symbolism, the loaves and fish are the complete Law, handed down a chain from Messiah to disciples to the whole people of God — with surplus.
> **Marty:** "He takes the complete Law and he gives it to his disciples. His disciples feed the people of God. ... That's 1,000 times 5. It's the whole community of ... Torah."
> **Marty:** "'I am the second Moses, when you let me interpret the law and completely trust me with it, there is more than enough to go around for all of God's people.'"

This is the arc made visible: the Messiah embodies Moses's role and demonstrates that trusting *his* reading of the story yields abundance for all.

### 7. Believing the miracle is nothing; letting it change you is everything
Ray's provocation reframes the whole debate about the story's "accuracy."
> **Marty (quoting Ray):** "All the demons that were on that hillside believe that Jesus did a miracle that day."
> **Marty:** "Do you believe that when you let him interpret the law and trust in his way of reading the world, that he's got enough to go around for all of God's people?"

## East / West Lens

Only the interpretive categories actually engaged in this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Numbers as symbol, not quantity (Eastern):** The entire payoff rests on numbers "saying things" to the Eastern mind.
  > **Marty:** "Numbers are important to the Eastern mind because numbers symbolize things — numbers say things."

- **Intertextual reading / everything a rabbi does is "in the Text" (Eastern):** The feeding is heard as a retelling of Moses and an echo of Elijah/Elisha.
  > **Marty:** "Everything a Rabbi does is always in the Text. ... any Jewish reader would have looked back on and went, 'Wait a minute. Well, that's like this.'"

- **Block logic / short sermon after long reading (East/West contrast):** The dereshah inverts the Western sermon-length expectation.
  > **Marty:** "They read the text for 30, 40 minutes, and they give a thirty to 90-second sermon, which is the flip flop of our experience in the Western world."

- **Lived obedience over cognitive assent (Eastern):** Belief that a miracle happened is worthless unless it reshapes life (an explicit James echo).
  > **Marty:** "What good is it if we believe that a miracle happened, if it doesn't actually change the way that we live?"

## Narrative Tensions

Surfaced problems, each classified, cited, verse-linked where relevant, and resolved Hebraically (or left open).

### 1. Matthew and Luke tell the Nazareth visit very differently
**Classification: contradiction / parallel-account divergence.** Matthew is accusatory from the start; Luke's crowd first praises Jesus.
> **Brent:** "In the Matthew thing, it's like, it's so much more accusatory, like, 'Where did he get these powers?' ... Then it explicitly says, 'And they took offense at him.'"
> **Marty:** "Matthew didn't give that impression ... and yet in Luke ... everybody speaks well of him."
**Verses:** [Matthew 13:53–58 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+13%3A53-58&version=NIV) · [Luke 4:14–30 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+4%3A14-30&version=NIV)
**Hebraic resolution (offered):** The hosts refuse to harmonize; Matthew is read as a *condensed* telling for an audience that already knows the pivotal synagogue moment, while Luke retells it fully for a less-familiar audience.
> **Chris:** "In the Luke account, you've got to retell the story ... whoever the Luke account is engaging might not be as familiar with this pivotal moment."

### 2. Jesus's reading matches no known lectionary
**Classification: unstated/withheld information (logic gap by Western expectation).** The text never tells us whether this was his assigned portion.
> **Marty:** "I want to know, what's his portion just before this, or just after it? Was this literally the only thing that he read? ... Are these the only verses that he read?"
**Verse:** [Isaiah 61:1–2 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah+61%3A1-2&version=NIV)
**Status:** Open — "dance with it." The gap is left deliberately unresolved; its very openness sharpens the possibility that Jesus chose the verses on purpose.

### 3. Did Jesus "magically" pass through the murderous crowd?
**Classification: interpretive reflex / "Star Wars moment" misreading.**
> **Brent:** "'These are not the stones you're looking for.'"
> **Marty:** "I don't think that's what takes place here at all."
**Verse:** [Luke 4:28–30 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+4%3A28-30&version=NIV)
**Hebraic resolution:** Within the mechanics of a lawful stoning (two witnesses required), no one can bring a true accusation, so Jesus simply walks out.
> **Marty:** "He's done nothing deserving of capital punishment ... 'Who has an accusation? Anybody? Nobody?' ... as mad as they are, they can do nothing to him."

### 4. Why does Matthew leave the Elijah/Elisha turn out?
**Classification: unexplained omission between parallel accounts.** The story fits Matthew's mumzer agenda well, yet he omits it.
> **Marty:** "This story would fit the mumzer narrative quite well. I was always curious why Matthew didn't put it in there and Luke did."
**Verses:** [1 Kings 17 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1+Kings+17&version=NIV) · [2 Kings 5 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2+Kings+5&version=NIV)
**Status:** Open — Marty explicitly leaves it unresolved: "I'll have to ask someday ... so who knows?"

### 5. One feeding or two? (5,000 vs. 4,000)
**Classification: harmonization question.**
> **Brent:** "Was it one time 4,000 and another time, 5,000? Or were they the same thing and one disciple was just ... couldn't see 1,000 people behind a little hill?"
**Resolution:** Gambino states plainly they are distinct stories and assigns it as a "mini Hagah Project" for the listener.
> **Chris:** "I just want to say they are distinct stories."

### 6. The number reading itself feels like "a stretch"
**Classification: interpretive-method skepticism (named by the hosts).**
> **Marty:** "There are always so many people that are like, 'I don't know, that's a stretch.' That's okay. Hold on to this for a few more episodes."
**Status:** Open by design — the payoff is deferred; the listener is invited to withhold judgment and keep reading the Gospels.

## Terminology

- **parashah** — the weekly Torah portion; the five books of Moses read through in a 52-week cycle.
  > **Chris:** "They are set up to be read through in 52 weeks."

- **haftarah** — the paired reading from the Prophets/Writings, on roughly a 3-year cycle. Jesus is handed the haftarah (Isaiah) in Nazareth.
  > **Chris:** "I think it's about an every-three-year cycle." / **Brent:** "Haftarah."

- **dereshah** (same root as **drash**) — the short synagogue sermon (30–90 seconds) delivered after a long reading.
  > **Marty:** "The dereshah as the short sermon, usually somewhere between 30 seconds and 90 seconds long."

- **netzer / Nazareth ("Shootville")** — "shoot/branch" (the shoot from Jesse's stump), the likely root of Nazareth's name and reputation.
  > **Marty:** "Nazareth — netzer — was basically Shootville ... in reference to the shoot that would come out of Jesse's stump."

- **s'micha** — informal authority; why rulers feared to touch John the Baptist.
  > **Marty:** "That informal s'micha that John has. He has the ear of the people so much so that rulers, tetrarchy, kings are unwilling to touch him."

- **mumzer (mamzer)** — the outsider; Matthew's agenda, evoked by Jesus citing the Gentile widow and the Syrian leper.
  > **Marty:** "The mumzer, using in a poetic sense of, like, the outsider. Jesus is here for these outsiders."

- **eremos topos** — the solitary place; traditional site of both the Sermon on the Mount and the feeding.
  > **Marty:** "That place that we called in one of our podcasts eremos topos, is the tradition."

- **Number symbolism** — a shared vocabulary of symbolic numbers: 1=God, 2=the tablets of Moses, 3=community, 4=the Gentiles (four corners), 5=the books of Moses, 6=sinful man, 7=the seven pagan nations / creation-completion, 10=complete community (3+7), 12=the tribes of Israel.
  > **Marty:** "5 loaves + 2 fish = 7. He takes all of the Law — the complete Law."

- **lectionary** — the fixed schedule of assigned synagogue readings; two main records (Babylonian and Palestinian/Jerusalem), neither of which contains Jesus's exact Isaiah selection.
  > **Marty:** "They had two different lectionaries. ... none of them is the passage that Jesus reads."

## Discussion Questions

1. Nazareth adored Jesus until he said the "year of the Lord's favor" was for the outsider — the widow of Zarephath, Naaman the Syrian. Where are you tempted to want the blessing kept "hometown," and what would it cost you to see it go to the people you'd least expect?

2. Marty suggests Jesus may have *chosen* an Isaiah reading that matched no assigned lectionary. If Jesus deliberately selected and declared "Today this scripture is fulfilled in your hearing," how does that change the way you read what Jesus does with Scripture?

3. The hosts read the feeding not as a flashy miracle but as a rabbi's lesson aimed at his disciples: trust the Messiah to interpret the Law and there is "more than enough to go around." Where in your life are you behaving as if there isn't enough — and what would trusting his reading of the story look like?

4. Ray's line — "all the demons on that hillside believe Jesus did a miracle" — insists belief is worthless unless it changes how you live. What would actually be different in your week if you lived as though God's provision and teaching were enough?

5. Jesus, hearing of John's death, withdraws to grieve. What does it do to your picture of Jesus (and of your own permission to grieve) to see him "create space for some mourning"?

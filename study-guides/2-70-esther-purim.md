# BEMA 70 — Esther: Purim — Study Guide

**Session:** 2 (The Prophets)
**Episode:** 70 · "Esther — Purim"
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings
**Primary text:** [Esther](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Esther&version=NIV) (the whole book — the last book of the Tanakh covered in Session 2), with [Esther 2:8–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Esther+2%3A8-11&version=NIV) and [Esther 2:15–17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Esther+2%3A15-17&version=NIV) read aloud

---

## Where This Sits in the One Story

Session 1 built the picture of **partnership**: God rescues a people out of Egypt and invites them to become a kingdom of priests who join his restoration project. Session 2 turns to the **Prophets**, and the arc of this stage is the covenant partner's (Israel's) **unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Esther sits at the far end of that arc — a **remnant-period** book under Persian rule, and the *last* book of the Tanakh the hosts study in this session.

What makes Esther a distinctive turn in the arc is who it is *for*. The books just before it (Ezra, Nehemiah, Haggai, Zechariah) were written to the small waves of people who *returned* to rebuild. Esther is written to the far larger contingent of Judaism that **stayed** in Persia — the roughly five-sixths of the people who never went home. God's refusal to quit, in this episode, reaches the partner even when the partner is scattered, assimilated, hiding its identity, and living under a drunken foreign king. The covenant is not confined to the land or the temple; it follows the people into exile and stays with them even when God himself seems absent — "God in disguise."

Relative to Session 1's partnership, this episode shows the partner learning a *new* form of faithfulness that the earlier stage had not yet demanded. In Session 1 the question was obedience and trust. Here, living inside empire requires something more: a **shrewd wisdom** that engages injustice and forces empire to crumble in the light of goodness. Esther, like Joseph before her, is a partner who perseveres, influences, and acts "for such a time as this" — and God, unnamed but everywhere, works providentially through her. It is the arc's "God's refusal to quit" sounded in its quietest key: no fire from heaven, no prophet's thunder, just a hidden God and a willing woman.

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **What Purim is (and isn't).** A festival *based on* biblical story but not one of the Levitical festivals ordained at Sinai; a later addition like Hanukkah. *Purim* means "lots." Esther is uniquely the only Old Testament book absent from the Dead Sea Scrolls, which fuels discussion of its late canonization.
2. **God never named, yet everywhere — "God in disguise."** The only book of the Tanakh not to mention God; the costumes of a Jewish Purim celebration ("the Jewish version of Halloween") picture a God present the whole time but hidden.
3. **Recommending Fohrman.** Marty returns to Rabbi David Fohrman (the Session 1 "go-to guy") and his book *The Queen You Thought You Knew: Unmasking Esther's Hidden Story* — promising a "flyby," a "skip a stone," rather than spoiling it.
4. **A writing, not a prophet; and who it's for.** Esther is in the Ketuvim (writings). It is a remnant-period book written for the Jews who *stayed* in Persia rather than those who returned to rebuild — a story about persevering, influencing, and obeying inside empire.
5. **The story opens: Xerxes, Vashti, and a drunken edict.** The king demands Queen Vashti present herself (the Hebrew insinuates a striptease); she refuses and is deposed, because the officials fear every wife will now "talk back."
6. **The contest and Esther's entry.** A "Bachelor/American Idol" gathering of unwed virgins into the harem — in context, sexual conquest. Hadassah enters under the Persian name Esther, adopted daughter of Mordecai, who tells her to hide her Jewish identity.
7. **The name Esther / Ishtar.** *Esther* derives from Ishtar (Canaanite Asherah/Ashtoret — goddess of sexual fertility); the aside that "Easter" is a Roman mishearing of Ishtar.
8. **Two readings of how Esther wins.** Take A (textual-critical): she wins by "sketchy means," but is perfectly placed "for such a time as this." Take B (dominant Jewish/midrashic): she wins not sexually but by her intellect and knowledge of Torah.
9. **The Joseph parallels (Esther 2:8–11, 2:15–17).** Brent reads both passages; Marty draws the harem/palace, winning favor, charge over the household, robes, crown, and exaltation — all lifted from the Joseph story — and the daily faithfulness of Mordecai teaching her the Text.
10. **Haman, the edict, and "doing nothing is condoning."** Mordecai saves the king, refuses to bow to Haman, and Haman engineers an edict to kill the Jews. Mordecai presses Esther (Fohrman links the phrasing to Numbers): to do nothing is to condone it.
11. **Esther's shrewd game.** Risking death before the unsummoned king, she asks not for a reversal but for a *banquet* — and insists Haman attend — planting suspicion in the king (a rival? an affair?).
12. **God in disguise again: the sleepless king.** The king can't sleep, hears the chronicle of Mordecai saving his life, and Haman — asked how to honor "the man the king delights to honor" — assumes it's himself and must parade Mordecai through the streets.
13. **The banquet, the gallows, and the counter-edict.** Esther deflects attention onto Haman, who is hanged on his own 70-foot gallows. The king will not reverse the edict (he must save face) but hands his signet authority to Esther and Mordecai, who issue a *counter*-edict forcing every subject to ask "what does the king really want?"
14. **Why "Purim" (lots) — the real meaning.** Not Haman's lots over the Jews' death, but **Esther's** lot cast before God: "if I die, I die; if I live, I live."
15. **The lesson, and the hand-off to the next episode.** To engage the injustice of empire requires more than obedience and moral rightness — it requires a shrewd wisdom that forces darkness to fall apart in the light; "we're going to come back to this in the next episode."

**Coverage verification:** All major transcript segments are represented above. Strays that do not fit a flow segment are parked in **Other Talking Points** below.

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 2 arc — the covenant partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **God refuses to quit even where he is never named — he works in disguise.** Esther is the one book of the Tanakh that never mentions God, and yet the whole plot turns on providence. God's refusal to quit does not require him to be visible; in the scattered, assimilated remnant he is present "the whole time, but he's in disguise."
   - *Marty:* "Esther... is the only book of the Bible to not mention God. God is nowhere in the book and yet God is everywhere in the book."
   - *Marty:* "it seems like in the book of Esther, God is there the whole time, but he's in disguise. He's never mentioned, but he's obviously there. God is in costume."

2. **God's covenant follows the partner into exile — even the part that never came home.** The books around Esther speak to the returnees; Esther speaks to the majority who stayed in Persia. The arc's "God's refusal to quit" extends to the partner precisely where it looks most lost: outside the land, under empire, hiding its name.
   - *Marty:* "Esther is a book that's written for the contingency of Judaism that is staying in Persia."
   - *Marty:* "we have a story about Persia and the King in Persia... what it means to persevere and to influence and to be obedient."

3. **Faithfulness inside empire now requires shrewd wisdom, not just obedience.** This is the new demand the arc places on the partner at its far end. Moral rightness alone cannot bring down Haman's edict; Esther must be "as shrewd as a serpent," playing the king so that empire's own machinery turns against the darkness.
   - *Marty:* "To engage the injustice of empire, to live in Persia... is going to require more than just obedience. It's going to require more than just moral rightness. It's going to require a shrewd wisdom in how you engage Empire in a way that forces Empire to crumble."
   - *Marty:* "Esther is the perfect example of somebody who is as shrewd as a serpent."

4. **To say nothing before injustice is to condone it.** Mordecai's charge to Esther is the moral spine of the book: survival is not neutrality. The partner cannot sit out the crisis and still be faithful.
   - *Marty:* "you can do something or you can do nothing, but understand that doing nothing is the same as condoning the action."
   - *Marty:* "To stand in the face of injustice and say nothing is the same as condoning the injustice in the first place."

5. **God can use a partner regardless of how they arrived — what matters is willingness in the moment.** Whether Esther got there by "sketchy means" or by Torah-wisdom, the book insists she is "perfectly placed at a critical moment," and the question is only whether she will be used. For a people ashamed of how far they have drifted, that is the arc's grace: God does not quit on a compromised partner.
   - *Marty:* "it's not going to matter how she got there, it's only going to matter whether or not she's willing to be used in that moment."
   - *Marty (recalling listeners):* "Are you telling me that God could even use me for such a time as this?"

6. **The partner's deliverance is cast as "lots thrown before God," not luck.** The festival's name is reclaimed: Purim is not about Haman's casting of lots but Esther's — her act of entrusting her life to God. Faithfulness in this stage looks like staking everything on a God you cannot see.
   - *Marty:* "It's referring to the lots that Esther cast before the Lord when she said I'm going to throw my lot before God. 'If I die, I die; if I live I live.'"

## East/West Lens

Only categories genuinely engaged by this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Reading the text for its hidden story rather than its surface (East).** The whole episode is organized around digging out "the hidden story and the hidden meaning of the treasures buried in the Text," following Fohrman's method against a flat, surface reading.
  - *Marty:* "always trying to dig out the hidden story and the hidden meaning of the treasures buried in the Text all the time."
  - *Marty:* "There is definitely, as there is with everything in Tanakh, there is a hidden story. There's a hidden message. There is a treasure."

- **A hidden, providential God rather than an explicitly intervening one (East).** The episode embraces the paradox that God is both "nowhere" and "everywhere" in the book — a Hebraic comfort with divine presence that works through ordinary events rather than demanding a named miracle.
  - *Marty:* "God is nowhere in the book and yet God is everywhere in the book."

- **Reading intertextually across the whole story — Esther as a new Joseph (East).** Meaning emerges by hearing Esther 2 deliberately echo the Joseph narrative in Genesis, so that an audience staying in empire learns from a predecessor who also stayed and persevered.
  - *Marty:* "There's all these parallels to Joseph, which would make a ton of sense if this is written to... the people who stayed in Persia. Because here's Joseph who's staying in Egypt."

- **Honoring the living Jewish/midrashic tradition as a reading partner (East).** The episode weighs the oral tradition, Midrash, and rabbinical teaching as serious interpretive voices — not just the critical-textual reading — and lets the dominant Jewish take "take the cake."
  - *Marty:* "There is another take of that in the oral tradition, in the Midrash, in the rabbinical teaching, rises to the top and ends up taking the cake."

- **Shrewd, wisdom-driven engagement over brute confrontation (East).** Deliverance comes not by force but by cunning righteousness — Esther out-thinks the empire, embodying Jesus' later "innocent as doves, shrewd as serpents."
  - *Marty:* "I can't help but think of Jesus' teaching, 'You've got to be innocent as doves but shrewd as serpents.'"

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension states the problem, tags its type, cites the transcript, links the passage, and gives a Hebraic resolution (or marks it open).

1. **How can the central book behind a Jewish festival never once mention God?**
   - *Type:* Theological/translation tension (divine absence).
   - *Problem:* Esther is unique in the Tanakh for omitting God entirely, which seems to undercut its status as Scripture about God's faithfulness.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "the book of Esther is the only book of the Bible to not mention God. God is nowhere in the book and yet God is everywhere in the book."
   - *Passage:* [Esther](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Esther&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The absence is the point. The book stages providence as "God in disguise" — present in every coincidence (a sleepless night, the right chronicle read aloud) without ever being named. The Purim costumes dramatize exactly this: a God who works unseen through faithful human action. Divine hiddenness is not divine absence.

2. **Did Esther get there righteously, or by "sketchy means"?**
   - *Type:* Interpretive tension / unstated moral assumption.
   - *Problem:* The text contains real indications that Esther won a sexual contest (reinforced by the Ishtar/Asherah associations of her very name), which collides with the expectation that a biblical heroine must be morally spotless.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "there are a lot of indications here that make you think Esther gets into this by some pretty sketchy means. She enters into a sex contest and wins." / *Marty:* "There is a more dominant Jewish take... that Esther, in fact, does not get into this by sketchy means... she impresses the king with her intellect—her understanding of Torah."
   - *Passage:* [Esther 2:8–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Esther+2%3A8-11&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The episode holds both takes without forcing one. Take A makes Esther human and hands hope to those who arrived at their calling through broken roads; Take B honors the midrashic reading that she won by Torah-wisdom. Either way the book's claim stands: God uses a partner "for such a time as this" regardless of the road she traveled — so the detail is held **open — dance with it.**

3. **Why does Esther throw a banquet instead of simply asking the king to reverse the edict?**
   - *Type:* Logic/plot gap (the obvious move not taken).
   - *Problem:* Having risked her life to reach the king, Esther inexplicably stalls — requesting a banquet, then inviting Haman — rather than making her plea.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "She doesn't ask, she gets before the king and her request was... 'I want to make you a banquet.' Which is a weird request. Like just ask him the question... why are you making this so much more complicated?"
   - *Passage:* [Esther 5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Esther+5&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The delay is the strategy. A king cannot simply unmake his own edict without losing face, so the real contest is between Esther and the *king's* ego, not Esther and Haman. By seating Haman at an intimate banquet she plants fear in the king — rival? affair? — and maneuvers him into *wanting* to be her rescuer. Shrewdness, not directness, is what cracks empire.

4. **If the king can't reverse the edict, how are the Jews actually saved?**
   - *Type:* Legal/plot tension (an irreversible decree).
   - *Problem:* The king refuses to revoke Haman's death-edict; the threat still legally stands even after Haman hangs.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "the king doesn't reverse the edict... he gives the ring and the authority and the power to Esther and Mordecai... 'Whatever you do, you have to save face.'"
   - *Passage:* [Esther 8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Esther+8&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* Esther and Mordecai answer an edict with a counter-edict (the Jews may defend themselves), leaving both decrees standing and forcing every subject to decide "what does the king really want?" The darkness is not overturned by decree but made to "fall apart in the light" of their wisdom — empire undone by its own machinery.

## Terminology

- **Purim** — the festival celebrating the book of Esther; the Hebrew word means **"lots."** The episode reclaims the name as referring not to Haman's lots over the Jews' destruction but to Esther's lot cast before God.
  - *Marty:* "the name Purim means 'lots.'" / *Marty:* "It's referring to the lots that Esther cast before the Lord."
- **Ketuvim (the Writings)** — the third section of the Tanakh, where Esther belongs; it is a *writing*, not a prophet.
  - *Marty:* "This is definitely in the Ketuvim, the writings section of Tanakh."
- **"God in disguise" / "God in costume"** — the episode's image for divine providence in a book that never names God; the reason Purim is celebrated in costume.
  - *Marty:* "God is there the whole time, but he's in disguise... God is in costume."
- **Hadassah / Esther** — the heroine's Hebrew name (Hadassah) versus her Persian name (Esther, from *Ishtar*), under which she hides her Jewish identity.
  - *Marty:* "Her name is Hadassah—that's her Hebrew name. In this story, she goes by the Persian name Esther."
- **Ishtar / Asherah (Ashtoret)** — the fertility goddess behind the name Esther; her cult was "incredibly sexually immoral and involved all kinds of prostitution," which fuels Take A.
  - *Marty:* "Asherah is the ancient Phoenician or Amorite or Canaanite version of Ishtar... The goddess of fertility, sexual fertility."
- **"Take A" / "Take B"** — Marty's shorthand for the two readings of how Esther won the king: by "sketchy means" (A) or by Torah-wisdom (B).
  - *Marty:* "Take A, perspective A, is that Esther gets into this with some pretty sketchy means."
- **"shrewd as a serpent"** — the wisdom Esther embodies (from Jesus' teaching), the mode of faithful engagement with empire the book is teaching.
  - *Marty:* "You've got to be innocent as doves but shrewd as serpents."
- **Remnant period** — the post-exilic era under Persian rule in which Esther is set; the same setting as Ezra, Nehemiah, Haggai, and Zechariah.
  - *Marty:* "This is definitely a Remnant period because we got Persia ruling."

## Illustrations & Images

- **God in costume / the Jewish "Halloween."** The governing image: a Purim celebration full of costumes because God, though never named, is present the whole time "in disguise."
  - *Marty:* "it's kind to like the Jewish version of Halloween. They're all dressed up in costumes... God is in costume."
- **"Skipping a stone" across the book.** Marty's image for his deliberately light treatment — a flyby rather than a deep dive — so as not to "ruin the book" by spoiling Fohrman.
  - *Marty:* "I'm going to skip a stone, as I like to say, across the book of Esther because I feel like if I really dive into what I would like to talk about I'm just going to ruin the book."
- **"The Bachelor meets American Idol."** The modern image for the virgin contest, used to then puncture the sanitized reading and expose it as sexual conquest.
  - *Marty:* "We're going to have a really wacky cross between like The Bachelor and American Idol."
- **The 70-foot gallows.** Haman's enormous execution structure (likely an impaling pole) built for Mordecai, on which Haman himself is hanged — a satirical, tongue-in-cheek reversal.
  - *Marty:* "he builds these huge 70-foot gallows... we're going to go hang Haman on his own gallows out back."
- **Esther reclining as Haman pleads.** The scene the king misreads as an affair — Haman "laying on her... pleading for his life" — sealing his doom through the very emotions Esther engineered.
  - *Marty:* "she is reclining on the couch, Haman is laying on her, the Hebrew says pleading for his life."
- **Darkness falling apart in the light.** The closing image of the book's lesson — wisdom and goodness forcing empire's darkness to collapse.
  - *Marty:* "to force the darkness to fall apart in the light—in the light of your wisdom."

## Discussion Questions

1. Esther is the one book of the Bible that never names God, yet "God is everywhere in the book." Where in your own life has God seemed absent in the moment but recognizable "in disguise" only in hindsight?
2. Mordecai tells Esther that "doing nothing is the same as condoning the action." Where are you tempted to treat silence or inaction as neutral when it is actually a choice?
3. Marty's "Take A" sermon angered some but gave hope to others who "got where they are through some pretty sketchy stories." Does it comfort or trouble you that God might use someone "for such a time as this" regardless of how they arrived there — and why?
4. Esther doesn't confront the empire head-on; she is "as shrewd as a serpent," working the king's own ego and machinery against the injustice. Where might faithfulness require wisdom and strategy, not just moral courage or blunt confrontation?
5. The episode insists Purim is really about the lots *Esther* cast before God — "if I die, I die; if I live, I live." What would it look like for you to "throw your lot before God" in a decision you're currently avoiding?
6. This book was written for the Jews who *stayed* in Persia — faithful people living permanently inside an empire not their own. How do you practice covenant faithfulness inside systems and cultures you did not choose and cannot leave?

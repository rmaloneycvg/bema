# BEMA 114: Fish in a Net — Study Guide

**Session 3 (The Gospels) · Episode 114**
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings · **Guest:** Corey Knadler (@FishGibblets)
**Primary texts:** Matthew 13:47–52; Ezekiel 47:6–12; Song of Solomon 7:9–13

---

## Overview

This episode closes out the string of parables in Matthew 13 by working the final parable — the net full of fish — through the Jewish interpretive method of **PaRDeS** (p'shat, remez, drash, sod). Marty, Brent, and returning guest Corey Knadler read the parable's surface meaning, chase its hints back into Ezekiel 47, Daniel 3, the Sodom-and-Gomorrah furnace, and the midrash of Abraham in Nimrod's furnace, and land on a drash: the fish are the Gentile nations, and the disciples' calling is not to *sort* them but to *bless* them. The episode ends on Matthew 13:52 — the discipled teacher who brings out treasures "new and old" — read as a remez to Song of Solomon 7:13.

**Where this sits in the one story:** Session 1 established the partnership — God calling a people (Abraham) to be a blessing to all nations. Session 2 traced that partner's unfaithfulness and God's stubborn refusal to quit. Session 3 shows **the King/Messiah embodying the story in person**: Jesus, teaching in parables, re-issues Abraham's original commission (bless all the "fish"/nations) and reframes the end of the age so that the mission — not the sorting — is the point. This episode advances the arc by putting the Abrahamic call back on the table *through the mouth of the Messiah himself*, and by insisting that even the teachers of the law must become disciples again to receive it.

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 3 arc — the Messiah embodying and re-issuing the one story — and backed by a transcript citation.

### 1. The mission is to bless the nations, not to sort them
The drash of the whole parable: if fish = Gentiles, then figuring out which Gentiles are "good" or "bad" is beside the point. The call is Abraham's call — be a blessing.
> **Marty:** "If the fish represent the Gentiles, why should we sit here and try to figure out which Gentiles are good and which Gentiles are bad? Just be about the business of blessing the fish, which was, by the way, Abraham's call."

This is the arc in miniature: the Messiah re-voices Genesis 12's blessing-to-all-nations as the actual content of "the kingdom of heaven."

### 2. Sorting is God's job; the disciple's job is the mission
In both the weeds and the net, the sorting agent is the angels at the end of the age — never the disciples.
> **Marty:** "The angels do the sorting... It's like God saying, 'You don't have to worry about how to distinguish. Your job is just go do the thing. Just go do the love agenda. Just go bless outsiders. Just go take care of the alien, the orphan, the widow.'"

Even getting the judgment right would be a failure of calling:
> **Marty:** "If they were to actually do the work, even if they got it right between the good fish and the bad fish, that wasn't the mission. The mission was to bless all nations, all fish good or bad."

### 3. In the parables, the *wicked* are removed — not the righteous "flying away"
A direct critique of rapture/"Left Behind" theology using the parable's own grammar.
> **Marty:** "Our typical evangelical Christian theology has us going away... but that's not how it works... it's the bad that are done away with. The good, like in a typical Christian, 'Left Behind' theology, we have the wicked remaining and being punished, and the good going away, but that doesn't fit like any of the parables."

### 4. Perseverance, not hedging your bets, is the posture of the faithful
The furnace remez (Daniel 3 + the Abraham/Nimrod/Haran midrash) carries a theme of standing firm against idolatry rather than waiting to see who wins.
> **Marty:** "Abraham's willing to stand in the face of idolatry... but then stand strong in his beliefs to persevere... Then Haran, he just wants to say the right thing... 'I'm going to hedge my bets.'"

In the Session 3 frame, the Messiah's followers are called to the Abraham-posture: embodied, costly faithfulness, not safe fence-sitting.

### 5. Even teachers of the law must become disciples again
Matthew 13:52 reframes expertise: the scribe who is "trained for the kingdom" is the one willing to un-know and relearn.
> **Marty:** "Every teacher of the law that is willing to become a learner... If you think you know, and you're not willing to go un-know, if you're not willing to go relearn... You have to be a teacher of law who's willing to become a disciple all over again."

> **Brent:** "New treasures as well as old. They're not discarding everything they've learned before necessarily, but they have to be willing to bring in new things."

## East / West Lens

Only the interpretive categories actually engaged in this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Block logic / layered meaning over linear proof (Eastern):** The entire PaRDeS exercise treats the text as layers to be mined rather than a single flat proposition.
  > **Brent:** "...searching for the p'shat, remez, and drash found in the story of fish caught in a net."

- **Intertextual (remez) reading vs. isolated verse (Eastern):** Meaning is carried by hinted connections across Ezekiel, Daniel, Genesis, and Song of Solomon rather than by the verse in isolation.
  > **Marty:** "All of these stories are brilliantly woven together, a string of pearls, if you will. Jesus connects all the previous parables he just told to even more remezes."

- **Rabbinic/midrashic truth vs. Western literalism (Eastern):** Akiva's "153 kinds of fish" is explicitly *not* a biology claim.
  > **Marty:** "If you look at that scientifically, you're like, 'No, Rabbi Akiva, you are completely wrong,' but he wasn't talking scientifically. He was talking rabbinically... he's talking about, well, there are 153 — because... they had identified 153 different nations, pagan nations."

- **Critique of Western end-times framework (East/West contrast):** The hosts name the Western eschatological reflex and set the Hebraic reading against it.
  > **Marty:** "We keep critiquing end times theology in Western versus Eastern mindsets, but your mind goes there."

## Narrative Tensions

Surfaced problems, each classified, cited, verse-linked where relevant, and resolved Hebraically (or left open).

### 1. An ambiguous, uncomfortable translation
**Classification: translation disagreement.** The NIV wording of the separation obscures *who* gets thrown where.
> **Brent:** "This is like a strangely unclear translation here in the NIV. 'The angels will come and separate the wicked from the righteous and throw them into the blazing furnace.' It feels a little ambiguous as to which of them is being thrown."
**Verse:** [Matthew 13:49–50 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+13%3A49-50&version=NIV)
**Hebraic resolution:** Reading the parable alongside its twin (the weeds) resolves the ambiguity — it is consistently the *wicked* who are removed, not the righteous.

### 2. The parable never says what makes a fish "good" or "bad"
**Classification: unstated/withheld information (logic gap by Western expectation).**
> **Corey:** "There's a lot of sorting of good and bad that is going on in this passage, but there's not a lot of talking about what is good and what is bad... it's not talking about what actually is making one set good fish and one set bad fish."
> **Marty:** "Why would it not tell us? How are we going to know?... It's probably good because it's a trick question. We're not supposed to know."
**Resolution:** The silence is intentional. Because the angels do the sorting at the end of the age, the disciple is freed from the burden of judgment and released to the mission.

### 3. "End of the age" pulls the Western mind toward rapture theology
**Classification: unstated cultural assumption / interpretive reflex.**
> **Marty:** "Your mind is thinking end times. Just on some level, your mind is going there."
**Verse:** [Ezekiel 47:6–12 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+47%3A6-12&version=NIV)
**Resolution:** Ezekiel 47 locates "the end of the age" as the *in-breaking* of the age to come and the Kingdom of God — the nations (fish) being drawn in and made alive — not a removal of the righteous.

### 4. The "fourth figure in the furnace" — Jesus, or Abraham?
**Classification: contested interpretation / anachronistic projection.**
> **Marty:** "I know when we read Daniel, we see a fourth figure in the furnace and we're always like, 'Oh, it's Jesus.'... We project all of our Christian thinking onto that parable."
**Verse:** [Daniel 3:24–25 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Daniel+3%3A24-25&version=NIV)
**Resolution offered:** If Daniel is dated after the well-known Abraham-furnace midrash, a Jewish reader would hear the fourth figure as **Abraham** — the father of faithfulness standing with his persecuted children.
> **Marty:** "Every Jewish reader has tears coming to their eyes like, 'Oh the faithfulness of Abraham. Abraham is in the furnace with them.'"

### 5. Marty's own unfinished drash on Song of Solomon
**Classification: open / "dance with it."** The host is candid that he hasn't closed the loop.
> **Marty:** "I believe the remez is somehow linked to Song of Songs 7:13... I just haven't nailed the drash... I'm wondering if there's a connection there and I just haven't cinched it up real tight."
**Verse:** [Song of Solomon 7:9–13 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Song+of+Solomon+7%3A9-13&version=NIV)
**Status:** Open — left deliberately unresolved for the listener to wrestle with.

## Terminology

- **PaRDeS** — the fourfold Jewish reading method: **p'shat** (plain/surface sense), **remez** (a hint pointing to another Hebrew text), **drash** (derived/applied truth), **sod** (God-given mystery).
  > **Brent:** "...searching for the p'shat, remez, and drash found in the story of fish caught in a net."

- **Fish = the Gentile nations** — Ezekiel's creatures stand for the nations; Akiva's "153 kinds of fish" counts the pagan nations.
  > **Marty:** "Fish are representative of the Gentile nations."

- **Remez** — a hinted link to an earlier text that unlocks deeper meaning.
  > **Marty:** "Jesus connects all the previous parables he just told to even more remezes."

- **Midrash** — rabbinic interpretive storytelling that fills gaps and draws out meaning (e.g., the Abraham/Nimrod/Haran story, Akiva's 153 fish).
  > **Marty:** "One of the most well-known midrash of Genesis is a midrash about Abraham. A midrash about Abraham being thrown into a fiery furnace."

- **Furnace** — a loaded biblical image; its first occurrence is the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah.
  > **Brent:** "Furnace shows up for the first time in Genesis 19." / **Marty:** "First usage is Sodom and Gomorrah."

- **The discipled teacher ("new and old")** — the scribe trained for the kingdom who stays a learner, bringing out treasures both new and old.
  > **Brent (reading Matthew 13:52):** "Every teacher of the law who has become a disciple in the kingdom of heaven is like the owner of a house who brings out of his storeroom new treasures as well as old."

## Discussion Questions

1. The parable deliberately withholds what makes a fish "good" or "bad," and Marty calls it a "trick question" — "We're not supposed to know." How does being released from the job of sorting change the way you relate to people you'd be tempted to judge as "bad fish"?

2. Marty argues the parables consistently show the *wicked* removed and the righteous *remaining*, pushing back on "Left Behind" theology. How does an "arriving kingdom" rather than an "escape from earth" reframe what you think the end of the age is for?

3. The Abraham/Haran midrash contrasts persevering faithfulness with hedging your bets to be "on the winning side." Where are you tempted to wait and see who wins before committing — and what would Abraham's posture cost you?

4. Jesus blesses "every teacher of the law who has become a disciple," one who brings out treasures "new and old." What "old" treasure do you most need to keep, and what "new" thing is hardest for you to be willing to un-know and relearn?

5. If the "fish" are the nations and the mission is to *bless* them (Abraham's call) rather than evaluate them, what would it look like this week for you to "be about the business of blessing the fish"?

# BEMA 65: 4 Isaiah — Hope — Study Guide

**Session 2 (The Prophets) · Book of Isaiah (4 Isaiah, chapters 55–66)**
Primary texts: [Isaiah 55:1–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2055%3A1-9&version=NIV) · [Isaiah 58](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2058&version=NIV)
Hosts: Marty Solomon (host), Brent Billings (co-host)

---

## Overview

This episode moves into the fourth and final voice of Isaiah (chapters 55–66) and, with it, into the **remnant** period of prophetic history. After an extended prophetic review — walking the whole table from the pre-Assyrian prophets through the Assyrian, Babylonian, and exilic voices — the hosts arrive at a tone that shifts yet again. Where 1 Isaiah was vineyard/warning, 2 Isaiah was woes, and 3 Isaiah was the suffering servant/comfort, 4 Isaiah opens with pure invitation: *"Come, all you who are thirsty, come to the waters... come, buy wine and milk without money and without cost"* (55:1). The suffering is over; God is providing. The one-word image for review is **hope**.

But it is hope with a charge. Marty reframes the famous "my ways are not your ways" (55:8–9) as a statement about God's **scandalous grace**, not divine mysteriousness. Then Isaiah 58 confronts a people performing piety — fasting — while exploiting workers and quarreling, and redefines the fast God wants as loosing the chains of injustice, feeding the hungry, sheltering the homeless. The chapter promises the remnant will become "Repairer of Broken Walls," circling all the way back to 1 Isaiah's vineyard. And it ends, strikingly, on **Sabbath** — mirroring how the Tanakh itself begins (Genesis 1) and, in the Jewish ordering, ends (2 Chronicles) on rest.

The guide tracks how the episode advances the one story. Session 1 established the **partnership**: God plants a people to bless the world. Session 2 (the Prophets) watches the partner — Israel — be chronically unfaithful and watches God refuse to quit. BEMA 65 shows that refusal at its tenderest: to the leftover remnant, the people who *persevered*, God says, "Come back home. It doesn't matter what you've done. I will forgive you." The hope is real, but it reattaches the partner to the original mission — care for the oppressed, rebuild the broken walls, keep the Sabbath — so that restoration is not sentiment but a return to being the people God called them to be.

## East / West Lens

Only the interpretive categories the hosts actually engage are tagged below; each is cited.

- **Reading a famous verse in its context, not as a detached slogan** — The hosts set the Western habit of quoting "God's ways aren't our ways" to mean "God works in mysterious ways" against the verse's actual grammatical context. Marty: *"Boy, do we put that out of context... But the context of this is: come back home."* And: *"the phrasing there, the context there is about God's scandalous grace. His scandalous forgiveness."*
- **Obedience as justice for the vulnerable, not performed piety** — Isaiah 58 is read as God rejecting ritual fasting that coexists with exploitation, in favor of concrete care. Marty: *"Don't think this is about pseudo, faux obedience. This is about true obedience that truly cares for people, especially those on the underbelly of the situation of society."*
- **Hebrew wordplay carrying the message (tzedakah / tze'akah)** — The near-identical sound of two Hebrew words is read as the point of 1 Isaiah returning in 4 Isaiah. Marty: *"I wanted to find good fruit... tzedakah and instead what I found was tze'ekah — the cry of the oppressed."*
- **Sabbath as the frame of the whole story** — The hosts read the placement of Sabbath — at the start of Genesis, the close of the Tanakh in 2 Chronicles, and the end of Isaiah 58 — as a deliberate theological bookend, not a stray command. Marty: *"They have Tanakh closing the same way that it began, and that's with an exhortation to rest."*
- **Sabbath as present-day resistance** — Following Brueggemann, the hosts treat Sabbath as a live, countercultural act rather than an ancient relic. Marty: *"one of the most countercultural, resistive things we could do, is observe a Sabbath... Not just obedience to a concept, but the heart behind Sabbath."*

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **"A prophetic review."** Marty calls for a review of the whole prophetic section before entering the final period: *"We've moved out of the Exilic period of our table, and we are pushing into the very last section, which we call the what, Brent?"* — **"The remnant."**
2. **Pre-Assyrian prophets recalled.** Amos (plumb line and ripe fruit), Hosea (the prostitute), Micah (the judge), 1 Isaiah (vineyard). Brent runs the images; Marty confirms.
3. **Assyrian prophets recalled.** Jonah (potential) and Nahum (**diyn**, judgment) held together; Zephaniah (**t'shuva**, repentance) and 2 Isaiah (woe). Marty narrates how Judah "immediately lost the plot of the story" after God spared them.
4. **Babylonian prophets recalled.** Jeremiah (weeping prophet), Lamentations (the alphabetic-chiastic-acrostic of lament and hope), Joel (locusts), Habakkuk (watchtower), Obadiah (to Edom; Petra).
5. **The exile and who was left.** Marty explains Babylon's practice of deporting the wealthy, educated, and useful while leaving the poor, sick, and lame to farm the land — and the detail that Jeremiah was left behind as "a great propaganda machine."
6. **Exilic voices recalled.** Ezekiel (strength; *Chazak-iel*), Daniel (son of man; the double chiasm, "the comeback kid" for Session 3), Job (perspective), and 3 Isaiah (servant).
7. **Entering 4 Isaiah and the remnant.** Marty keeps the Isaiah voices together, moving from 3 Isaiah straight into 4 Isaiah, and notes he "used to not even have a fourth voice" but is increasingly convinced of a distinct shift at chapter 55.
8. **Isaiah 55:1–7 read — the tone changes.** Brent reads the invitation. Marty: *"you can hear the tone changing completely. All of a sudden it's like, there's no more suffering. It's come. Come get what you need."*
9. **Defining "remnant."** The leftover group when all seems destroyed — "a small remaining quantity... a surviving trace"; always a remnant (Elijah's 7,000). To this persevering remnant God says come back home.
10. **"My ways are not your ways" (55:8–9) recontextualized.** Marty corrects the popular misreading; the context is scandalous grace, immediately following "he will freely pardon." Credits Rachel Held Evans's *Evolving in Monkey Town*.
11. **Isaiah 58:1–4 read — hope with warning.** Brent reads the confrontation over fasting. Marty: it is not "Care Bear hope" but "rainbow-Noah hope" — hope rooted in warning.
12. **The 1-and-4-Isaiah theory.** Marty notes the theory that 1 and 4 Isaiah were one voice with 2–3 dropped in the middle, and that 4 Isaiah also pairs well with Malachi (still episodes away).
13. **Isaiah 58:5–9 read — the fast God chooses.** Loose the chains of injustice, set the oppressed free, share food, shelter the poor, clothe the naked. Marty ties it back to 1 Isaiah's vineyard: *tzedakah* vs. *tze'akah*.
14. **Isaiah 58:10–12 read — Repairer of Broken Walls.** The well-watered garden, rebuilt ancient ruins, raised foundations. Marty returns to the vineyard's broken-down walls and briars from 1 Isaiah, now promised rebuilt.
15. **Hope for restoration, through hard work.** Marty considered naming the image "restoration" but settled on *hope* — hope for restoration, contingent on becoming the people God called them to be.
16. **The Sabbath bookend (58:13–14) — "a PS rabbit trail."** Marty reads the Sabbath close and connects it to Genesis 1 (begin on rest) and 2 Chronicles (the Tanakh's close on Sabbath, explaining the exile as forgetting the invitation to rest).
17. **"The inheritance of your father Jacob."** Marty hears a double echo — Genesis's Jacob and 3 Isaiah's servant (Israel/Jacob) — reading it as "all that suffering you did led to this inheritance."
18. **Brueggemann and Sabbath as resistance.** Marty closes on *Sabbath as Resistance*: for 21st-century Americans, truly keeping a Sabbath — the heart behind it, not a formula — may be among the most countercultural things we can do.

**Coverage verification:** Both read passages are covered — Isaiah 55:1–9 (segments 8–10) and Isaiah 58 in full (segments 11, 13–14, 16–17). The extended prophetic review (segments 1–6) is represented; the remnant definition (9), the "my ways" recontextualization (10), the 1-and-4-Isaiah/Malachi theory (12), the tzedakah/tze'akah wordplay (13), the Repairer-of-Broken-Walls return to the vineyard (14), and the Sabbath bookend with its Genesis/2 Chronicles/Jacob echoes (16–18) are all represented. Strays (the Jeremiah-propaganda detail, the "comeback kid" aside) are parked in **Other Talking Points** or captured in **Callbacks** below.

## Key Lessons

Framed against the Session 2 arc — the partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **To the remnant — the people left when all seems lost — God says, "Come back home."** The leftover, persevering group is exactly who the invitation is for. Marty: *"to this remnant group of people, God says, 'Come back home. Come, if you're thirsty... let me restore you.'"* **Arc link:** the partnership is not canceled by the partner's collapse; God pursues the surviving trace of a nation that failed, insisting there is still something to rebuild with.

2. **"My ways are not your ways" is a statement of scandalous grace, not divine mysteriousness.** The verse's context is pardon freely given. Marty: *"I know it doesn't make any sense, but my ways aren't your ways, and my thoughts aren't your thoughts, so all you gotta do is come back home."* **Arc link:** God's refusal to quit looks, from a human ledger of justice, absurd — forgiveness that outruns the partner's track record is precisely the "way" that is higher than ours.

3. **The fast God chooses is justice for the vulnerable, not performed piety.** Ritual that coexists with exploitation is rejected outright. Marty: *"This is about true obedience that truly cares for people, especially those on the underbelly of the situation of society."* **Arc link:** restoration reattaches the partner to the original mission — the vineyard was always meant to yield care for the oppressed, and 4 Isaiah refuses a comeback that skips it.

4. **Hope comes with a warning and a charge; restoration is hard work.** This is not sentimental hope. Marty: *"It's not all light, fluffy, rainbow Care Bear hope... This is a hope that's rooted in warning... you will experience the restoration if you do the things that you forgot to do at the beginning."* **Arc link:** God does not quit, but the renewed partnership still asks the partner to become who they were called to be — rebuilding the walls they let fall.

5. **The whole story bookends on Sabbath — rest, trust, and the goodness of creation.** Isaiah 58, like Genesis 1 and (in the Jewish ordering) 2 Chronicles, lands on Sabbath. Marty: *"Maybe it really is as important as where we started in session one. Maybe it is about the goodness of creation, trusting the story and finding a place of true Sabbath rest, out of which we partner with God to bless the world."* **Arc link:** this explicitly re-sounds Session 1's founding call — trust the story, rest in God's goodness — now spoken to the unfaithful partner on the far side of exile as the ground of any genuine restoration.

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension: the surfaced problem, a classification tag, the cited quote, a verse link, and a Hebraic resolution (or "Open — dance with it").

- **"My ways are not your ways" — mysterious God, or scandalous forgiveness?** *(Translation / out-of-context tension.)* Marty: *"We love to quote that verse when it's like, 'Well, God works in mysterious ways.'... But the context of this is: come back home."* Text: [Isaiah 55:7–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2055%3A7-9&version=NIV). **Hebraic resolution:** read with the preceding verse ("he will freely pardon"), the higher "way" is grace that makes no human sense — the point is pardon, not inscrutability.

- **A people fast and seek God daily, yet God rejects it — why?** *(Logic / expectation tension.)* Brent reads: *"'Why have we fasted,' they say, 'and you have not seen it?'... Yet on the day of your fasting, you do as you please and exploit all your workers."* Text: [Isaiah 58:3–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2058%3A3-7&version=NIV). **Hebraic resolution:** the fast God chose is justice — loosing chains, feeding the hungry, sheltering the homeless; piety that coexists with oppression is counterfeit obedience.

- **Is this fourth voice even a distinct Isaiah, or part of the first?** *(Source / composition tension.)* Marty: *"There's even a theory that 1 Isaiah and 4 Isaiah are actually one voice, and 2 and 3 Isaiah are dropped in the middle... I could definitely see somebody making that case, and I could even have my mind changed."* Text: [Isaiah 5:1–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%205%3A1-7&version=NIV). **Open — dance with it.** Marty holds the question loosely, naming the deliberate full-circle tie-back to 1 Isaiah's vineyard while admitting he could be persuaded either way.

- **Why does a soaring poem about justice suddenly narrow to Sabbath-keeping?** *(Logic / "feels out of place" tension.)* Marty: *"it gets really specific about Sabbath and it feels out of place, like, why that? Because that's really what this is all about."* Text: [Isaiah 58:13–14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2058%3A13-14&version=NIV). **Hebraic resolution:** Sabbath is not an afterthought but the root — the same invitation that opens Genesis and closes the Tanakh; true fasting and broken-yoke justice flow from being reconnected to rest.

- **How much weight can "the inheritance of your father Jacob" bear?** *(Interpretive-reach tension.)* Marty: *"I don't know if I'm making a stretch there or going too far with that, but I love how this gets connected back to Sabbath."* Text: [Isaiah 58:14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2058%3A14&version=NIV). **Open — dance with it.** Marty offers the double echo (Genesis's Jacob and 3 Isaiah's servant Israel/Jacob) as a reading he loves without insisting on it — perspective to play with, not a settled claim.

## Terminology

- **Hope** — the one-word image for review in 4 Isaiah; the shifted tone of open invitation and promised restoration. Marty: *"Hope will be the image of 4 Isaiah... Hope, 4 Isaiah."*
- **Remnant** — the group left over when everyone seems to have fallen away and everything is destroyed; "a small remaining quantity... a surviving trace." Marty: *"You're never utterly destroyed... There's always a remnant."*
- **Tzedakah** — righteousness / good fruit; what God came looking for in the vineyard. Marty: *"I wanted to find good fruit... tzedakah."*
- **Tze'akah** — the cry of the oppressed; the near-homophone God found instead of righteousness. Marty: *"instead what I found was tze'ekah — the cry of the oppressed."*
- **Scandalous grace** — Marty's framing of 55:8–9: forgiveness that makes no human sense, offered to any who simply return. *"the context there is about God's scandalous grace. His scandalous forgiveness."*
- **Sabbath** — the rest/trust that bookends the story (Genesis 1, 2 Chronicles, Isaiah 58); here redefined as the heart behind the practice, not a formula. Marty: *"the heart behind Sabbath and what it releases in our spiritual walk."*
- **"Noah-rainbow hope" (vs. "Care Bear hope")** — Marty's contrast for the kind of hope 4 Isaiah offers: real, covenantal, and rooted in warning rather than sentimental. *"It's maybe rainbow-Noah hope, but it's not Care Bear hope."*
- **Repairer of Broken Walls** — the remnant's promised identity in 58:12, returning to 1 Isaiah's vineyard walls. *"you will rebuild those walls that have been broken down."*

## Illustrations

- **Come buy without money (Isaiah 55:1).** The opening image of free water, wine, and milk signals the tonal shift from suffering to provision. Marty: *"Come get what you need. Come if you're thirsty... there's no money needed. God's providing, God's taking care of you."*
- **The vineyard's broken walls (1 Isaiah → 4 Isaiah).** God once threatened to remove the hedge and let briars and thistles grow; 4 Isaiah promises those same walls rebuilt. Marty: *"'I'm going to come take away their hedge. I'm going to break down their walls'... and now 4 Isaiah... 'you will rebuild those walls that have been broken down.'"*
- **The well-watered garden / spring whose waters never fail (58:11).** The image of restoration for the people who do the works of justice. Brent reads: *"You will be like a well-watered garden. Like a spring whose waters never fail."*
- **The Sabbath bookend of the Tanakh.** The story begins (Genesis 1) and, in the Jewish ordering, ends (2 Chronicles) on rest — and so does Isaiah 58. Marty: *"They have Tanakh closing the same way that it began, and that's with an exhortation to rest."*
- **Sabbath as resistance for 21st-century Americans.** Following Brueggemann, keeping a Sabbath today is cast as a countercultural, resistive act. Marty: *"one of the most countercultural, resistive things we could do, is observe a Sabbath."*

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty insists "my ways are not your ways" is about **scandalous grace**, not divine mysteriousness. How have you heard (or used) that verse? Does reading it as forgiveness-that-makes-no-sense change anything for you?
2. Isaiah 58 rejects fasting that coexists with exploiting workers. Where in your own religious life might **performed piety** be covering for a failure to care for "the underbelly of society"?
3. The hope of 4 Isaiah is "Noah-rainbow hope," not "Care Bear hope" — real but **rooted in warning and hard work**. What is the difference between sentimental hope and the kind that comes with a charge?
4. God's word to the **remnant** is simply "come back home." Who are the "leftover" people — in your community or your own story — most in need of hearing that invitation without conditions?
5. The episode lands on **Sabbath** as the bookend of the whole story and, following Brueggemann, as present-day resistance. What would it cost you to keep a real Sabbath — and what might it release?
6. 4 Isaiah circles all the way back to 1 Isaiah's vineyard, promising the remnant will become "**Repairer of Broken Walls**." Read against Session 2's arc — an unfaithful partner God won't abandon — how does restoration here mean returning to the original mission rather than escaping it?

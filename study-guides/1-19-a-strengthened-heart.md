# A Strengthened Heart

BEMA 19 · Session 1
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/1-19-a-strengthened-heart.md`
Book: Exodus (Exodus 5–13 — the Passover and its ten plagues, God's contest with Pharaoh, the hardening of Pharaoh's heart, and the call of Israel to be God's firstborn)
Co-host: Brent Billings · Host: Marty Solomon
Primary source leaned on throughout: Rabbi David Fohrman, *The Exodus You Almost Passed Over*

> **Session 1 overarching arc: partnership.** This episode reads the Passover not as "God wheeling and dealing plagues" but as a double pursuit — of two hearts at once. Through Rabbi Fohrman's "list of questions" method, the hosts expose a pile of oddities (why "Passover"? why the firstborn? why *ten* plagues? why a "three-day" request? who hardens whose heart?) and resolve them around one claim: God is not only rescuing His people, He is also pursuing Pharaoh's heart — "He also wants to rescue Pharaoh." The two Hebrew hardening-words (*kavod* = made heavy/blind; *chazak* = strengthened/resolved) turn the Calvinist/Arminian stalemate into a love story about a God who takes ten plagues because He wants *everyone* — even the enemy — to say yes. And the firstborn thread lands squarely on the arc: God is making a whole nation His **bechor**, His firstborn partner, "born again" through a bloody door and a Red-Sea birth canal to carry His values to the world.

## Key Lessons

- **The strangeness of the story is the doorway in — ask the questions before you reach for answers.** Marty models Fohrman's method and BEMA's own: *"let's engage the problems we can find in the text first. Let's just mess up the story by noticing all the problems."* The naming of the holiday, the firstborn obsession, the ten plagues, the "three-day" ask, and the who-hardens-whose-heart puzzle are all deliberately piled up unanswered.
- **Pharaoh is fixated on *precision*, not *power* — and that exposes his worldview.** Pharaoh is unbothered that frogs come or livestock die; what rattles him is timing and control. Marty: *"Pharaoh seems to be very unconcerned with power... What seems to have his complete attention is this idea of precision."* A polytheist expects the gods to throw power around; precision — a God in personal relationship with creation — is foreign to him.
- **Monotheism means God is intimately *on the board*, not a distant observer.** Via Fohrman's Monopoly image, YHWH is like Parker Brothers to a game piece — timeless, "outside your understanding" — yet, because there is no other god, "intimately present on the board with you." Marty: *"this God is intimately present on the board with you... in the game because there is no other God."* That is why precision terrifies Pharaoh.
- **There are two hardenings, not one — and English hides it.** *kavod* ("heavy, weighty, stubborn") is when Pharaoh *cannot see*; *chazak* ("strong, resolve") is when he *does see and powers through*. Marty: *"there's two different words... Does that seem significant?"* The whole Calvinism-vs-Arminianism debate, Marty says, missed that distinction entirely.
- **God hardens Pharaoh's heart because He is pursuing Pharaoh, not torturing him.** The reason for ten plagues is that God refuses to let Pharaoh fold until Pharaoh has truly *grappled*: *"God's not just trying to get His people rescued. He's also trying to get Pharaoh rescued... I would love, Pharaoh, to have you come on my team."* When Pharaoh finally *sees* (at the hail) and *chazak*s his *own* heart to say no, God honors the decision and moves on.
- **Israel is being made God's *bechor* — the firstborn partner who carries the Father's values to the world.** Marty: *"God is asking His people to be the bechor... I don't need you to have a message to give to the world. I need you to go be the message."* The firstborn's mark is a double portion of *responsibility*, not just inheritance.
- **The Exodus is a birth — Israel is "born again" through a bloody door and a Red-Sea birth canal.** Marty relays the rabbinic birth-image: a people leaving in haste "through a bloody door," the Red Sea as birth canal opened by the *ruach*. *"Water and spirit is Exodus language"* — the very language Jesus will use with Nicodemus.
- **This is who God is: relentless love even for the enemy, run out "to shalom's end."** Marty's closing: God is *"really pursuing, really chasing down... I want all of you to say yes to my love... If you're not going to, well, we're going to pursue justice here and we're going to run this out to shalom's end."*

## East/West Lens

- **Start with the problems in the text (Eastern wrestle) rather than rushing to systematic answers (Western resolution).** Marty names the method explicitly: *"let's engage the problems we can find in the text first... let's just mess up the story by noticing all the problems."* Fohrman's "10 to 20 questions" that make "the Western mind... freak out" are the point, not a flaw.
- **Names are loaded word-pictures, not flat labels.** *El Shaddai* is read by the sages as *Mi She'amar Leolamo Dai* ("the one who said to his world, enough"); *YHWH* evokes timelessness ("I am that I am"); *Elohim* carries a plural feel ("the gods"). Marty: *"Shaddai really means nothing... the rabbinical conversation played on this."*
- **God is known by concrete presence-in-relationship, not abstract attribute.** The Monopoly image insists God is *in the game* with His creation; monotheism means "the God is in relationship with His creation and nothing else" — precision flows from personal presence, not detached power.
- **Hebrew words carry the theology (two hardenings).** Reading *kavod* vs *chazak* dissolves a centuries-old Western either/or (free will vs determinism) by showing the Text is tracking two distinct interior states — blindness and resolve.
- **The firstborn is defined communally and vocationally (double *responsibility*).** Against the Western "double portion of inheritance," the bechor's weight is to "carry on the legacy; to follow in the footsteps of their dad; to spread dad's values to the rest of the kids" — a calling for a whole nation, not a privilege for an individual.
- **"Born again" is read first in its Jewish, Exodus key.** Marty: the phrase is "rabbinically centuries ago" about "being born a second time, this time as God's bechor" — recovering an Eastern meaning Western Christianity often assumes it owns.
- **Justice is relational and runs toward shalom, not mere retribution.** God's "justice" against Pharaoh is framed as the far edge of a refused love — "run this out to shalom's end" — not arbitrary wrath.

## Recurring Through-lines

- **Partnership (Session 1 arc) — God recruits a *national* partner, His bechor.** The episode is explicitly the moment God moves from an individual partner to a people: *"He found a partner in Abraham, but now He's found a partner in an entire... nation of people... Israel becomes the bechor of God."* The partnership is vocational: be the message to the world.
- **The God who knows when to say "enough" (*El Shaddai* / Episode 2 callback).** The patriarchs' name for God — the God of restraint who "said to his world, enough" — is now contrasted with the active, redeeming YHWH. Marty ties it straight back to the series' beginning: "The God who knows when to say enough, we said in Episode 2."
- **bechor — chosen/firstborn as calling, double responsibility.** The firstborn motif carried through the patriarch arc returns as the organizing key of the whole Passover: the plague of the firstborn, the firstborn donkey in the tefillin, Israel as God's firstborn.
- **God's pursuit of the outsider / the enemy's heart.** God chasing Pharaoh belongs with the series' repeated insistence that God loves those outside His people — "this is how much God loves even our enemies."
- **Being born again / water and spirit (forward pointer to the Gospel of John).** The Red-Sea-as-birth-canal and "water and spirit" explicitly reach ahead to Jesus and Nicodemus in Session 6.
- **Noticing the "awkward" detail as the key (nakedness → firstborn).** The method callback to Genesis: the oddly insistent theme (there, nakedness; here, the firstborn) is the thread to pull.

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat in the episode, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Framing.** Brent sets the topic: "the Passover and its plagues, God's interaction with Pharaoh, and the call of God's rescued people." Marty notes this is an "evolving conversation" and that the first-round version was a lean 30 minutes.
2. **The source: Fohrman's *The Exodus You Almost Passed Over*.** Marty recounts hunting Fohrman's scattered YouTube videos (pre-Aleph Beta, "22 parts," out of order), their disappearance, and their becoming the book he can't recommend enough. He will summarize, not plagiarize, and sends listeners to the book.
3. **Fohrman's method: a wall of questions.** Marty describes the signature "10 to 20 questions" opening that makes the Western mind "freak out," then the slow reverse-tying of them.
4. **Question 1 — Why "Passover"?** Of ten plagues, why name the holiday after the final one and not "Freedom Day" / "Independence Day"? Brent's hymn-title parallel (songs named after their first line).
5. **Question 2 — Why the firstborn?** The 10th plague, Exodus 11–12, then the jarring Exodus 13 interruption about consecrating the firstborn (sons and animals) at the cliffhanger. Callback to Episode 2's "awkward" nakedness motif.
6. **The tefillin and the broken-neck donkey.** Fohrman's observation: of 613 commands, the three in the tefillin boxes are Shema, Ve'ahavta, and — strangely — the law of the firstborn (broken-neck) donkey (Exodus 13). Why *that* obscure law? The firstborn keeps surfacing.
7. **Israel "is not" God's firstborn.** Fohrman's puzzle: Israel will be *called* God's firstborn though Abraham was chosen and many nations exist. Feels "weird."
8. **Question 3 — Why ten plagues?** It "feels almost sick" to drag out a rescue; why not one plague?
9. **Question 4 — The "three-day" request.** Moses asks Pharaoh for a three-day journey, not outright release — "a long weekend." Why?
10. **Question 5 — Who hardens the heart?** God or Pharaoh? The Calvinist/Arminian centuries-long argument flagged.
11. **Question 6 — Pharaoh "playing weird with time."** "Tomorrow" for the frogs instead of "now"; indifference to his own dying livestock but concern for whether Israel's survive. Pharaoh cares about *precision*, not *power*.
12. **The Tale of Two Speeches (Fohrman, ch. 4).** Reading Exodus 5:1–3. Fohrman's thought-experiment: as Moses, how do you respond to Pharaoh's flat "I don't know the LORD"? Two options — retreat, or up the ante. Moses' second speech ("He may strike us with plagues") seems destined to backfire; Pharaoh accuses the people of laziness and piles on work.
13. **Question 7 — Why does God suddenly care about His name?** Up to now God accepted names people gave Him (Hagar's "God who sees," Abraham's "God who provides") and refused to give His name (to Jacob at the wrestling). Now, in Exodus 6, God insists on His name, and later tells Moses to remember the "names" conversation.
14. **Reading Exodus 6:2–8.** God: known to the patriarchs as *El Shaddai*, now revealed as YHWH; the "I will" covenant promises (bring out, free, redeem, take as a people, bring to the land).
15. **El Shaddai unpacked.** Shaddai "means nothing" literally (not quite *shaddah*, "breast"); the sages (Genesis Rabbah 5:8) read it as *Mi She'amar Leolamo Dai* — "the one who said to his world, enough." Episode 2 callback: the God of restraint. Now God wants to be known for what He *does*.
16. **YHWH and timelessness.** Yod-Heh-Vav-Heh is "confounding"; "Jehovah" is debunked as a manuscript-vowel misnomer; the focus is God's timelessness ("I am that I am").
17. **The Monopoly image (Fohrman).** God is like Parker Brothers to a game piece — you can see His name on the board but can't comprehend Him from inside it. Monotheism = God intimately *on the board*; this is why precision (not power) unsettles Pharaoh.
18. **Why Moses has two speeches, explained.** Speech 1 ("let us go love and worship Yahweh") is a foreign concept to Pharaoh; Speech 2 meets Pharaoh on polytheistic terms ("God will be angry and crush us"). Pharaoh refuses.
19. **The two hardenings — *kavod* and *chazak*.** Two Hebrew words hidden under English "hardened." *kavod* = heavy/blind (can't see); *chazak* = strong/resolve (sees and powers through). Brent offers Blue Letter Bible links for a word study.
20. **Walking the plagues.** Nile-to-blood: Pharaoh *chazak*-ed (by whom unstated; Marty leans God). Frogs: "tomorrow" — Pharaoh *kavod*-ed/kavods his own heart (can't see). God sometimes *chazak*s a seeing Pharaoh because "he's not where I want him yet."
21. **The theological turn: God is pursuing *Pharaoh*.** Ten plagues because God wants Pharaoh rescued too — "would love... to have you come on my team." God won't let him fold prematurely.
22. **The hail — Pharaoh finally sees.** At the hail Pharaoh gets it and, for the first time, *chazak*s his *own* heart to refuse. God then honors the decision: "if that's what we're doing," the rest of the story becomes God speaking to His people.
23. **Back to the firstborn — the bechor.** Why the firstborn emphasis? God is asking His people to be the *bechor*. Brent asks for a reminder; Marty: double portion of *responsibility* — carry the Father's legacy and values. Israel becomes a *national* partner, a "kingdom of priests," God's firstborn.
24. **"Be the message."** As with Moses ("be the message to Pharaoh"), Israel is to *be* the message to the world.
25. **Born again / the bloody door / the birth canal.** Rabbinic "born again" as being born a second time as God's bechor; the (unverifiable, maybe-Akiva) teaching that the lintel/threshold frames a *birth* image; the Red Sea as birth canal opened by the *ruach*. Forward pointer to John / Nicodemus ("water and spirit").
26. **Honest loose ends.** Marty flags Fohrman's Part 4 (why Moses may have expected Pharaoh to relent) as his favorite — deliberately left for listeners to buy the book.
27. **Brent's observation on the name-dance.** Brent notices Moses himself may be juggling names (LORD/Adonai vs God/Elohim) to get Pharaoh to understand; Marty agrees Fohrman sees a deliberate "dance."
28. **Closing reflection.** The heart of God for insiders *and* outsiders; not plague-dealing but relentless pursuit, "run this out to shalom's end."

## Terminology

- **El Shaddai** (אֵל שַׁדַּי) — the name by which the patriarchs knew God, usually rendered "God Almighty." *Shaddai* "really means nothing" literally; the sages read it as a contraction of *Mi She'amar Leolamo Dai*, "the one who said to his world, enough." Marty: *"the God that the patriarchs knew... was a God that knew how to practice restraint."*
- **Mi She'amar Leolamo Dai** — the sages' phrase behind *Shaddai*: "the one who said to his world, enough" (Genesis Rabbah 5:8). The God of restraint from Episode 2.
- **YHWH** (יהוה, Yod-Heh-Vav-Heh) — the timeless, personal covenant name revealed in Exodus 6, emphasizing God's timelessness ("I am that I am"); rendered LORD in English. Marty: *"Jehovah is a total misnomer... It's not a biblical name at all."*
- **Adonai** (אֲדֹנָי) — the spoken substitute read in place of YHWH (the "capital LORD" of English Bibles); the intimate name Pharaoh does not recognize. Marty: *"Whenever it says the LORD in your English translation, that's the word Adonai."*
- **Elohim** (אֱלֹהִים) — the generic word for God/gods, with a plural feel (*ha-Elohim*, "the gods"); the concept of deity Pharaoh *does* recognize, contrasted with the covenant name. Marty: *"Elohim would be the generic word that Pharaoh knows, the gods."*
- **kavod** (כָּבֵד) — "heavy, weighty, stubborn." When Pharaoh's heart is *kavod*-ed it is made heavy so he *cannot see* what is in front of him. Marty: *"Kavod is when I don't see it... My heart is kavod-ed. It's heavy."*
- **chazak** (חָזַק) — "to be strong, to strengthen"; a word of resolve. When a heart is *chazak*-ed the person *sees clearly and chooses to power through* (cf. *chazak v'ematz*, "be strong and courageous"). Marty: *"chazak is when I do see it and I choose to power through."*
- **bechor** (בְּכוֹר) — "firstborn," carrying not merely a double portion of inheritance but a double portion of *responsibility* — to carry on the father's legacy and spread his values. Marty: *"the bechor has this calling to carry on the legacy... God's asking a nation of people to be His bechor."*
- **Shema** (שְׁמַע) — "Hear, O Israel," the first command placed in the tefillin. Marty: *"Command number one is shema, 'Hear, O Israel.'"*
- **Ve'ahavta** (וְאָהַבְתָּ) — "Love the LORD your God with all your heart...," the second command in the tefillin. Marty: *"number two is what they call Ve'ahavta."*
- **ruach** (רוּחַ) — "spirit/wind"; the *ruach* blows over the Red Sea to open the birth canal — "water and spirit is Exodus language." Marty: *"What blows over the Red Sea to open up that birth canal? The spirit, the ruach."*

## Illustrations & Stories

- **Fohrman's scattered YouTube "treasure hunt" (Marty).** The pre-Aleph-Beta videos "all mixed up based on your algorithm... a treasure hunt just to get through the whole series," which later became the book — a vivid origin story for the teaching's source.
- **Fohrman's wall of questions (Marty).** The signature method: *"his teachings always begin with a list of questions... 10 to 20 questions... And the Western mind is like, if you don't start answering these questions, I am going to freak out."* Models the Eastern wrestle.
- **The hymn/song named after its first line (Brent).** Brent's parallel for the odd "Passover" naming: *"they just name the song based on the first line of the song... that doesn't really tell you a whole lot about what the song is about."*
- **The tefillin and the broken-neck donkey (Marty, via Fohrman).** Leather straps and black boxes "right on your bicep, really close to your heart" and on the forehead (Deuteronomy's "bind them"), containing — of 613 commands — the Shema, Ve'ahavta, and the obscure firstborn-donkey law. A memorable image for how insistently the firstborn theme recurs.
- **The Monopoly board / Parker Brothers (Fohrman, retold by Marty).** The game-piece ("the hat or the race car") can see "Parker Brothers" on the board but cannot comprehend a creator who exists outside it — God's timelessness, and the monotheistic claim that He is nonetheless "intimately present on the board with you."
- **"Be the message, not give the message" (Marty).** As God told Moses, *"I don't need you to go give a message to Pharaoh — I need you to go be the message,"* so Israel is to *be* the message to the world — the vocation of the bechor.
- **The bloody door and the Red-Sea birth canal (Marty, rabbinic / possibly Akiva).** The lintel-and-threshold read as a *birth* image: a people leaving "in haste, in hurry, through a bloody door," the Red Sea as birth canal — Israel "being born again." (Marty candidly flags he could not source the "Akiva" attribution.)

## Discussion Questions

1. The episode's whole method is to pile up *questions* before reaching for answers — the move that makes "the Western mind freak out." Where in your own Bible reading do you rush to resolve the strangeness of a text, and what might change if you let the problems sit and "mess up the story" first?
2. Marty says God takes ten plagues because He is pursuing *Pharaoh's* heart, not just rescuing Israel — "I would love, Pharaoh, to have you come on my team." Who is the "Pharaoh" in your life you've written off as merely an obstacle? What would it mean to believe God is relentlessly pursuing even them?
3. The two hardenings — *kavod* (I can't see it) and *chazak* (I see it and power through anyway) — name two very different hard hearts. Which describes a stubbornness you recognize in yourself right now: a blindness you can't yet see past, or a resolve you've chosen against something you *do* see?
4. To be the *bechor* is to carry a "double portion of responsibility" — to *be* the message, not just deliver it. If God is inviting you (and your community) to be His firstborn to the people around you, what values of the Father would others actually read off your life this week?
5. The rabbis read the Exodus as a *birth* — a people leaving through a bloody door, the Red Sea as a birth canal opened by the *ruach*. Where do you sense God trying to birth something new in you that requires leaving an "Egypt" behind in haste? What makes the doorway feel bloody or costly?
6. God refuses to be known any longer only as the God of restraint (*El Shaddai*) and insists on the active, redeeming name YHWH. Have you mostly known God for "what He doesn't do" (limits, prohibitions, restraint) or "what He does do" (rescue, redemption, pursuit)? How would leaning into the latter reshape your picture of Him?

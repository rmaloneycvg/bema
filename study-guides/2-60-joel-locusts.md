# BEMA 60 — Joel: Locusts — Study Guide

**Session:** 2 (The Prophets)
**Episode:** 60 · "Joel — Locusts"
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings
**Primary text:** The whole book of Joel (three chapters) — [Joel 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+1&version=NIV), [Joel 2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+2&version=NIV), [Joel 3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+3&version=NIV)

## Where This Sits in the One Story

Session 1 built the picture of **partnership**: God rescues a people out of Egypt and invites them to become his covenant partner, carrying his character into the world. Session 2 turns to the **Prophets**, and the arc of this stage is the covenant partner's (Israel's) **unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Joel is the **hinge** of that arc. Marty names the shape of the whole prophetic witness — *"warning, woes, and hope"* — and places Joel exactly where the tone turns: *"Now that everyone has experienced the woes, and we start to head into the exilic period... you feel this shift of the prophet saying... 'hang in there because God's going to put it back together.'"* The partner has been catastrophically unfaithful; Babylon has come through like a locust plague and left nothing. And yet the God who refuses to quit does not merely threaten — he promises to *"repay you for the years the locusts have eaten"* and to *"pour out my Spirit on all people."* Crucially, the episode closes by sending us back to Session 1: the restoration vision ends with a fountain from God's house watering the valley of **acacias** — the desert tree and tabernacle wood from the journey out of Egypt. The woes of the prophets, Marty says, are asking the partner to *"remember our own story."* Joel advances the one story by turning the unfaithful partnership toward restoration without ever letting the partner forget where it came from.

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **Framing Joel** — a *"short little prophet, three chapters"* to be read in full; recap of the recent Babylonian prophets and their images (Jeremiah = weeping; Lamentations = the lament/hope acrostic; Habakkuk = watchtower; Obadiah = Petra). ([Joel 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+1&version=NIV))
2. **The image arrives immediately — locusts (ch. 1).** Reading Joel 1; the governing metaphor *"strikes us right off the bat."* ([Joel 1:1-12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+1%3A1-12&version=NIV))
3. **Marty on the locust plague.** The science of Middle-Eastern wind-driven swarms; the staggering magnitude; the Pacific-Northwest cricket-infestation parallel; the invitation to Google-image "locust plague in the Middle East"; locusts as the picture of Babylon's invasion and its aftermath of nothing.
4. **First call to repent.** *"Declare a holy fast, call a sacred assembly... cry out to the LORD"*; *"the day of the LORD is near."* ([Joel 1:13-20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+1%3A13-20&version=NIV))
5. **Return to the image (ch. 2).** The metaphor resumes and now blends locust and Babylonian army so you cannot tell which — *"I think that's on purpose."* ([Joel 2:1-11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+2%3A1-11&version=NIV))
6. **Second, deeper plea — "rend your heart, not your garments."** Brent explains garment-rending; Ray's story of the guide's death and the son tearing his gown; the PS about the temple curtain torn at Jesus' death as God rending his garments. ([Joel 2:12-17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+2%3A12-17&version=NIV))
7. **The decisive turn to hope.** *"I will repay you for the years the locusts have eaten"*; *"I will pour out my Spirit on all people"* — sons and daughters, old and young, male and female servants. ([Joel 2:18-32](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+2%3A18-32&version=NIV))
8. **"On ALL people" — the gender exchange.** Marty's mock-objection that only men lead and get the Spirit; Brent: *"I guess we'll see about that."* The point: Joel says sons *and* daughters, men *and* women.
9. **Warning → woes → hope.** Marty locates Joel at the hinge; the parent-disciplining-a-child analogy for God's shifting tone heading into exile.
10. **Judgment of the nations (ch. 3).** The Valley of Jehoshaphat trial; nations accountable for trading boys for prostitutes, selling girls for wine, selling Judah to the Greeks; the Sabeans aside (Marty doesn't know who they are); *"beat your plowshares into swords."* ([Joel 3:1-16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+3%3A1-16&version=NIV))
11. **The bookend of new wine, and the acacias.** New wine *snatched from the lips* at the start vs. new wine *dripping from the mountains* at the end; Marty's wine-tasting image; the fountain watering the valley of acacias; *"remember our own story."* ([Joel 3:17-21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+3%3A17-21&version=NIV))

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 2 arc — the covenant partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **God disciplines, but his aim is restoration, not abandonment.** Even inside the worst devastation, God has not left and does not plan to desert his partner — the refusal-to-quit is the engine of the whole book.
   - *Marty:* "the situation with God's people is definitely bad... but it's far from hopeless. God has not left their side. God is not planning on deserting them in their future. In fact, God is at work right here in their midst of this horrible metaphor of a locust plague."

2. **Real repentance is a torn heart, not torn clothes.** The unfaithful partner is called not to a show of grief but to actual change — *"care about what's going on. Be changed, be moved, be compelled to be different."* This is what God's refusal to quit asks of the partner in return.
   - *Marty:* "God's saying... 'What I really am asking you to do is actually tear your heart in two, care about what's going on. Be changed, be moved, be compelled to be different, rend your heart.'"

3. **The prophets move from warning to woes to hope — and Joel is the turn.** Understanding the partner's story means knowing *where you are* in the discipline; Joel marks the shift toward "God's going to put it back together."
   - *Marty:* "Oftentimes in class when we talk about the prophets, we're going to talk about warning, woes, and hope... now we start to head into the exilic period... 'hang in there because God's going to put it back together,' and the whole voice is going to shift."

4. **God's restoring Spirit is poured out on *all* people, not a privileged few.** The hope-vision explicitly includes sons and daughters, old and young, male and female servants — a startling widening of the partnership centuries before Pentecost.
   - *Marty:* "Even centuries before, Joel says on all people, sons, and daughters, men and women, adults and children, young men, old men, women included. He's pointing forward to this great and glorious day when everything's going to be made right."

5. **The discipline is meant to make the partner remember its own story.** The restoration deliberately recalls the exodus images (acacias, new wine) so the woes do their intended work: calling the people back to who they are.
   - *Marty:* "We find ourselves confronted yet again with images that began our journey out of Egypt so long ago, and that is what the woes of the prophets and the discipline of God is asking us to do. Will we remember our own story?"

## East/West Lens

Only categories genuinely engaged by this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Image/story thinking over systematic abstraction (East).** The book is carried by a single governing picture — the locust plague — rather than a doctrinal argument; Marty says there is little commentary to add because the image *is* the message.
  - *Marty:* "many of these prophetic voices have a picture or an image that they like to use to communicate their message. Joel introduces us to the plot of God's people by using the metaphor of a locust plague."

- **Embodied grief and gesture over inward-only sentiment (East).** Grief is enacted with the body — tearing the garment from collar to hem — and the text then presses *past* the gesture to the heart; the lens is explicitly flagged as foreign to Western readers.
  - *Brent:* "something that we're not really used to in our culture... The rending of garments was an ancient Eastern method of showing your grief, releasing the grief that you were experiencing."

- **God who feels — ethos/pathos (East).** The turn to hope is driven by God's compassion and jealousy *for* his people, not an abstract legal recalculation: he *"was jealous for His land and took pity on His people."*
  - *Marty:* "God is at work right here in their midst... There's something redemptive to be found in the destruction that the Babylonians have left behind, and Joel insists on this throughout the rest of the entire book."

- **Communal, inclusive restoration over individual/hierarchical privilege (East).** The outpouring of the Spirit is deliberately communal and boundary-crossing — the whole community, across gender, age, and status.
  - *Brent:* "On all people." / *Brent:* "Men and women." / *Marty:* "women included."

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension states the problem, tags its type, cites the transcript, links the passage, and gives a Hebraic resolution (or marks it open).

1. **Is Joel describing a literal locust plague or the Babylonian army?**
   - *Type:* Deliberate ambiguity / referential blur (literary).
   - *Problem:* Chapter 1's locusts become, in chapter 2, an army that "scales walls like soldiers" and enters windows "like thieves" — and you cannot tell which Joel means.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "now that he's taken the metaphor of the locust and talked about Babylon... you now don't know whether or not he's talking about locust plague or Babylonian army. I think that's on purpose."
   - *Passage:* [Joel 2:4-9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+2%3A4-9&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The blur is the point, not a flaw to resolve. Hebrew poetry layers the images so that the swarm *is* Babylon and Babylon *is* the swarm; both are "my great army that I sent among you" (2:25). The ambiguity lets the one metaphor carry God's discipline and its agent at once.

2. **Did the people actually repent between the two pleas — or not?**
   - *Type:* Narrative gap / ambiguous outcome (literary-historical).
   - *Problem:* After the first call to repent, Joel returns to the devastation imagery; it is unclear whether the people responded.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "whether the author, whether Joel is trying to imply that maybe the people didn't respond to that invitation to repent he thought they should, for whatever reason, Joel returns to this image of the locust plague to continue to make a point about God's discipline among his people."
   - *Passage:* [Joel 2:12-13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+2%3A12-13&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* Marty holds both readings — it may be a literary tool of poetic repetition, or it may signal an inadequate response. The text is content to leave it unsettled while repeating the invitation; the renewed, deeper plea ("rend your heart") does the work either way. **Open — dance with it.**

3. **How can the God "slow to anger and abounding in love" be the same God sending the devouring army?**
   - *Type:* Values clash / theodicy (theological).
   - *Problem:* God is named merciful and compassionate, yet he calls the destroying horde "my great army that I sent among you."
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "the conquest of Babylon was not light or trivial or a fun time by any stretch of the imagination. It's bad, but it's far from hopeless. God has not left their side." And the text: "I will repay you for the years the locust have eaten... My great army that I sent among you."
   - *Passage:* [Joel 2:25-27](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joel+2%3A25-27&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The episode frames this through the parent-and-child analogy: the discipline is real and painful, but it is bounded, purposeful, and oriented toward restoration — "you've experienced your punishment and now you need to get to the end." The same hand that sent the army repays the years it ate. Judgment and love are not opposites here but phases of one faithful relationship.

## Terminology

- **Joel** — a Babylonian-period prophet; a short book of three chapters, read in full; image: **locusts**.
  - *Marty:* "It's a short little prophet, three chapters... the image that we're going to talk about with Joel... is going to be exactly what you said, which is locusts."
- **Locust plague** — the governing metaphor: wind-blown Middle-Eastern swarms of staggering magnitude that devour everything; Joel's picture of Babylon's invasion and its barren aftermath.
  - *Marty:* "Joel introduces us to the plot of God's people by using the metaphor of a locust plague... 'Babylon has come in and it was like a locust plague.'"
- **"Rend your heart, not your garments" (2:13)** — the call past the ancient Eastern gesture of tearing one's clothing (collar to hem) to genuine inner change.
  - *Marty:* "What I really am asking you to do is actually tear your heart in two... rend your heart."
- **The day of the LORD** — the near and dreadful day of reckoning repeatedly announced ("Alas for that day. For the day of the Lord is near... Who can endure it?").
  - *Brent (reading):* "The day of the Lord is great. It is dreadful. Who can endure it?"
- **"Pour out my Spirit on all people" (2:28-32)** — the hope-vision of the Spirit given across gender, age, and status; a forward-pointing image that lands in the New Testament.
  - *Marty:* "this beautiful poetic image that's going to show up in the New Testament in a really big way... I'm going to pour out my spirit, on how many people... On all people."
- **Warning, woes, and hope** — the three-movement shape of the prophets Marty teaches in class; Joel sits at the woes-to-hope hinge.
  - *Marty:* "we're going to talk about warning, woes, and hope. We have now navigated the first two."
- **Valley of Jehoshaphat / valley of decision (ch. 3)** — where God gathers and judges the nations for what they did to his inheritance.
  - *Brent (reading):* "Multitudes, multitude in the valley of decision. For the day of the Lord is near in the valley of decision."
- **Acacias** — the desert tree / tabernacle wood from Session 1, recalled by the closing fountain that waters "the valley of acacias"; the exodus images resurface.
  - *Marty:* "acacias. Interesting. Been there before. We know what those are, session one."
- **Sabeans (3:8)** — a "nation far away" named in the judgment; left unidentified on air.
  - *Marty:* "I read that and went, 'Gosh dang, this doesn't ring a bell.' I'll do some study there..."

## Illustrations & Images

- **The locust plague.** The central image: wind-driven Middle-Eastern swarms so vast they leave nothing — the picture of Babylon's numberless army and of the barren aftermath.
  - *Marty:* "when these plagues come through an area, the amount of locusts we're talking about... 'Look around you, people of God, Babylon has come in and it was like a locust plague.'"
- **The Pacific-Northwest cricket infestation.** Marty's modern parallel: a stretch of freeway shut down because the road was slick with crushed cricket guts — a felt sense of the magnitude.
  - *Marty:* "we actually had a cricket infestation go through the Southern Pacific Northwest, so much so that they actually shut down a stretch to the freeway because there were so many crickets... the roads were actually slick from all the crushed cricket guts."
- **Ray's story — the son rending his gown.** On a trip, a longtime guide died of a heart attack; Ray brought the 2 a.m. news; the wife fainted, and her son (a doctor) tore his gown top to bottom — Ray never forgot the sound.
  - *Brent:* "he grabs his gown and he rips it. Ray says, he'll never forget the sound of that garment rending from top to bottom, and ripping in two."
- **The temple curtain torn (PS).** The four-to-six-inch-thick curtain torn at Jesus' death heard by a Jewish reader as God the Father rending his garments at the death of his Son.
  - *Brent:* "when the curtain of the temple is torn in the Gospels when Jesus dies... a Jewish reader would hear this as God the father rending His garments at the death of His son."
- **The wine-tasting snatched away.** Marty's image for the stolen future: the glass raised, the swirl, the nose — then the wine grabbed from your lips just before you taste it; the book opens here and ends with new wine dripping from the mountains.
  - *Marty:* "as they get it up to their mouth, all of a sudden it's snatched from their grasps. This is what happened to their futures."
- **The parent disciplining a child.** God's shifting tone across the prophets likened to a father who ends the chaos, doles out the punishment, then walks the grounded child through to the end: "it's going to be okay and we're going to make it."
  - *Marty:* "There's a period where I just put an end to the chaos... Then there's a period where... 'you've been grounded for a week... now I'm here to get you through the rest of the week.'"

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty insists that even in the worst devastation "God has not left their side... God is at work right here in their midst of this horrible metaphor of a locust plague." Where in your own hardest season is it most difficult to believe God has not deserted you — and what would it change to read that season as discipline oriented toward restoration?
2. God's call is to "rend your heart, not your garments" — "actually tear your heart in two, care about what's going on. Be changed." Where are you tempted to perform grief or repentance outwardly without letting your heart actually be torn and changed?
3. Joel deliberately blurs the locust plague and the Babylonian army so you "don't know whether or not he's talking about locust plague or Babylonian army." Why might a prophet *want* that ambiguity, and what does it do to how you read God's role in the disaster?
4. The hope-vision pours out the Spirit on "sons and daughters, men and women, adults and children" — "women included." How does Joel's inclusiveness, centuries before Pentecost, challenge assumptions about who gets to carry God's Spirit and voice?
5. The book bookends new wine *snatched away* with new wine *dripping from the mountains*, and ends by watering the acacias of the exodus story. Marty asks: "Will we remember our own story?" What is *your* "own story" that the discipline is meant to call you back to?

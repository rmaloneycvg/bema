# Faithful and Wise Steward

BEMA 128 · Session 3
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/3-128-faithful-and-wise-steward.md`
Book: Matthew (Matthew 24:1-51; Matthew 25:1-46) — the Olivet discourse and three parables; with remez to Jeremiah 25:8-11, 2 Kings 22:3-7, Ezekiel 34:17-19
Co-host: Brent Billings · Host: Marty Solomon

> **Session 3 overarching arc: the king/Messiah embodying the story.** Session 1 built the vision of *partnership* — God seeking a people to carry the message and restore shalom. Session 2 traced the partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit — the monarchy, the corrupt priesthood, and prophets calling a people back. Session 3 is the Gospels: the king/Messiah stepping into Israel's story to embody it himself. This episode sits in Jesus's final week, teaching on and just off the Temple Mount. Jesus answers the disciples' question about *when the temple will fall* — and his answer is not end-times speculation but a prophet's verdict on an unsustainable, corrupt system: "this system is doomed." He reinforces it with three parables (bridesmaids, talents, sheep and goats), each remezing a prophet (Jeremiah, 2 Kings, Ezekiel) whose context is the destruction of Jerusalem and the failure of its shepherds. The episode advances the one story by showing the Messiah pronouncing judgment on the very leadership whose unfaithfulness Session 2 exposed — and then handing the stewardship forward: *the priests aren't doing it, so you will have to.* The partnership of Session 1 is re-commissioned to the ones who will "be ready" by caring for the oppressed, giving an account for what God entrusted, and doing *mishpat* to the world. Jesus does not merely predict the end of a system; as the king embodying the story, he becomes the standard by which the sheep and goats are divided — "whatever you did for one of the least of these... you did for me."

## Key Lessons

- **The whole Olivet discourse is about the destruction of Jerusalem, not "the end times."** The disciples ask when the temple's stones will fall; Jesus answers *that* question. Marty: *"This entire discourse is about the destruction of Jerusalem. That is the topic that got Jesus into this conversation... There is nothing in Jesus's words that would warrant a shift in the subject matter."* Reading it as end-times prophecy ignores Jesus's own closing point that no one can know the future.
- **Jesus is doing prophetic reading of his own world, not fortune-telling.** He doesn't need "God Goggles" to see a corrupt priesthood is unsustainable. Marty: *"He knows that this thing, that the gate—he doesn't have to put on his God Goggles and look into the future to know simply by looking at the corruption of the priesthood, this is not sustainable."* The same critique can be aimed at any unsustainable system — including our own.
- **"Keep watch" means nobody can time it — the opposite of using it as a prophecy chart.** Jesus blatantly says you will *not* know the day, which is exactly why people shouldn't be chasing signs. Marty: *"he just says no, you'll never know, go and keep moving."* Destruction "always comes when people least expect it."
- **"One taken, one left" is not "Left Behind" — you *want* to be left.** Like Noah's flood and the wheat-and-weeds, it is the *wicked* who are taken away (to slaughter/exile); what is good always remains. Marty (citing his professor Dale Cornett): *"a theology best left behind... you want to be left behind, that's good theology."*
- **The three parables reinforce Matthew 24, they don't change the subject.** Bridesmaids = be ready; talents = you will give an account; sheep and goats = care for the oppressed. Marty: *"All three of these parables reinforce the exact same teaching he just gave in Matthew 24."* Matthew deliberately stacks parables back-to-back to teach a larger body at once.
- **The priests are the faithless steward — so the job passes to those who will do it.** The faithful-and-wise steward "represents the who? ... The priests, the priesthood... However, as we have seen, they are certainly not getting the job done." The implied re-commission: God's people will have to do what the leaders won't.
- **Whatever you have — five talents, two, or one — God expects you to use it.** The amounts map to depth of knowledge: five (books of Moses), two (tablets of Moses), one (**echad**, just God). Marty: *"it doesn't matter if you're a five-talent person, a Books of Moses person, a tablets of Moses person, or a nothing person; whatever you do have, God expects you to use appropriately."*
- **The harsh "king" in the talents is Herod Antipas, not God.** The parable's strangeness dissolves once you supply Luke 19's backdrop and read it as **kal va'chomer**. Marty: *"the character in that parable is not God, it's Herod Antipas, and the original story hearers understand that."* God is not the hard man harvesting where he did not sow.
- **The criterion of judgment is *mishpat* — how you treated the marginalized.** The sheep are praised and the goats condemned on the same axis: did you "hear the cry and care for the marginalized"? Marty: *"The goats are condemned because they do not join and partner with him in bringing justice... Mishpat to the world."*
- **The remezes don't add new teaching — they explode the same teaching with color.** Each parable's prophetic quotation (Jeremiah 25, 2 Kings 22, Ezekiel 34) deepens, not redirects. Marty: *"this is in fact the same story we've been learning all along... the same lesson, the same meaning, the same teaching. I like this Jesus guy. He's an impressive rabbi."*

## East/West Lens

- **Reading in context versus pulling a chapter out of a vacuum (an Eastern, whole-narrative instinct).** The entire episode is a protest against lifting Matthew 24 out of its setting. Marty: *"we pull it out of its context. We read it, particularly Matthew 24, in a vacuum and it totally screws up what... Jesus is doing in his rabbinical teaching."*
- **Levels of interpretation — p'shat / remez / drash as a Jewish reading practice.** Jesus (and Matthew) work the plain sense first, then the hinted-at prophetic allusion, then the applied meaning. Marty: *"We are going to want to ask questions about remez, drash, levels of interpretation... let's just deal with p'shat. Go through all three."*
- **Scripture quoting Scripture — the Messiah speaks in the words of the Nevi'im.** Jesus's apocalyptic imagery is not invention but citation of the Prophets. Marty: *"Jesus isn't making stuff up, Jesus quotes prophets whose messages will be about perseverance."* (Daniel's "abomination of desolation"; Isaiah's darkened sun and falling stars.)
- **Numbers carry meaning (gematria-style symbolism).** The talents are not arbitrary sums. Marty: *"the amount of the talents may have to be... pointing to something because we said numbers matter."* Five / two / one map onto the books of Moses, the tablets, and **echad** (God is one).
- **Rabbinic argument forms — *kal va'chomer* (lesser-to-greater).** The talents/minas parable uses a negative example to establish a universal, then presses "how much more." Marty: *"a rabbinical tool known as kal va'chomer—greater and lesser... the rabbi uses a negative example to portray a universal trait."*
- **Hebraic weddings as the lived backdrop of the bridesmaids parable.** The parable only makes sense inside the betrothal custom where the groom's return time is unknown. Brent/Marty: the groom goes "to build onto the father's house," returns at an unknown hour, and "there will be a lookout, they will wake everybody up and they will start a massive party."
- **Justice as *mishpat* — a concrete, action-shaped Hebraic category.** The sheep-and-goats criterion is named with the Hebrew word for justice rather than an abstract fairness. Marty: *"What was the word for justice Brent, remember?" / Brent: "Mishpat."*

## Recurring Through-lines

- **Trust the story / the one unified story (Session 1 anchor motif).** The episode's payoff is that this is not new teaching but the same story deepening — p'shat, remez, and drash all land on the same point. *"this is in fact the same story we've been learning all along... the same lesson, the same meaning, the same teaching."* (Marty)
- **Partnership — God's people must do the work the leaders won't (Session 1 arc, re-commissioned).** The faithless priest-steward means the stewardship passes to those who will care for God's household. *"the priests, the priesthood, right? However, as we have seen, they are certainly not getting the job done."* (Marty) — implied: *you* will have to.
- **The failure of Israel's shepherds / corrupt leadership (Session 2 thread, now judged).** Jesus pronounces the unsustainability of the corrupt priestly system that Session 2 traced, and the Ezekiel 34 remez explicitly condemns the shepherds who muddy the water. *"simply by looking at the corruption of the priesthood, this is not sustainable."* (Marty)
- **Mishpat / justice — caring for the marginalized as the heart of the covenant.** The dividing line between sheep and goats is doing justice to the oppressed. *"They hear the cry and they care for the marginalized... Mishpat to the world. What does God care about?"* (Marty)
- **Hearing the cry (continuation of the Exodus "God who hears" motif).** The sheep are those who "hear the cry and... care for the marginalized," echoing the covenant God who hears the oppressed. *"They hear the cry and they care for the marginalized."* (Marty)
- **God's patience and refusal to quit — judgment aimed at restoration (Session 2 arc).** The Jeremiah remez frames even Rome's coming destruction as God doing what he did with Nebuchadnezzar: tearing down "in order to bring us back on track." *"I've done this before. I've used Nebuchadnezzar to destroy my people in order to bring us back on track. Why wouldn't I use Rome?"* (Marty)

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Cold open — end times or ancient times?** Brent frames "one of the most perplexing passages," asking whether Matthew 24 is about the end times or ancient times. Marty is excited; he's tried for years to teach this well and thinks he's "close."
2. **Setting the stage (Matthew 24:1-3).** Leaving the temple, the disciples marvel at Herod's Temple Mount; Jesus says not one stone will be left on another. The disciples ask *when*, and "what will be the sign of your coming and of the end of the age" — Jewish eschatology (this age / the age to come) they keep trying to time.
3. **Herod's stones (illustration).** The Temple Mount as a gigantic box; stones fitting without mortar ("you can't even slip a piece of paper between these stones"); a foundation stone ~2000 tons / 4 million pounds that modern cranes couldn't move; the rabbi tunnels; the stones the Romans later threw down still lying at the base. (Chris-standing-next-to-a-stone photo; "big babies.")
4. **The framing claim.** We usually launch into end-times discourse and "start drowning in all the details." Even the NIV subtitle ("destruction of the temple and the signs of the end times") is debated. Marty makes the case up front: the *entire* discourse is about the destruction of Jerusalem; nothing warrants a subject shift.
5. **Matthew 24:4-21 read.** Wars and rumors, false messiahs, persecution, the "abomination of desolation" (Daniel), flee to the mountains. "Let the reader understand" flagged as a parenthetical — "Jesus didn't say that."
6. **Jesus's point: the system is doomed.** Not God-Goggle fortune-telling but a prophet reading a corrupt, unsustainable priesthood. Marty's modern analogy: America's debt/economic systems are "not sustainable"; lots of figures (candidates, teachers, podcasters — "hopefully not our podcast") will claim to have the answer. "Let the listener understand."
7. **Matthew 24:22-35 read.** False messiahs performing signs; lightning from the east; sun darkened, stars falling (a couple of lines from Isaiah); the Son of Man on the clouds; the fig-tree lesson; "this generation will not pass away."
8. **It's in the Text — Nevi'im, not invention.** The imagery is quoted from the Prophets (Daniel, Isaiah). Nevi'im (Prophets) vs. Ketuvim (Writings) clarified. The prophets' message is *perseverance*: "there will be no escaping what's coming on the world."
9. **Matthew 24:36-41 read + "one taken, one left."** Noah's flood: the ones *taken away* are the wicked (by the waters); like the wheat-and-weeds and the fish-net, "everything that's good always remains." So you *want* to be left — "a theology best left behind" (Dale Cornett).
10. **Matthew 24:42-44 read.** Keep watch; the thief in the night. Marty: this is about the *destruction* — those "taken away are going to be slaughtered, killed, taken into slavery." Jesus's point is that nobody can tell the future and destruction comes when least expected.
11. **Matthew 24:45-51 read — the faithful and wise steward.** The steward = the priests, who are "not doing their job." (Brent's "throwback to Joseph" on perseverance.) The question raised: what am I supposed to be ready *for* and *with*?
12. **The packaged six-point answer.** Context recap (Jesus has been going back and forth with Sadducees and Pharisees on the Temple Mount — the Sadducees' "home-court advantage"). Marty outlines: (1) rumors/false messiahs — don't believe; (2) the world gets crazy, unstoppable; (3) it must happen, persevere; (4) be ready = care for God's people; (5) the priests aren't doing it, so you must; (6) persevere and be ready by caring for the oppressed.
13. **Three parables, one teaching — p'shat first.** Not one parable in three (that's a Luke move); Matthew stacks three. Ask remez/drash *later*; first just hear the plain sense of all three in light of Matthew 24.
14. **Parable 1 — the bridesmaids (Matthew 25:1-13).** P'shat: be ready; the foolishness of being unprepared. The Jewish-wedding backdrop (groom returns at an unknown hour, a lookout wakes the village for a massive party). Some of God's leaders are not ready for the groom.
15. **Parable 2 — the talents (Matthew 25:14-30).** P'shat: you will give an account for what you've done with God's kingdom. Number symbolism: 5 (books of Moses) / 2 (tablets of Moses) / 1 (**echad**, just God) — deep, basic, or only-an-awareness-of-God; use whatever you have.
16. **Parable 3 — the sheep and goats (Matthew 25:31-46).** P'shat: the criterion of judgment is caring for those in need — *mishpat*. The goats are condemned for not partnering with God in bringing justice; the sheep "hear the cry."
17. **Summary — the parables reinforce Matthew 24.** Bridesmaids (be ready), talents (give an account), sheep and goats (care for the oppressed) all restate the Matthew 24 teaching on a p'shat level.
18. **Remez 1 — Jeremiah 25:8-11 (bridesmaids).** Nebuchadnezzar as God's tool to destroy and remove "the voices of bride and bridegroom." Drash: God using Rome as a Nebuchadnezzar-of-sorts to destroy Jerusalem — "I've done this before... Why wouldn't I use Rome?"
19. **Remez 2 — 2 Kings 22:3-7 (talents) + Herod Antipas backdrop.** The harsh "king" is Herod Antipas (Luke 19's ten minas; the three sons sailing to Rome, the Pharisees' fourth ship, Antipas named tetrarch and slaughtering Jews). The remez: temple workers so honest they needn't give account — unlike the priests of Jesus's day; non-priests should do the honest work of restoration.
20. **Remez 3 — Ezekiel 34:17-19 (sheep and goats).** "Almost indisputable." Ezekiel 34 condemns the priesthood/shepherds who trample pasture and muddy water; the dividing factor is how they treat others. "How many sermons have... dealt with the remez of Ezekiel 34?"
21. **Grand synthesis.** Read together: *God is about to use Rome as a Nebuchadnezzar to judge his people; be faithful workers who can be trusted with the kingdom's investments even if the priests won't; you will be judged by how you treated others.* Same teaching as Matthew 24, "the same story we've been learning all along." "I like this Jesus guy. He's an impressive rabbi."

## Terminology

- **p'shat** (Hebrew פְּשָׁט, "plain / simple sense") — the first level of interpretation; the straightforward meaning before deeper layers. Marty: *"let's just deal with p'shat. Go through all three and just think p'shat all at once."*
- **remez** (Hebrew רֶמֶז, "hint") — a hinted-at allusion, here a prophetic quotation that opens a deeper layer. Marty: *"the remez to every single one of these parables should only reinforce, explode with color."*
- **drash** (Hebrew דְּרַשׁ, "to seek / inquire") — the applied/interpretive meaning drawn out from the remez. Marty: *"Would it make sense that Jesus's drash would be the use of Rome to destroy the city of Jerusalem?"*
- **Nevi'im** (Hebrew נְבִיאִים, "Prophets") — the prophetic division of the Hebrew Bible, source of Jesus's apocalyptic imagery. Marty: *"this whole section here is full of Jesus, using multiple prophetic images from the Nevi'im."*
- **Ketuvim** (Hebrew כְּתוּבִים, "Writings") — the third division of the Tanakh, distinguished from the Nevi'im in the exchange (Brent first mislabels Nevi'im as "the writings"). Marty: *"Not quite, that's Ketuvim."*
- **echad** (Hebrew אֶחָד, "one") — "one" as in the Shema's "God is one"; the symbolic value of the single talent (all a person has is an awareness of God). Marty: *"One was always symbolic of just God, echad, God is one."*
- **kal va'chomer** (Hebrew קַל וָחֹמֶר, "lesser and greater" / "how much more") — a rabbinic argument form using a negative example to establish a universal, then pressing "how much more"; the device behind the talents/minas parable. Marty: *"a rabbinical tool known as kal va'chomer—greater and lesser."*
- **mishpat** (Hebrew מִשְׁפָּט, "justice") — the Hebrew word for justice; the criterion separating the sheep from the goats (caring for the marginalized). Marty: *"Mishpat to the world. What does God care about?"*
- **this age / the age to come** — the two-age Jewish eschatology the disciples keep trying to time (this present world vs. the coming age). Marty: *"They have this age, which is the age of this world, and then the age to come... When is the age to come going to show up?"*
- **"one taken, one left"** — the "left behind" image Jesus draws from Noah's flood: contra popular rapture readings, it is the *wicked* who are taken (to slaughter/exile), so you *want* to be left. Marty (via Dale Cornett): *"a theology best left behind... you want to be left behind, that's good theology."*
- **"let the reader understand"** — a parenthetical in Matthew 24:15 (likely narratorial), repurposed in the episode as "let the listener understand." Brent: *"that's a parenthetical. It's set off by dashes in the NIV... There's no punctuation in the original text."*
- **the faithful and wise steward** — the figure of Matthew 24:45, identified as the priesthood put in charge of God's household but failing the job. Marty: *"The faithful and why steward represents the who... The priests, the priesthood, right?"*

## Illustrations & Stories

- **Herod's Temple Mount stones (Marty).** The Temple Mount as a gigantic box of perfectly quarried stones with no mortar — "you can't even slip a piece of paper between these stones" — including a foundation stone of ~2000 tons (4 million pounds) that even six or seven of today's largest cranes couldn't move; the "rabbi tunnels" below; the stones the Romans later threw down still lying at the base. A concrete picture of what Jesus said would be "torn down."
- **"A theology best left behind" — Professor Dale Cornett (Marty).** Marty's Bible-college eschatology professor who repeatedly quipped that dispensational "left behind" theology is "a theology best left behind" — "you want to be left behind, that's good theology." Used to flip the popular rapture reading of "one taken, one left."
- **America's unsustainable systems (Marty).** Jesus reading a doomed system is likened to critiquing modern America's debt and economic systems as "not sustainable" — "it will either change or it will crumble" — and to the parade of candidates, teachers, authors, and "podcasters—hopefully not our podcast" who claim to "have the answer."
- **The "frustrated listener" email (Marty).** Marty's first critical email, from a listener frustrated he critiques America — offered as a live example of why God's people must "think critically about the world we live in."
- **Herod Antipas and the four ships (Marty).** The historical backdrop to the talents/minas parable: on Herod the Great's death, his three sons sail to Rome to petition Caesar to honor the will; the Pharisees send a fourth ship pleading against Antipas; Caesar names Antipas tetrarch ("all the responsibility of a king without the title"), and Antipas returns furious and slaughters the Jews who opposed him by the thousands. This is the "evil king" of the parable.
- **Josephus-style "throwback to Joseph" (Brent/Marty).** Brent hears a "throwback to Joseph" in the cut-to-pieces steward imagery; Marty connects it to Joseph in Genesis as "another story of perseverance" — fitting Jesus's point about enduring the coming crisis.

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty insists the whole Olivet discourse is about the destruction of Jerusalem, not the end times — and that reading it as prophecy-charting "completely ignore[s] Jesus's closing points about not knowing the future." How were you taught to read Matthew 24? What changes if Jesus's point is "keep watch *because* you'll never know," rather than "here are the signs to decode"?
2. Jesus doesn't need "God Goggles" to see a corrupt system is doomed — he reads it the way a prophet reads his own world. Marty applies the same lens to modern "unsustainable" systems. Where might that prophetic reading apply today, and how do you tell a faithful critique from mere cynicism or partisanship?
3. The faithful-and-wise steward is the priesthood — and they're "not getting the job done," so the stewardship passes to those who will. If God's people inherit the work the leaders won't do, what does "giving the household their food at the proper time" look like for you and your community?
4. The talents map onto what you've been given — five (deep knowledge of the Text), two (basic knowledge), one (just an awareness of God). Which are you, honestly? And since "God expects you to use whatever you have," what does burying your one talent in the ground look like in your life — and what would investing it look like instead?
5. The sheep and goats are divided by *mishpat* — whether they "heard the cry" of the marginalized. The king says "whatever you did for one of the least of these... you did for me." Who are "the least of these" in your actual proximity, and what would it mean that caring for them is caring for the king himself?
6. Three times Jesus takes a parable and remezes a prophet (Jeremiah, 2 Kings, Ezekiel) so the "same teaching" explodes "with color." Pick one of the three remezes — how does knowing the Old Testament passage change the parable for you? What do you lose when you read the parable "in a vacuum"?
7. Session 3 shows the king embodying the story and judging the faithless shepherds of Session 2. Jeremiah's remez frames even Rome's destruction as God tearing down "to bring us back on track" (as with Nebuchadnezzar). How do you hold together a God who judges a corrupt system *and* a God whose aim in judgment is restoration?

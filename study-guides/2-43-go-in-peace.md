# Go in Peace

BEMA 43 · Session 2
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/2-43-go-in-peace.md`
Book: 2 Kings 5:1-19 — Naaman the Aramean commander, his leprosy, and Elisha's "Go in peace"
Hosts: Marty Solomon & Brent Billings

> This episode closes BEMA's walk through the kings before the move into the wisdom literature ("the tools"). Marty opens with a full meta-narrative review — Preface, Introduction, Tale of Two Kingdoms, the Sinai wedding, the redemption cycle, the two-sources history of the kings, the "Boam Boys," and the split-kingdom mess — then lands on one last story out of that chaos: a foreign *ish gadol* with leprosy, pointed to Israel's God by a nameless captive girl, washed clean in a dirty crick, and sent home with latent idolatry still intact under the words "Go in peace." Against the Session 2 arc — **the partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit** — this story runs a startling counter-current: while *Israel's* own kings keep rejecting God, a *pagan outsider* comes to genuine *yada* of Adonai, and Elisha extends grace into the gray instead of demanding a clean break. The God who won't quit on an unfaithful covenant partner also won't quit on a half-converted Aramean who still has to bow in the temple of Rimmon.

## Key Lessons

- **"Go in peace," not "turn or burn."** The whole episode drives at Elisha's final word. Marty lists everything his own training would have demanded — *"This is the call of the gospel. Turn or burn, you know. Heaven or hell. You gotta make a choice."* — and then asks which of those lines Elisha uses. Brent: *"Go in peace."* Marty: *"in the midst of all of this... all of the misunderstanding and all of the bad theology and maybe even like latent idolatry... His big response is: You'll be okay. Go in peace."*
- **Naaman "totally gets it" and "totally doesn't" — and that is allowed to stand.** His confession is enormous, yet his theology is primitive (geographical gods, two mules of dirt, bowing in Rimmon). Marty: *"here's my point: Naaman totally gets it. And at the same time, Naaman totally doesn't."* He *"still needs some good theological training... He's headed back to a pretty rough neighborhood. There's no synagogue."* Grace does not wait for the theology to be finished.
- **Relationship, not resolved answers, is what carries a person.** Marty's takeaway for pastors, parents, and friends: *"it's not about the answer to the question. It's about the relationship you have with the God who's going to be with you as you go live in your context, in Aram or a temple of Rimmon or anywhere else. Go in peace, go with God, it will be okay."* The assumption behind the blessing is *"you'll figure it out."*
- **Insecurity reads a cry for help as a threat — the "bad eye."** The king of Israel tears his robes at a sick man's request: *"Am I God?... See how he is trying to pick a quarrel with me."* Marty: *"Here's somebody looking for help, but all we see is a problem... How destructive can our insecurity be?"* — tied forward to Jesus's good-eye/bad-eye teaching.
- **God works through the lowest, not the *ish gadol*.** The deliverance comes not through the great man's gold and motorcade but through a traumatized, nameless slave girl who *"sees"* Naaman as a sufferer. Marty: *"This girl sees Naaman. Like she sees him. She sees the plight he's in."* Elisha won't even come to the door, and takes no gift: *"That's not how this God works. I wasn't impressed when you came to the door. I'm not taking your gifts now."*
- **God's power doesn't need grandeur — it meets you in the dirty crick.** Naaman wants spectacle (*"I thought for sure he would come out, stand there, invoke the name of the LORD... wave his hand"*). Instead: wash seven times in an unimpressive river. His servants' *kal va'chomer* logic — *"if the prophet had told you to do some great thing, would you not have done it? How much more... Wash and be cleansed"* — gets him into the water, and he comes out clean "like a young boy."
- **A pagan outsider reaches true *yada* while Israel's kings keep rejecting God.** Brent names it: *"that's yada right there. He has experienced—"* Naaman's *"Now I know that there is no god in all the world except in Israel"* is experiential knowing, not intellectual assent — and it lands in the mouth of a foreigner, during the very stretch of story where the covenant partner is most unfaithful.

## East/West Lens

- **Faith as relational experience vs intellectual creeds** — Naaman's confession is *yada*, knowing-by-experience: Brent, *"that's yada right there. He has experienced—"* Marty frames the whole lesson as relationship over resolved doctrine: *"it's not about the answer to the question. It's about the relationship you have with the God who's going to be with you."*
- **Community vs individual** — Deliverance is routed through the social web, not the hero: a captive girl → her mistress → Naaman → the king → Elisha → the servants who talk him into the river. Marty lingers on the girl who *"sees"* Naaman, and on the practical servants (*"My father, if the prophet had told you to do some great thing..."*) as the ones who actually move the story.
- **God's description (concrete relationship / story)** — The lesson is told in felt images, not propositions: the room shifting when an *ish gadol* enters, the dirty crick, two mules of dirt, a king tearing his robes. Marty resists sermonizing the slave girl's trauma — *"I'm not going to make a sermon out of this. It's just crazy"* — keeping it narrative.
- **Numbers / symbol over literal** — "Wash *seven times* in the Jordan"; Marty treats the unimpressive crick symbolically against Naaman's grand Damascus rivers (Abana, Pharpar), and reads Naaman's two mules of dirt as a window into an entire ancient worldview (gods ruled by geography) rather than a mere travel detail.
- **Truth over time (unfolding, dynamic)** — Elisha's "Go in peace" explicitly defers resolution: *"I think the assumption is: you'll figure it out. You will figure it out."* Marty also points forward (good eye/bad eye "in Session 3," the move into wisdom literature), letting meaning accrue across the arc.

## Recurring Through-lines

- **Trust the story / the one meta-narrative (the arc itself).** The episode is framed as a deliberate review: *"Let's do a little review. We have a story, and it starts in the book of Genesis."* Marty rehearses Preface → Introduction → Two Kingdoms → Exodus/Sinai → Leviticus → Numbers → Deuteronomy → Joshua → Judges/Ruth → the kings, so Naaman lands as one more beat of the single story.
- **Empire vs. Shalom / A Tale of Two Kingdoms (callback to ep. 18).** Named directly in the recap: *"We call the story A Tale of Two Kingdoms: Empire vs. Shalom... Empire, which is a world of force, of coercion, of right-handed power... and another kingdom, Shalom... invitation and wholeness and forgiveness."* Naaman arrives as Empire's great man — motorcade, gold, pomp — and is met by Shalom's refusal to be impressed.
- **A kingdom of priests / bless all nations (callback to Sinai).** The recap re-sounds the vocation: *"God just told them that they would be a kingdom of priests."* Naaman is the mission in miniature — a foreigner from Aram brought to Adonai through Israel's God and Israel's prophet.
- **The redemption cycle, not the sin cycle (callback to Judges/Ruth).** Brent: *"the redemption cycle, where Israel has problems. They make bad choices, but every single time God comes back around and brings them back into His arms."* Marty: *"a lot of people might call that the sin cycle, but we don't. We call it the redemption cycle."* The same patience frames God's grace to Naaman.
- **Remember where you came from (callback to Deuteronomy, ep. 31).** Recapped as God's refrain: *"I need you to remember... that you were aliens, and you were orphans, and you were widows... look out for those same groups of people."* The nameless alien slave girl is exactly the person Deuteronomy told Israel to see — and she becomes the hinge of the whole deliverance.
- **Elijah → Elisha / the first rabbi–disciple pair (callback to the previous episode).** *"the disciple of Elijah. The great Elijah in the last episode made a disciple in Elisha."* The continuity sets up who the "man of God in Samaria" turns out to be.
- **Good eye / bad eye (foreshadowing to Session 3).** Marty flags the king's robe-tearing as the bad eye and quotes Jesus ahead of time: *"how blessed are you when your eye is good... But if your eye is bad, it fills your body with darkness."*

## Terminology

- **ish gadol** (אִישׁ גָּדוֹל, "great/large man") — Naaman's status: *"He is a great man. Gadol means large or great. Ish is man... this weighty greatness. Like when Naaman walks into the room, you feel it."* Related in *idea* (not etymology) to **kavod**, heaviness → glory.
- **kavod** (כָּבוֹד, "heaviness/glory") — invoked as the conceptual cousin of *gadol*: *"we looked at kavod back in Exodus. To kavod, to heavy. Kavod can mean glory... this idea of glory and heaviness being connected."*
- **yada** (יָדַע, "to know" by experience) — Brent names Naaman's confession as *yada*: *"that's yada right there. He has experienced—"* Experiential knowing of Adonai, not mere intellectual agreement, from a former polytheist.
- **"Go in peace" (lech l'shalom)** — Elisha's closing word in place of a demand for a clean conversion: *"His big response is: You'll be okay. Go in peace... I think the assumption is: you'll figure it out."* Shalom-shaped grace sent into a complicated life.
- **gods were geographical** — the ancient-world assumption behind the dirt request: *"if I want to worship the God of Israel, I have to be standing in Israel... the god has a rule geographically."*
- **the "Boam Boys"** — recap shorthand for Rehoboam and Jeroboam and the split kingdom, credited to BEMA-OG listener JT Manning: *"It's a good throwback to our BEMA OG days there with JT Manning."*
- **two sources (Source A / Source B)** — the kings-history framework re-stated: *"Source A, which is Samuel and Kings... Source B, which is Chronicles. But they all tell the same story"* — Samuel–Kings "agenda-driven headlines," Chronicles "historical hindsight."

## Illustrations & Images

- **"Ken Griffey Jr. walked right behind you"** (Marty) — the definitive picture of an *ish gadol*: at a Reds game Marty felt the entryway "shift," assumed the podcast had made him famous, and learned Griffey had just passed behind him. *"That is a great example of the ish gadol... You could feel it. You could sense it."*
- **The motorcade / entourage** (Marty) — Naaman doesn't travel humbly: *"He's got his motorcade... the horses and the chariots... money and gifts... He's going to come rolling into town like he would in the ancient world, with pomp and circumstance."*
- **The nameless slave girl who "sees" Naaman** (Marty) — a traumatized captive who nonetheless perceives her master as a sufferer and points him to the man of God: *"All I know is that she sees Naaman as a guy who suffers with leprosy."*
- **The Jordan as a "dirty crick"** (Marty & Brent) — against grand rivers (the Snake, the Ohio, the Tigris/Euphrates), the Jordan is tiny; Brent: *"you could sit down and your chest would still be out of the water."* Marty has struggled on tours to find water "more than knee deep."
- **The servant sent to the door** (Marty) — Elisha won't perform for the great man: *"An ish gadol from Aram shows up with a motorcade... And Elisha sends a servant to the door. Doesn't even go to the door himself."*
- **"Fountain of Youth, baby" / skin "like a young boy"** (Brent & Marty) — the restored flesh after the seventh dip, the comic payoff of obeying the unglamorous instruction.
- **The country song — "if you don't stand for something, you'll fall for anything"** (Marty) — the "turn or burn" theology Elisha pointedly does *not* use, sung as a gag to mark the contrast with "Go in peace." Brent: *"I don't need the clip, Marty. We got it."*
- **The gray cubicle world** (Marty) — the pastoral application: most people *"live in a world of grays, not black and whites... a nuanced world... hard to know what it means to follow God."* Into that, "Go in peace."

## Discussion Questions

1. Elisha answers bad theology and latent idolatry with "Go in peace," not "turn or burn." Who in your life is "running hard after God" in a complicated situation where you've been tempted to demand a clean answer instead of a blessing?
2. Marty says Naaman "totally gets it and totally doesn't" — and that's allowed to stand. Where in your own faith might God be extending grace to parts of you that aren't theologically "finished" yet?
3. The king sees a cry for help and reads a threat (the "bad eye"). When has your own insecurity turned someone's need into a problem you only wanted to defend against?
4. Deliverance runs through a nameless, traumatized slave girl rather than the great man's gold. Who are the overlooked "slave girls" in your world whom God might be using — and are you positioned to listen to them?
5. Naaman wanted spectacle and got a dirty crick. Where do you resist God's quiet, unimpressive instructions because they don't match the grandeur you expected?
6. "It's not about the answer to the question. It's about the relationship you have with the God who's going to be with you." Where do you need to trade the pressure to resolve everything for the freedom of "go with God, it will be okay"?
7. In Session 2, Israel's own kings keep rejecting God — yet here a pagan Aramean comes to real *yada* of Adonai. What does it say about the God "who won't quit" that outsiders find Him in the very era the covenant partner is most unfaithful?

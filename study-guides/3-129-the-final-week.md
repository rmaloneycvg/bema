# BEMA 129: The Final Week — Study Guide

**Session 3 (Gospels) · Episode 129**
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings
**Primary texts:** Matthew 26; Matthew 27; Psalm 22:1

---

## Overview

This is a deliberately atypical episode. Rather than the usual read-a-few-verses-then-comment rhythm, Marty keeps a promise to cover "every verse of Matthew" by having Brent read the entirety of Matthew 26 and 27 aloud, with Marty's reflections placed between and after the chapters.

> **Marty:** "I made that promise that we were going to do every verse of Matthew. What we're going to do is we're going to read every verse of Matthew 26 and 27 today."

The episode functions as a threshold — it *sets the stage* for the final week of Jesus's earthly ministry rather than exegeting it. Marty names a roadmap for the episodes to follow (the plot to kill Jesus, the betrayer, the resurrection and Great Commission, then the capstone), and candidly confesses how hard this material is for him to teach.

> **Marty:** "Today, we set the stage for the final week of Jesus's earthly ministry."

**Where this sits in the one story:** Session 1 traced God's invitation to *partnership*; Session 2 traced the partner's *unfaithfulness* and God's refusal to quit on the relationship. Session 3 watches the king/Messiah step inside the story and *embody* it. In this episode the Messiah does not merely teach the way of the kingdom — he walks it into Gethsemane, the trial, and the cross, becoming the Passover lamb inside the very festival that remembers God's deliverance. The story God has been telling since the beginning is now being lived out in a body.

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

The episode's movement, in order:

1. **Opening banter and framing** — Marty announces the unusual format: read all of Matthew 26 and 27, with commentary in the middle and at the end.
2. **Brent reads Matthew 26** (75 verses) — the plot, the anointing at Bethany, Judas's bargain, the Passover meal, Gethsemane, the arrest, the trial before Caiaphas, Peter's denial.
3. **First Q&A exchange** — the post-Passover hymn; Marty's honest struggle to teach this week.
4. **Marty's "post" / personal reflection** — why he resists teaching the cross; grief at his grandfather's funeral weekend; "Were You There When They Crucified My Lord?"; a turn toward atonement.
5. **Atonement as a "bag of golf clubs"** — Scot McKnight's metaphor; the promise to treat atonement theory later in Session 4.
6. **Roadmap for the next episodes** — the plot/"who killed Jesus," the betrayer (Judas and Peter), resurrection and the Great Commission, then the capstone.
7. **Brent reads Matthew 27** (66 verses) — Judas's remorse and death, Pilate, Barabbas, the crucifixion, the cry from Psalm 22, the torn curtain, the burial, the sealed tomb.
8. **Closing suggestions** — read the parallel Gospel accounts (Mark, Luke, John) as a group to become intimate with the story.
9. **Closing Q&A** — the "Eli/Elijah" mishearing; the hyssop sponge; why Pilate releases the body to Joseph; the naming problem (many Jesuses, Marys, Josephs); Preparation Day and Passover timing.

**Coverage verification:** All nine movements are represented in the sections below. Scripture read: Matthew 26:1–75; Matthew 27:1–66; Psalm 22:1 (cited in Jesus's cry). Strays and smaller items are parked in **Other Talking Points** so nothing is lost.

## East/West Lens

Only the lenses the episode actually engages are tagged; each is cited.

- **Hebraic — story over systematic abstraction.** Marty resists reducing the cross to an academic exercise, insisting the account is first a *story* to be entered, not merely a doctrine to be dissected.
  > **Marty:** "I don't like to make one of the most sacred and significant acts of all human history merely an academic exercise... the Gospel accounts on the crucifixion caused me to want to write poetry and speak eloquently and not talk about history on a podcast."

- **Hebraic — meaning carried by language and sound.** The "Eli / Elijah" mishearing only works in the Hebrew/Aramaic soundscape of a dying man's cry, where *Eliyahu* ("Yahweh is God") blurs into the prophet's name.
  > **Marty:** "Remember, Elijah's name is Eliyahu... They're hearing the God part, and they're thinking he's crying out Elijah's name."

- **Hebraic — a God who joins us rather than stays aloof.** The cross is read as God entering human sorrow, not an abstract transaction viewed from a distance.
  > **Marty:** "There are moments when I feel as though God has joined us in all of our sorrow and it is so good to have his company."

- **Western (acknowledged and valued, not rejected).** Marty names that academic, scientific, and historical treatment has its place and that he has learned from it — the lens is held alongside, not against, the Hebraic.
  > **Marty:** "I don't think there's anything wrong with dealing with the cross from an academic perspective. I'm thankful for people that have done that, I've learned a ton from their studies."

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 3 arc — the king/Messiah embodying the story — and tied to a transcript citation.

1. **The Messiah embodies the Passover he celebrates.** Jesus eats the Passover meal, then becomes the Passover sacrifice. The story of deliverance God has told for centuries is now lived out in the king's own body on the right day of the festival.
   > **Marty:** "He's being sacrificed as a Passover lamb on Friday. Then he is being resurrected on the first Sunday to follow Passover, which is always First Fruits."
   *Arc link:* Session 1's partnership and Session 2's covenant-keeping God converge here — God does not abandon the unfaithful partner; the king steps into the role of the lamb to keep the relationship.

2. **The cross resists being reduced to data.** The most faithful first response to the passion is to be captivated and moved by it, not to master it. Marty models entering the story before analyzing it.
   > **Marty:** "Maybe it's that I still find myself captivated by its story and its testimony."

3. **Atonement is a toolbox, not a single hammer.** No one theory exhausts what the cross means; each has its proper use.
   > **Marty (summarizing Scot McKnight):** "Every theory has its place, but you need to know when to use it... You don't pull out your putter on the tee box, nor do you have your 7 iron on the green."
   *Arc link:* The king embodies a story rich enough that the church will spend centuries describing it from multiple angles — the meaning is bigger than any one frame.

4. **Familiarity with the whole story is the prerequisite for understanding it.** Before analyzing the plot, the betrayer, or the resurrection, the listener is told to read all four Gospel accounts and become intimate with the narrative.
   > **Marty:** "The more familiar we are with the stories, the better we're going to be able to interact with them... Maybe spend some time just becoming really intimate and familiar with the story."

5. **Even the mockery is drenched in the story's own symbols.** The crowd mishears a dying man as calling Elijah; a Roman soldier likely weaponizes hyssop — the very plant of Passover deliverance — into an instrument of humiliation. The world's cruelty unknowingly keeps quoting the story it is trying to end.
   > **Marty:** "Hyssop was used as toilet paper in their world... they're lifting it up to Jesus, mocking him, acting like they're trying to give him some relief."

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension states the problem, tags its type, cites the transcript, links the text, and gives the Hebraic resolution — or marks it "Open — dance with it."

1. **A teacher who struggles to teach the most important thing.** *(Unstated assumption / vocational tension.)* Marty, who thrives on critical thinking and lightheartedness, confesses he cannot engage this material the usual way.
   > **Marty:** "I don't like teaching about this last week of Jesus's life. As a teacher, I just don't like it. I'm not really sure, totally why."
   **Hebraic resolution:** The discomfort is itself appropriate reverence. The response he lands on is to *read the whole text and let it speak* rather than tame it with commentary — honoring the gravitas by stepping back. See [Matthew 26 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+26&version=NIV).

2. **"Why have you forsaken me?" — the cry of abandonment.** *(Theological / translation tension.)* Jesus quotes Psalm 22:1 from the cross, raising the question of God forsaking God.
   > Transcript (Matthew 27, read by Brent): "Eli, Eli, Lama Sabachthani," which means, "My God, My God. Why have you forsaken me?"
   **Open — dance with it.** The episode does not resolve the atonement question here; Marty explicitly defers it ("I don't want to talk today about atonement, we'll do that later in Session 4"). The Hebraic instinct is that quoting a psalm's opening line invokes the *whole* psalm, which turns toward vindication — but the episode leaves the weight of the cry intact rather than explaining it away. See [Psalm 22 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm+22&version=NIV) and [Matthew 27 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+27&version=NIV).

3. **"Eli" misheard as "Elijah."** *(Translation / sound-and-language tension.)* Bystanders think Jesus calls Elijah.
   > **Marty:** "If he's gasping and crying out, 'My God, my God'... They're hearing the God part, and they're thinking he's crying out Elijah's name."
   **Hebraic resolution:** The confusion is linguistically native — *Eliyahu* ("Yahweh is God") and the cry to *Eli* share the sound; a dying man's slurred cry and a messianic expectation of Elijah's return make the mishearing natural. See [Matthew 27 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+27&version=NIV).

4. **Is the hyssop sponge a *remez* to Elijah?** *(Logic / intertextual tension.)* Brent asks whether the sponge-on-hyssop points back to something Elijah did.
   > **Brent:** "This isn't some kind of a remez to something that Elijah did with a sponge?"
   > **Marty:** "No. That's a fantastic question. I don't think so, but boy I've been wrong about that before."
   **Open — dance with it.** Marty declines to force a connection but affirms the instinct to keep digging; the text is "always a great assumption, a place to dig in." See [Matthew 27 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+27&version=NIV).

5. **Why does Pilate hand the body to Joseph of Arimathea?** *(Historical / cultural tension.)* Crucified "terrorists" were normally thrown into shallow graves, not given honorable burial.
   > **Marty:** "In the eyes of the Romans, he's a terrorist... They just take these guys down, and then they just throw them into a shallow grave."
   **Hebraic/historical resolution (partial, speculative):** Marty reads Joseph as a wealthy, well-connected man — possibly with Sanhedrin/priestly ties and influence with Rome — so Pilate, who "doesn't care," simply lets him have the body. Marty flags this is speculation beyond the text. See [Matthew 27 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+27&version=NIV).

6. **When exactly did Passover fall this year?** *(Historical / calendar tension.)* The meal appears on Thursday but the sacrifice on Friday.
   > **Marty:** "Because Passover falls on Sabbath... they would have celebrated Passover on that year a day earlier, on Thursday night, but the actual Passover sacrifice would have still happened on Friday night, which is why you see Jesus doing the Passover meal on Thursday."
   **Hebraic resolution:** First-century practice shifted the meal when Passover coincided with Sabbath, letting the meal (Thursday) and the sacrifice (Friday) land on different days — which lets Jesus eat the meal and still be sacrificed "as a Passover lamb." See [Matthew 26 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+26&version=NIV).

## Terminology & Hebrew Words

- **Gethsemane (gat shemanim)** — "oil press," in the Kidron Valley, where Jesus prays in anguish.
  > Transcript (Hebrew Words Explained): "Gethsemane (gat shemanim) — 'oil press' in the Kidron Valley, where Jesus prays."
- **Eli / Eliyahu / "Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani"** — "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1); *Eliyahu* = "Yahweh is God," which is why a slurred cry is misheard as calling Elijah.
  > **Marty:** "Eliyahu means Yahweh is God. If he's gasping and crying out, 'My God, my God'... they're thinking he's crying out Elijah's name."
- **Hyssop** — the plant whose leaves were used as toilet paper in that world; harmonizing the Gospels, likely a Roman soldier's cruel mock-"drink."
  > **Marty:** "Hyssop was used as toilet paper in their world... what they've probably done here... is they've probably taken some used toilet paper, they've hid it in a sponge."
- **Preparation Day** — the day Passover is prepared and the sacrifice occurs.
  > **Marty:** "Preparation Day is the day where you prepare for the Passover... where everything about Passover is prepared."
- **First Fruits** — the Sunday following Passover, on which Marty places the resurrection.
  > **Marty:** "His resurrection falls on First Fruits on that following Sunday."
- **Crucifixion** — a Roman punishment reserved largely for zealots/insurgents, to make a public spectacle.
  > **Marty:** "Crucifixion is a sentence that was saved almost uniquely for the church of terrorism, insurgency. It was saved for zealots. You crucified zealots to make a spectacle."
- **Remez** — (used by Brent) a hint/allusion pointing to a deeper intertextual connection.
  > **Brent:** "This isn't some kind of a remez to something that Elijah did with a sponge?"
- **Atonement theories** — ransom/captive, Christus Victor, (penal) substitutionary, with outliers; to be treated in Session 4.
  > **Marty:** "They would have probably talked about ransom captive theory... Things like Christus Victor... Penal substitutionary atonement? That's the theory that most evangelicals are familiar with today."

## Illustrations & Stories

- **"Were You There When They Crucified My Lord?"** — the hymn that still undoes Marty, bound up with the grief of a weekend he never fully understood.
  > **Marty:** "To this day, hearing the hymn, Were You There When They Crucified My Lord? Will certainly bring me to my knees."
- **The grandfather's funeral he did not attend.** On a Good Friday / Easter weekend as a young man, Marty chose to stay at a Good Friday service instead, weeping uncontrollably "for reasons that I still don't really understand."
  > **Marty:** "Instead I was at Good Friday service all by myself weeping uncontrollably for reasons that I still don't really understand. I've never cried like that in all my life."
- **The bag of golf clubs (Scot McKnight).** Each atonement theory is a different club; wisdom is knowing which to use when.
  > **Marty:** "You don't pull out your putter on the tee box, nor do you have your 7 iron on the green."
- **Three "Life of Christ" classes in Bible college** — an entire semester devoted to just the final week, underscoring how much is here.
  > **Marty:** "We did an entire semester-long class on the final week of Jesus's life."
- **The homily that "went over like a bag of bricks."** Marty's self-deprecating memory of struggling to preach this material well.
  > **Marty:** "I wrote out a homily and that went over like a bag of bricks."

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty says he resists making the cross "merely an academic exercise," yet also values rigorous historical study of it. Where is the line for you between studying the passion and *entering* it? Can you do both at once?
2. Scot McKnight compares atonement theories to a bag of golf clubs — each with its proper use. Which "club" (ransom, Christus Victor, penal substitution, others) have you reached for most, and what might you be missing by only using one?
3. Marty frames this episode as *setting the stage* and urges groups to read the parallel accounts in Mark, Luke, and John before analyzing them. Try it: read one other Gospel's crucifixion narrative aloud. What stands out that Matthew leaves out — and how does that change what you see?
4. Jesus cries Psalm 22:1 from the cross. Read the whole of Psalm 22. How does knowing where the psalm *ends* shape how you hear its opening line on Jesus's lips? (Notice the episode deliberately leaves this open.)
5. The king who taught the way of the kingdom now becomes the Passover lamb inside the festival of deliverance. How does Jesus *embodying* the story — rather than only teaching it — reframe what you thought the final week was about, given Session 1's partnership and Session 2's unfaithful partner whom God refuses to abandon?

## Other Talking Points

- **The post-Passover hymn.** After the meal they sing a hymn (Matthew 26:30); Marty doesn't know the first-century liturgy and invites listeners to research it.
  > **Marty:** "It should be a post-Passover hymn. I have no idea... That is a wonderful question."
- **Brent's imagined "life psalm" moment.** Brent speculates Jesus might have sung a personal psalm the disciples recognized — "It'd make a great movie moment."
- **The naming problem.** Multiple Jesuses (even "Jesus Barabbas"), Marys, and Josephs — which is why "son of [father]" mattered more than the given name.
  > **Marty:** "Even more important than your name was, son of Joseph."
- **The torn temple curtain, earthquake, and opened tombs** (Matthew 27:51–53) are read in the text but not expounded in this stage-setting episode — flagged here so the detail isn't lost before later treatment.
- **John's volume of final-week material.** Brent notes John has "chapters and chapters of discourse" from this week, reinforcing the call to read across the Gospels.

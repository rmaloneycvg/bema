# The Gates of Hell

BEMA 119 · Session 3 (The Gospels)
**Source transcript:** `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/3-119-the-gates-of-hell.md`
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings
**Primary texts:** Matthew 16:13–28; Joshua 3:9–10; Deuteronomy 5:22–29

---

## Overview

Jesus marches his already-rattled disciples to the single most pagan place in the region — **Caesarea Philippi** (Banias/Panias), a cult center built over a cave-spring the Roman world literally called **the Gates of Hell**, home to the fertility worship of Baal and then Pan and the debauched festival of "Pandemonium." There, at the worst-of-the-worst, Jesus asks "Who do you say that I am?" Peter answers, "You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God," and Jesus declares that *on this rock* he will build his church and the gates of Hades will not overpower it. The episode reads that confession and that promise through a Jewish-rabbinic lens (the hosts invoke **sod**, **remez**, **Petros/Petra**, **Shimon/shema**), then watches Peter's highest moment collapse into "Get behind me, Satan" when he rebukes his rabbi.

**Where this sits in the one story:** Session 1 established the **partnership** — God choosing and shaping a people to be a blessing to all nations. Session 2 traced that partner's **unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit**. Session 3 shows **the King/Messiah embodying the story in person**. This episode advances the arc by relocating the whole mission from the "safe little corners" of Galilee into the heart of pagan chaos: the Messiah does not defend holy ground, he *invades* unholy ground with shalom. It is the Abrahamic blessing-to-the-nations made aggressively concrete — Jesus points at the literal Gates of Hell and says the Kingdom of God will advance *there*, on offense, and will not be overpowered.

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 3 arc — the Messiah embodying the one story — and backed by a transcript citation.

### 1. The Kingdom advances on offense, into the chaos — not defense, in the safe corners
The rabbinic reading of "on this rock… the gates of hell shall not prevail" is the heart of the episode. Jesus deliberately plants the church's defining promise at the literal Gates of Hell.
> **Marty:** "Jesus is saying, I'm not going to build my church in the safe little corners, tucked into the corners of the triangle in Galilee. I'm going to build my church right here- and this mess, this chaos, this paganism, is not going to prevail against what the Kingdom of God is going to do."

> **Marty:** "Jesus's movement in the world is an offensive strategy. It's not a defensive one. I have grown up with a faith that worked hard to play it safe… This message is telling me that Jesus is running into the chaos with shalom."

In the arc, this is the Abrahamic blessing-to-the-nations re-issued by the Messiah himself and aimed, pointedly, at the most pagan ground available.

### 2. The sod of Peter's confession is the *text*, not the deduction
Jesus says the revelation came "not by flesh and blood." Marty argues the mystery (**sod**) is not that Peter figured out Jesus is Messiah, but the loaded phrase Peter chose — "Son of the **living God**."
> **Marty:** "I think it's not the Messiah piece that is Sod, it's what Peter chooses to say… 'It's not the Messiah piece. I can deduce that with my logic. It's the reference to the Text, Peter.'"

This models the Hebraic conviction that Scripture speaks through allusion (remez): Peter's answer is powerful because it is pulling on Joshua 3 and Deuteronomy 5, whether or not Peter fully realized it.

### 3. A rabbi teaches by pointing at concrete things, never in the abstract
The whole "on this rock" argument rests on the Eastern-teacher principle that Jesus went to a *place* in order to point at a *thing*.
> **Marty:** "A rabbi is never going to talk about something that he can't [point to]… Eastern teachers are not abstract teachers, they don't talk about things in the abstract."

> **Marty:** "A rabbi is going to come to a region so that he can make a physical, concrete point, not an abstract point… in my mind, as a Jewish rabbi with Jewish disciples, Jesus is somewhere where He can point to the rock."

### 4. Peter's best day and worst day are the same day — and the cure is to listen
Minutes after the confession, Peter rebukes Jesus and is called "Satan" — the *opposer*. The rebuke is a rabbinic re-ordering: disciple, get back in line and listen.
> **Marty:** "Peter's best moment, his best day was coupled with his worst day. His rabbi just called him Satan. The opposer-not Satan the guy with red tights and a pitchfork-but, 'Get behind me, opposer.'"

> **Marty:** "Peter, you need to become less Peter right now and more Shimon. You need to become more of the one who listens, you need to step back and step in line."

The echo is Deuteronomy 5: "we will listen" to the voice of the living God. The Messiah's follower is formed by hearing, not by instructing the rabbi.

### 5. The call to take up the cross may be shouted *at the pagans*
Harmonizing the Synoptics, Mark/Luke place a "crowd" at this scene — which, at Caesarea Philippi, can only be the Pan worshipers. That reframes "deny yourself, take up your cross" as an invitation hurled into Pandemonium itself.
> **Marty:** "At some level, this has to be about the pagans, right?" … **Brent:** "The Pan worshipers."

> **Marty:** "He turns and he looks them in the eye and he says, 'Anyone who is ashamed of me, I will be ashamed of him before my father. Don't you be ashamed of me as I sit here and tell you that this is where we're going to build our church.'"

The Messiah embodies the story by offering the Kingdom to the very people the disciples most want to flee.

## East/West Lens

Only the categories the episode actually engages are tagged, each with a citation.

- **Words (abstraction vs. word-picture/story).** Jesus refuses abstraction and teaches by physical reference.
  > **Marty:** "Eastern teachers are not abstract teachers, they don't talk about things in the abstract… a rabbi is going to come to a region so that he can make a physical, concrete point."

- **Faith (intellectual proof vs. relational/experiential).** Peter's confession is received as revelation and testimony, not deduced doctrine.
  > **Marty:** "Only God could have given you that gift. You didn't deduce this. We didn't teach this to you around a campfire."

- **Community vs. individual.** The "church" promise and the "deny yourself" call are aimed outward — at a people, even a pagan crowd — not merely at a private believer.
  > **Marty:** "This has to be about the pagans… He's in the region of Caesarea Philippi."

- **Error & sin (wrong belief vs. wrong posture/behavior).** "Get behind me, Satan" is not a doctrinal correction but a repositioning of Peter's posture — step back, get in line, listen.
  > **Marty:** "Peter, you forget your place. I'm Rabbi, you are a disciple. You step in line, you get back behind me, Peter, and you get where you belong and you listen."

## Narrative Tensions

"Doesn't-make-sense" surface items, each with a plain-language problem, a classification tag, a cited quote, a verse link, and a resolution (Hebraic lens) or an honest "Open."

### 1. "On this rock" — which rock? Peter, the confession, or a cliff?
**Surface problem:** Centuries of debate treat "this rock" as either Peter or his confession; the plain text doesn't settle it.
**Tag:** `translation-disagreement`
> **Marty:** "He named him Petros, Peter, Rocky… but 'I will call you Petros and on this Petra'… remember Petra from the Book of Obadiah-on this bedrock, on this cliff face, I will build my church."
**Verse:** [Matthew 16:18 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+16%3A18&version=NIV)
**Hebraic resolution:** The Greek shift from **Petros** ("Rocky," a stone) to **Petra** (bedrock/cliff face) plus the Eastern-teacher principle (a rabbi points at concrete things) lets the hosts hold all three readings: Peter *and* the confession *and* the literal rock face of the Gates of Hell, which Jesus is physically pointing at.

### 2. Why would anyone guess Jesus was *Jeremiah*?
**Surface problem:** John the Baptist and Elijah are intuitive guesses; Jeremiah is odd.
**Tag:** `unstated-cultural-assumption`
> **Marty:** "Of all the people, why Jeremiah? I have a hunch. I wonder if Jesus says, 'Forgive your enemies'… His message is so pro Babylon… I wonder if Jesus… is being perceived by some, as being so pro-Roman, that they're like, 'Oh, he's Jeremiah.'"
**Verse:** [Matthew 16:14 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+16%3A14&version=NIV)
**Resolution (offered, tentative):** Jeremiah's "surrender to Babylon" message made him sound like a collaborator; a Jesus who preached "forgive your enemies" could sound dangerously pro-Roman, hence the association. Marty flags it as a hunch — **Open — dance with it.**

### 3. If Jesus can "deduce" Messiahship with logic, what exactly was revealed as sod?
**Surface problem:** Jesus calls the confession a God-given revelation, yet the Messiah claim seems like something Peter could reason to.
**Tag:** `logic-gap`
> **Marty:** "I've always struggled with that because I'm like, 'He wasn't able to deduce that Jesus was the Messiah. Like, what?' I think it's not the Messiah piece that is Sod, it's what Peter chooses to say."
**Verse:** [Matthew 16:16–17 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+16%3A16-17&version=NIV)
**Hebraic resolution:** The sod is not the proposition "you are Messiah" but Peter's **remez** — invoking "the living God," a phrase loaded from Joshua 3:10 and first from Deuteronomy 5. The revelation is the textual connection, which could only be given, not deduced.

### 4. In Matthew it reads like two separate conversations — confession, then passion prediction
**Surface problem:** Matthew's "Then he ordered them… From that time on Jesus began…" reads as a scene break, as if the cross teaching is a new, private episode with the disciples.
**Tag:** `contradiction` (inter-Gospel harmonization)
> **Marty:** "In Matthew's Gospel, it almost seems like these are different conversations… When you look at the other Gospels, I don't think we should read it that way… Mark and Luke make that more clear. It's more of a seamless conversation."
**Verse:** [Matthew 16:20–24 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+16%3A20-24&version=NIV); cf. [Mark 8:34 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+8%3A34&version=NIV); [Luke 9:23 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+9%3A23&version=NIV)
**Resolution:** Read as one seamless scene (per Mark/Luke's "crowd"), the "deny yourself, take up your cross" words are spoken into the pagan setting of Caesarea Philippi, not in a private aside — which reframes the whole teaching.

### 5. A disciple *rebuking* his rabbi
**Surface problem:** Peter telling Jesus what must not happen inverts the entire rabbi–disciple relationship.
**Tag:** `unstated-cultural-assumption`
> **Brent:** "It seems like a strange idea to tell your rabbi what to do." / **Marty:** "A disciple rebuking the rabbi? Peter, he's flying a little too high."
**Verse:** [Matthew 16:22–23 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+16%3A22-23&version=NIV)
**Hebraic resolution:** "Get behind me, Satan [opposer]" is a rabbinic re-ordering of roles: Simon the listener (*shema*) has forgotten his place; he must step back behind the rabbi and return to listening (Deuteronomy 5's "we will listen").

## Terminology

- **Caesarea Philippi / Banias (Panias)** — the pagan cult center at the cave-spring; named by Herod's son Philip ("Caesarea of Philip"). Panias (after Pan) → Banias in Roman mouths.
  > **Marty:** "He says, 'Well, I will call it Caesarea of Philip.' That's how we get Caesarea Philippi."
- **The Gates of Hell** — the site's secular/cultural name; where the underworld met this world, source of fertility.
  > **Marty:** "They called this the Gates of Hell. Literally, you have the water coming out of this mysterious water source… Literally the Gates of Hell."
- **Pandemonium** — the Pan festival; the origin of the English word.
  > **Marty:** "The festival was actually called Pandemonium. That's where we get that word of Pandemonium."
- **sod** — the fourth level of PaRDeS; the God-given mystery/revelation ("not by flesh and blood").
  > **Marty:** "Only God could have given you that gift. You didn't deduce this."
- **remez** — a hint/allusion to another text; "Son of the living God" as a pointer to Joshua 3 / Deuteronomy 5.
  > **Marty:** "I wonder which one Peter is thinking of as a remez when he says it."
- **Petros vs. Petra** — Peter is renamed *Petros* ("Rocky," a stone), but the church is built on *Petra* (bedrock/cliff face — the literal rock of the Gates of Hell).
  > **Marty:** "It's not, 'I will call you Petros and on this Petros,' but 'I will call you Petros and on this Petra.'"
- **Simon / Shimon / shema** — Simon means "one who listens" (from *shema*, "to hear"); Peter must return to being Shimon.
  > **Marty:** "Simon-which you hear shema, 'to hear.' Simon means 'somebody who listens.'"
- **chutzpah** — bold fire/nerve; why some guessed Jesus was Elijah.
  > **Brent:** "Jesus had more chutzpah."
- **Satan (the opposer)** — not a red-tights devil but "opposer"; a rabbinic rebuke repositioning the disciple.
  > **Marty:** "The opposer-not Satan the guy with red tights and a pitchfork-but, 'Get behind me, opposer.'"

## Discussion Questions

1. Jesus plants the church's defining promise at the "Gates of Hell" — the most pagan place around. Where are the "Caesarea Philippis" in your own context, and what would it look like to bring shalom *into* them rather than retreating to the "safe little corners"?
2. Marty contrasts an **offensive** faith with the **defensive**, "play it safe" faith he was handed. Which have you been formed by, and where is the line between courageous engagement and recklessness?
3. Peter's best day and worst day were the same day. How do we hold the reality that a moment of real revelation (sod) can be immediately followed by presuming to "rebuke the rabbi"? What does becoming "more Shimon" (the one who listens) look like for you?
4. If "deny yourself, take up your cross" was shouted *to the pagan crowd* at Pandemonium, how does that change the way you hear it — as an invitation to outsiders rather than a private word to insiders?
5. The hosts hold three readings of "the rock" (Peter, the confession, the literal cliff) without collapsing them into one. Where else might you be forcing a single "right answer" onto a text that is doing more than one thing at once?

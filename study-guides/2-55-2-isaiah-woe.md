# BEMA 55: 2 Isaiah — Woe — Study Guide

**Session:** 2 (The Prophets)
**Episode:** 55
**Hosts:** Brent Billings & Marty Solomon
**Primary Text:** Isaiah 13–39 ("2 Isaiah"), with ch. 12 as a possible hinge
**Prophetic image / word:** **Woe**

---

## Overview

This episode closes out the Assyrian period of the prophets by flying through the second voice of Isaiah (chapters 13–39). Where 1 Isaiah's image was the *vineyard* and its target was God's own people, 2 Isaiah's word is **woe** — a relentless litany of judgment pronounced over nation after nation, including Judah. Brent frames it at the top: "we wrap up the Assyrian period of history by looking at one of the most distressing prophets, the voice of Second Isaiah as he delivers numerous woes to the nations of the world, including God's people. Yet, even this is not void of hope."

Within Session 2's arc — Israel's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit — this episode widens the lens from the covenant partner to the whole world. The woes are not primarily about picking the wrong god; they are about how people treat one another. God is looking for a partner (anyone — pagan or covenant people) to help "put the world back together," and finding none, pronounces woe. The partnership vocation launched in Session 1 is now exposed as universally abandoned, yet "islands of hope" keep erupting through the judgment, because God will not let the story end in woe.

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

The episode is a guided "flyby" through 2 Isaiah's subtitles rather than a verse-by-verse study. Segments in order:

1. **Setup — "a lot of text."** Marty establishes the scope: Isaiah 13–39, four (or so) voices of Isaiah, ch. 12 as a possible hinge between 1 and 2 Isaiah. Advice: grab a Bible with good subtitles (NIV) and follow along.
2. **Isaiah 13 — prophecy against Babylon.** Reading of ch. 13; Brent's puzzle that Babylon hasn't conquered Judah yet; the "pieces, not an essay" reading principle introduced.
3. **The redundancy / dating puzzle.** Why Babylon is condemned "early"; the Medes (→ Persia) raised up against Babylon; possible foresight vs. later authorship.
4. **Isaiah 14:3–15 — the taunt against the king of Babylon.** The deliberate v.3 framing ("king of Babylon"); the "Morning Star / Lucifer" verses; the Lucifer-as-translation-artifact correction.
5. **The long litany of woes (chs. 13–23).** Flyby of the subtitles: Babylon, Assyria, Philistia, Moab, Damascus, Cush, Egypt, Egypt+Cush, Babylon again, Edom, Arabia — and God's people (ch. 22).
6. **Isaiah 24 — the capstone.** The LORD's devastation of the whole earth; the James Jordan / Mama Liz note on possible Sadducee connections.
7. **Isaiah 25 — abrupt island of hope.** The whiplash from 24's judgment into praise; the banquet "on this mountain," swallowing up death, wiping away tears — then Moab trampled in the manure.
8. **More woes return (chs. 28–31).** Woe to Ephraim/Judah (28), to David's city / Ariel (29), to the obstinate children who "go down to Egypt" (30), to those who rely on Egypt (31) — don't trust Empire.
9. **Isaiah 32 — short-lived hope.** "A king will reign in righteousness" (Session 1 desert imagery) followed immediately by warning to the complacent.
10. **Chapters 33–35.** Distress and help (33), judgment among the nations (34), "joy of the redeemed" (35).
11. **The redactor's bridge (chs. 36–39).** Poetry shifts to prose history (reads like 2 Kings), narrating Sennacherib; evidence of compilation / multiple voices before poetry resumes at ch. 40.
12. **Review.** The word is **woe**; recap of prophetic images Amos → 2 Isaiah; next episode is Jeremiah (the Babylonian prophets).

**Coverage verification:** All read/discussed passages are addressed above — Isaiah 13 (Babylon), 14:3–15 (king of Babylon / Morning Star), the 15–23 subtitle flyby, 24 (capstone), 25 (praise + Moab), 28–31 (returning woes), 32 (king + complacent women), 33–35, and 36–39 (prose bridge). Strays (Lucifer etymology, James Jordan/Sadducees) are parked in **Other Talking Points**.

## Key Lessons

### 1. The prophets are *compiled*, not continuous — read them as "prophetic tweets"
The single most important reading principle in the episode. Marty: "Do not think of Isaiah as one big long essay that Isaiah sat down and wrote. It's not how the prophets worked... These are little chunks, and bits and pieces... little poems that are compiled and put together." And later: "A collection of tweets, if you will. Prophetic tweets that have been arranged, and compiled together." This explains the jarring tonal shifts (woe → praise → woe) and apparent redundancies (Assyria then Damascus; Babylon condemned twice).

*Arc relevance:* The compiled, over-years nature of the woes shows God pursuing the partner relentlessly across time — not one speech but a sustained, repeated refusal to walk away even as the message is overwhelmingly judgment.

### 2. The woes are about **how people treat each other**, not merely idolatry (Source A / Source B)
Marty's mental image is the heart of the episode: "God looking out over the earth, and He's so frustrated because nobody cares about other people. Everybody is serving themself, including His own people... 'You don't care about other people, and you don't care about other people... Even my people don't care about others.'" And: "I don't know if God needs our help, but God says, 'I want your help... I want somebody to help me put the world back together. Nobody is doing it, and so, woe to you.'"

*Arc relevance:* This is the Session 1 partnership vocation (be a blessing, put the world back together) measured against reality — and found abandoned by everyone, covenant people included. God's response is woe, but woe aimed at restoration, not abandonment.

### 3. Translate honestly — "Lucifer" is a translation artifact, not a Satan backstory
Marty deliberately slows down to show the taunt of ch. 14 is explicitly (v.3) "against the king of Babylon," and the famous "Morning Star, son of the dawn" (14:12) is not originally about Satan. Brent confirms the research: "Lucifer is the Latin word for Morning Star that is found in this passage." Marty: "We've even created a name out of misapplying this passage for the devil... Be careful in how we deal with the Text. Make sure we're using it appropriately." The grandiose boasts were standard ancient royal self-exaltation (every nation had its sacred mountain — Olympus, Tyre, Babylon, Persia).

*Arc relevance:* Keeps the focus on the real indictment — human kings and human pride — rather than a cosmic sideshow, sharpening that the story's problem is the partner's (and the nations') unfaithfulness.

### 4. Hope keeps erupting — God refuses to let the story end in woe
Even in the darkest prophet, "this is not void of hope" (Brent). Isaiah 25 bursts into praise: a refuge for the poor, a feast "on this mountain" for *all peoples*, "he will swallow up death forever," "wipe away the tears from all faces." Chapters 26–27, 32, and 35 ("joy of the redeemed") keep the hope flickering.

*Arc relevance:* This is the clearest Session 2 signal of God's refusal to quit. The partner has failed universally, yet God still promises a king who reigns in righteousness and a banquet for all nations — the partnership project is wounded but not cancelled.

## East/West Lens

*(Only categories genuinely engaged by this episode are tagged; each is cited.)*

- **[WEST → EAST] Literature: single-essay vs. compiled collection.** A Western instinct reads Isaiah as one continuous, logically-transitioning argument; the Eastern/Hebraic reality is a compiled anthology of occasional pieces. Marty: "Do not think of Isaiah as one big long essay... These are little chunks... a collection of tweets... Prophetic tweets that have been arranged, and compiled together." Reading it the Western way produces the "whiplash" (Brent) that the Hebraic reading dissolves.

- **[WEST → EAST] Reading Scripture into our own theology vs. its own context.** The West imports a ready-made Satan-origin myth onto Isaiah 14; the Hebraic move asks what the text says in its own setting. Marty: "before we use some of these passages to create all this backstory, we want to make sure we're actually seeing them appropriately in context, especially when it's stated directly as it is in this passage."

- **[EAST] Religion as how you treat your neighbor, not merely which god you serve.** The Hebraic conscience measures faithfulness horizontally. Marty: the woes are because "nobody cares about other people... Even my people don't care about others" — if it were only about choosing the wrong god, the charge would simply be idolatry; instead it is pride, oppression, and neglect of the poor.

## Narrative Tensions

### Tension 1 — Babylon condemned before Babylon has conquered (chronology)
**Classification:** Time-jump / dating problem.
Brent: "I guess I'm confused because I thought they haven't been conquered by Babylon yet." Marty concedes: "They have not, but that's why I put this more towards the end... It's a little tricky to figure out how all these pieces go together. Some people would say this is evidence for a later authorship of 2 Isaiah."
**Resolution (Hebraic / honest-reading):** The compiled nature of the book means pieces aren't in strict chronological order, and Babylon was already a rising power "on the horizon." Whether foresight or later authorship, the "pieces not essay" principle absorbs the difficulty. **Partly open — dance with it:** Marty explicitly leaves the authorship/foresight question unsettled ("was that foresight by Isaiah? Was this a later authorship?").

### Tension 2 — Isaiah 14's "Morning Star": king of Babylon or Satan?
**Classification:** Translation / interpretation disagreement.
The passage is popularly read as Satan's fall. Marty: "Now it's about Satan all of a sudden. No, this is about the king of Babylon." Brent: "Oh well, now we've shifted gears, right?"
**Resolution (Hebraic):** The text itself frames the taunt (v.3) as "against the king of Babylon," and "Lucifer" is merely the Latin for the Hebrew "Morning Star." The grandiose claims were conventional royal boasting, not a demonology. Resolved in favor of the plain, contextual reading.

### Tension 3 — The whiplash between woe and praise (ch. 24 → 25; praise → Moab)
**Classification:** Tonal contradiction / logic gap.
Brent: "It's no longer poetry... you get whiplash here." And on Moab appearing mid-praise: "Even before you get to the next chapter, it's like Moab is swimming in manure."
**Resolution (Hebraic):** Because the book is compiled sound bites, abrupt transitions are expected, not errors. Marty: "it's not like it flows well. This is not like a great standup comedy routine where there's transitions. This is compiled bits and pieces." The hosts also imaginatively reconstruct a lost immediate context for Moab ("It must have been a bad week for Moab").

### Tension 4 — One Isaiah or many? The prose bridge of chs. 36–39
**Classification:** Authorship / unstated-assumption problem.
Marty: "Most of the scholars you're going to read are going to talk about the work of the redactor... 'I've got to bridge the gap between 35 and 40.'" Yet he keeps the door open: "this could still be Isaiah. He just flips into historian mode."
**Resolution — Open, dance with it:** Marty deliberately refuses to force a verdict: "Not that I have to be right because I could be wrong, but it's always fun to study the scriptures and be honest about the things that we're looking at there." The observable shift from poetry to 2-Kings-style prose is the point; the single-vs-multiple-author verdict stays open.

## Terminology

- **Woe** — the episode's word for 2 Isaiah. "W-O-E, that kind of woe," the prophetic cry of lament/judgment — explicitly *not* "whoa." Brent: "Not, 'Whoa there, horsey.'" Marty: "more like, 'Woe to you.'"
- **Prophetic tweets** — Marty's image for the compiled, bite-sized nature of the prophetic material: "A collection of tweets, if you will. Prophetic tweets that have been arranged, and compiled together."
- **Lucifer / Morning Star** — Brent: "Lucifer is the Latin word for Morning Star that is found in this passage" (Isaiah 14:12); "Morning Star, Lucifer... light-bringer."
- **Ariel** — Isaiah 29:1. Marty: "Ariel, which means lion," and (footnote) "The Hebrew for altar sounds like the Hebrew for Ariel," so it alludes to Jerusalem, the city of David.
- **Redactor** — the editor who compiled the voices of Isaiah into one book. Marty: "The redactor, the person doing the work to put it together, said, 'Man, I've got to bridge the gap between 35 and 40.'"
- **1 / 2 / 3 Isaiah** — the "multiple voices" framing. 1 Isaiah ≈ chs. 1–12, 2 Isaiah ≈ 13–39, with poetry resuming at ch. 40 for what the podcast will later call 3 Isaiah.

## Illustrations & Images

- **God's lament, "nobody cares about others."** Marty's extended image of God surveying the earth in frustration: "You don't care about other people, and you don't care about other people. You don't care about others, and you don't care about others. Even my people don't care about others. Nobody cares about others... and so, woe to you, and woe to you, and woe to you."
- **"Prophetic tweets."** The compiled-sound-bites image (see Terminology) used to explain the book's structure and its tonal whiplash.
- **"It must have been a bad week for Moab."** Brent and Marty imaginatively stage Isaiah mid-sermon: "in that very moment in his pause, somebody from Moab does something stupid," so the praise suddenly pivots to "Moab will be trampled in their land." A picture of the lost immediate context behind compiled pieces.
- **Standup comedy with no transitions.** Marty: "This is not like a great standup comedy routine where there's transitions" — the woe-to-praise jump isn't smooth because the material was never meant to flow continuously.
- **The sacred mountains of the nations.** To normalize the king of Babylon's boasts: "the Greeks had Mount Olympus. Tyre, Babylon, Persia, they all had their mountains that the gods resided on."

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty insists we read the prophets as "prophetic tweets" — compiled bits and pieces, not a continuous essay. How does that change the way you handle the jarring shifts from woe to praise and back? Where have you felt "whiplash" reading Scripture that this principle might explain?
2. The woes, by Marty's reading, are driven by *how people treat one another* ("nobody cares about others"), not simply by worshiping the wrong god. How does that reframe what "sin" and "judgment" mean in the prophets — and what it means to be God's partner in "putting the world back together"?
3. The hosts show that "Lucifer" is a Latin translation of "Morning Star" and that Isaiah 14 is explicitly about the king of Babylon. What's at stake — practically and theologically — in reading a passage "appropriately in context" rather than importing a familiar backstory onto it?
4. Even in the darkest prophet, "this is not void of hope" (Isaiah 25, 32, 35). In Session 2's arc of Israel's unfaithfulness, why do you think God keeps interrupting the woes with promises of a feast for all peoples and a king who reigns in righteousness? What does that reveal about God's refusal to quit on the partnership?
5. Marty keeps the single-vs-multiple-Isaiah question *open* ("I could be wrong... it's always fun to study the scriptures and be honest about the things that we're looking at"). What is gained by holding an interpretive question open rather than forcing a verdict?

## Recurring Through-lines

*(Motifs genuinely surfaced by THIS episode, each with a cited quote. Explicit motifs captured; implied ones noted as such. Registry file intentionally not consulted or modified.)*

- **Putting the world back together / partnership (explicit).** The core Session 1 vocation reappears as the measuring stick for the woes. Marty: "I want somebody to help me put the world back together. Nobody is doing it, and so, woe to you." *How it advances the story:* the partner's role is now shown universally abandoned, which is precisely why woe falls — yet God still wants a partner.

- **Religion as caring for others / the poor and needy (explicit).** The woes indict pride and neglect of neighbor. Isaiah 25 praises God as "a refuge for the poor, a refuge for the needy in their distress"; the charge against the nations and Judah is that "nobody cares about other people." *Advances:* ties the prophets' judgment directly to the horizontal, neighbor-love shape of covenant faithfulness.

- **Don't trust Empire (explicit).** A repeated Session 2 refrain surfaces in chs. 30–31: woe "to those who carry out plans that are not mine... who go down to Egypt," and "Woe to those who rely on Egypt." Marty: "don't put your trust in Empire. God's people, why are you doing this?" *Advances:* names a specific form of the partner's unfaithfulness — seeking security in imperial power instead of God.

- **God's refusal to quit / islands of hope (explicit).** Brent: this prophet is "not void of hope"; Isaiah 25's banquet "for all peoples," "he will swallow up death forever," and ch. 32's "king will reign in righteousness." *Advances:* the defining Session 2 counter-movement — judgment never gets the last word.

- **Read the Text honestly / in context (explicit).** The Lucifer correction and the open authorship question both model it. Marty: "it's always fun to study the scriptures and be honest about the things that we're looking at there." *Advances:* a methodological through-line of the whole series, applied here to resist a popular misreading.

- **A coming righteous king (implied → explicit via Session 1 link).** Isaiah 32's "a king will reign in righteousness" is explicitly tied to the Session 1 desert imagery; implied as a forward-pointing hope thread. *Advances:* keeps alive the promise of a faithful human partner-king amid the failure of actual kings.

# The Lord's Mouthpiece

BEMA 47 · Session 2
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/2-47-the-lord-s-mouthpiece.md`
Book: N/A (overview/orientation episode — introduces the prophets; no passage read verbatim)
Co-host: Brent Billings · Host: Marty Solomon

> **Session 2 overarching arc: the partner's (Israel's) unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** This is the on-ramp into the Prophets. Having spent Session 1 building the partnership — God rescuing a people out of empire, marrying them at Sinai, and commissioning them to "be the message" (a kingdom of priests) — the hosts now step back into the narrative to read the prophets, who speak into what happens *after* the partner proves unfaithful. Marty frames the whole prophetic era as a single movement: **"this whole period of history goes from warning to woe to hope."** That movement *is* this session's stage of the one story: the prophet shows up as God's **mouthpiece** — "a present-day message for a present-day audience in a present-day situation" (Marty) — to warn an unfaithful partner, to name the woe when the warning is ignored, and (crucially) to insist that "the story doesn't end with destruction and annihilation. It moves to hope and God continuing to make good on his promises" (Marty). Where Session 1 asked whether the partner would trust the story, Session 2 asks what God does when the partner *doesn't* — and the answer is that God keeps speaking, keeps sending, and keeps the covenant.

## Key Lessons

- **A prophet is a mouthpiece, not a crystal ball.** The single biggest reframe of the episode. Marty: *"A prophet is bringing a present-day message for a present-day audience in a present-day situation. They're not showing up to a present-day audience with some future message about what's to come later."* Amos is a prophet "because God has sent him to talk to the people of Israel today, in his day" — not because he forecasts centuries ahead. This resets how the whole session will read every prophetic book.
- **Read the prophets *contextually* — always ask when, where, and to whom.** Even though the genre is not narrative, "it is very contextual" (Marty): *"as we read each prophet, we want to remind ourselves: when are they writing? Where are they at? Who are they writing to?"* Marty cares about where a prophet's voice is meant to be *heard in the narrative*, deliberately setting aside the academic debates over date and authorship.
- **The prophets carry two agendas at once — Source A (idolatry/immorality) and Source B (justice/empire) — and both are true.** Marty inherited the prophets as mostly Source A ("they were worshiping the wrong gods and committing immorality") but found that *"it's far more in the prophets about justice"* — Source B dominates. His charge to listeners: don't pick. Brent: *"I guess both."* Marty: *"Both are correct. We don't have to pick."* This is the interpretive tension that will run through the entire session.
- **The prophetic era moves warning → woe → hope.** Marty's governing map: *"The prophets are going to warn God's people. When they don't heed the warning, there will be woe... but the story doesn't end with destruction and annihilation. It moves to hope and God continuing to make good on his promises."* The partner's unfaithfulness drives the first two movements; God's refusal to quit drives the third.
- **The prophets fit five historical lanes (Marty's framework).** Pre-Assyrian (all is well — *warning*), Assyrian (army at the door), Babylonian (laying waste — *woe*), Exile (a new address in Babylon), and Remnant (return/rebuild, or stay — *hope*). Placing each book in its lane is the organizing project for the rest of Session 2. Marty flags it as his *personal* presentation: "This is debated. Some scholars are going to move the prophets around."
- **Isaiah is more than one voice.** Nearly all scholarship hears at least two voices (chs. 1–39 and 40–66), most hear three, and Marty favors a four-voice reading because "Isaiah spans too great of a time period... It covers more than the life of Isaiah as a singular voice." This is new and jarring to many ("What is this nonbiblical nonsense?") — and the hosts hold it loosely: it's "a helpful handle, not the only way" (Brent's point about Marty's later, differently-framed Isaiah series).
- **The prophet is an *active partner*, not merely a passive messenger.** Citing Heschel, Marty raises the deeper point that "a prophet is not *just* a mouthpiece... The prophet has leaned in and experienced the very heart of God. The prophet is an active partner, not just a passive messenger." Even as God's partner proves unfaithful, God raises up *other* partners — the prophets — who share his heart (his *pathos*). The partnership motif of Session 1 does not die; it migrates into the prophet.

## East/West Lens

- **Word-and-message over prediction (Hebraic prophecy vs. Western fortune-telling).** The Western instinct is a "future teller... a prophetic crystal ball" (Brent's pre-BEMA assumption). The Hebraic category is a *spokesperson* delivering God's word into a concrete present. Marty: *"the prophet in the Hebrew scriptures is not a future teller... What the prophet is doing is the prophet is functioning as a mouthpiece."*
- **Contextual/embodied meaning over abstract, timeless proposition.** The prophets resist being read as a floating body of predictions; their meaning is bound to a situation. Marty: *"they are definitely writing their prophecy at a particular historical location."* Truth here is located, not decontextualized.
- **Knowing a prophet by *function in the story*, not by resolving modern authorship questions.** An Eastern, narrative-first posture: *"What interests me is where the voice of the prophet is supposed to be heard within the narrative... I'm not going to get lost in: was it written here or here?"* (Marty). The text's role in the storyline outranks the critical-date debate.
- **Prophecy as *pathos* — sharing God's heart — rather than detached delivery.** Via Heschel: the prophet "has leaned in and experienced the very heart of God." Knowledge of God is participatory and affective (Eastern relational knowing), not merely the relay of a sealed envelope.
- **Holding "both/and" instead of forcing "either/or" (Source A *and* Source B).** The hosts refuse the Western impulse to resolve the two agendas into one correct answer. Marty: *"we don't have to pick between the two... they're both correct and true."* A Hebraic tolerance for productive tension.

## Recurring Through-lines

- **Partnership → the prophet as God's new partner.** Session 1's spine (God seeking a partner who shares his heart) is explicitly deepened here: the prophet is "an active partner, not just a passive messenger" who has "experienced the very heart of God" (Marty, via Heschel). As the covenant *partner* (Israel) grows unfaithful, God raises up prophets as partners who still carry his heart — the arc's hinge from Session 1 into Session 2.
- **Trust the story / staying in the narrative.** Marty frames the entire move into the prophets as a return to the storyline: *"we're jumping back into the narrative... throughout this entire podcast journey, we've really been following the story and the narrative of God's people."* The through-line that opened the whole series ("trust the story") now governs how an unfaithful partner's history is read.
- **Empire (the tale of two kingdoms).** The Session 1 empire-vs-shalom motif reappears as the engine of the prophetic timeline: Assyria, Babylon, and Persia are the successive empires that lay waste and exile the partner. Marty: a king "falls into lust for Empire in Solomon," and the lanes are literally named for empires on the doorstep ("Assyria is going to be the first army that shows up on the doorstep").
- **Justice and the margins (Source B).** The justice through-line carried from Deuteronomy ("look for the foreigner and the orphan and the widow... because you know what it's like to be on the margins," Marty's review) is named as the dominant prophetic concern: *"It's far more in the prophets about justice."*
- **Idolatry / faithful vs. unfaithful worship (Source A).** The other half of the prophetic indictment — "make sure we are not idolatrous people... our failure to worship God correctly. We worship God incorrectly. And that's idolatry" (Marty) — the unfaithfulness motif that this session's arc foregrounds.
- **The redemption cycle (not a "sin cycle").** Marty's deliberate re-labeling from the Session 1 review carries forward: *"Some call it a sin cycle, but we don't. We call it a redemption cycle. And we just keep seeing God redeem us."* God's refusal to quit, stated as a pattern, is the hopeful counter-melody to the partner's failure.

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Cold open & the aim.** Brent: today we examine "the remaining books in Tanakh, most of which are prophets," trying to "understand prophetic history as a whole and the setting for each period of history."
2. **"A beautiful mess."** Marty names the dread many feel toward the prophets ("just a bunch of condemnation... a broken record") and reframes the session as *jumping back into the narrative* after the wisdom-literature detour (Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs).
3. **Genre caveat + book plug.** The prophets are *not* historical narrative but are intensely *contextual*; Marty plugs his book *Asking Better Questions of the Bible* (chapters on history and on the prophets) as a parallel journey.
4. **Rapid Session-1 review (Genesis → Joshua).** Genesis 1 (the story is good) → the preface (1–11) → the family-of-God introduction (12–50: Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph) → Exodus/Passover rescue from empire → the Sinai marriage → the Tabernacle "honeymoon suite" (a retelling of Genesis 1–3) → Leviticus priesthood → Numbers wilderness → Deuteronomy ("don't forget where you came from," the margins) → Joshua at "the crossroads of the earth" and the redemption cycle (Ruth/Boaz as faithfulness).
5. **One story, two sources.** Review of the monarchy period: Source A = Samuel & Kings (real-time commentary on idolatry/immorality); Source B = Chronicles (documentary, wider retrospective). Saul (donkey herder from Benjamin), David (shepherd from Judah), Solomon (lust for empire), the kingdom split into Israel and Judah.
6. **The two sources set up the prophets.** The prophets "engage in the same conversation" — some feel Source A, some Source B. Marty's confession: he was taught Source A (idolatry) but found Source B (justice) dominates; both are true, don't pick. Brent's reframing question: when Chronicles was assembled, "what prophets did they look to to bolster their case?"
7. **"What is a prophet?"** Brent's old assumption: a future-teller with a crystal ball. Marty's correction: a **mouthpiece** bringing a present-day message to a present-day audience — Amos as the test case.
8. **Heschel's deeper caveat.** *The Prophets* (the ~900-page tome): a prophet is *not* a future-teller and *not merely* a passive mouthpiece but an *active partner* who shares God's heart (*pathos*); the short, dense *The Sabbath* also noted.
9. **The driving questions.** When / where / to whom — and specifically "where the voice of the prophet is supposed to be heard within the narrative," setting aside date-and-authorship debates (though a few will be poked at — e.g., Daniel — later).
10. **The empty chart (five periods).** Marty introduces the show-notes diagram: an initially blank table of the historic periods into which the prophets will be slotted. A personal, debated framework.
11. **Period 1 — Pre-Assyrian.** Everything's fine ("Stock market is great"); no army on the doorstep. A word of *warning*.
12. **Period 2 — Assyrian.** Assyria lays waste to the northern kingdom and is stopped short of Judah; "Assyrian presence in power." (Some call it the "pre-Babylonian" period for the south.)
13. **Period 3 — Babylonian.** Babylon lays waste to Assyria, Israel, and Judah over a long span — "an entire lifetime of, say, a guy by the name of Jeremiah."
14. **Period 4 — Exile.** After the destruction: "You no longer live in Judah. You now live in Babylon," having children in another empire.
15. **Period 5 — Remnant.** Return/rebuild Jerusalem or stay in Persia; prophetic voices plus histories. A word of *hope*.
16. **The filled chart + the Isaiah surprise.** The prophets slotted in: pre-Assyrian — Amos, Hosea (Israel), Micah, 1 Isaiah (Judah); Assyrian — Jonah, Nahum, Zephaniah, 2 Isaiah; Babylonian — Jeremiah, Lamentations, Habakkuk, Obadiah, Joel; Exile — Ezekiel, Daniel, Job, 3 Isaiah; Remnant — 4 Isaiah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi, plus Esther and Ezra–Nehemiah. The multi-voice Isaiah explained (two/three/four voices; "Deutero-" and "Trito-Isaiah").
17. **The movement named.** "This whole period of history goes from warning to woe to hope" — the shape of the entire session.
18. **Closing reassurance + reading recs.** "This is brand new to me... Totally understand." *Out of Babylon* by Walter Brueggemann recommended (read it later in the session); a note that Marty's *later* Isaiah series uses a different framework — hold these handles loosely. Brent: don't feel bad, we'll go book by book.

## Terminology

- **prophet / "mouthpiece"** — in the Hebrew scriptures, one who brings a present-day message from God to a present-day audience, *not* primarily a future-teller. Marty: *"A prophet is bringing a present-day message for a present-day audience in a present-day situation."*
- **pathos (the prophet as active partner)** — Heschel's point that the prophet does not merely relay a message but has "leaned in and experienced the very heart of God... an active partner, not just a passive messenger" (Marty).
- **Source A / Source B** — the two agendas carried into the prophets: **Source A** = Samuel & Kings, "real-time commentary on idolatry and immorality" (Brent); **Source B** = Chronicles, "a documentary perspective, looking back... with a wider idea of what was going on" (Brent). Marty: the prophets "engage in the same conversation."
- **Tanakh / Nevi'im / Ketuvim** — the Hebrew canon; the prophets occupy the *Nevi'im* ("prophets") section. The histories (Esther, Ezra–Nehemiah) technically belong to the Ketuvim but are placed in the Remnant period for the storyline. Marty: "They don't fall in the Nevi'im category of the Tanakh. They're stories of history, but they're going to show up here too."
- **the five periods (Marty's framework)** — (1) **Pre-Assyrian** (all is well; *warning*): Amos, Hosea (Israel); Micah, 1 Isaiah (Judah). (2) **Assyrian** (army at the door): Jonah, Nahum; Zephaniah, 2 Isaiah (Judah still warned). (3) **Babylonian** (laying waste; *woe*): Jeremiah, Lamentations, Habakkuk, Obadiah, Joel. (4) **Exile** (new address in Babylon): Ezekiel, Daniel, Job, 3 Isaiah. (5) **Remnant** (return/rebuild or stay in Persia; *hope*): 4 Isaiah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi, plus Esther and Ezra–Nehemiah. Marty: "this whole period of history goes from warning to woe to hope."
- **warning → woe → hope** — the shape of the prophetic era: warnings before the armies, woe during destruction, and hope in return/restoration. Marty: *"the story doesn't end with destruction and annihilation. It moves to hope and God continuing to make good on his promises."*
- **Deutero-Isaiah / Trito-Isaiah** — scholarly labels for the second (chs. 40–66) and third voices heard in Isaiah; Marty favors a four-voice reading. Marty: *"You'll hear people talk about Deutero-Isaiah. You'll hear people talk about Trito-Isaiah."*
- **redemption cycle (not "sin cycle")** — the recurring pattern of failure and rescue, deliberately named for God's redeeming action rather than human sin. Marty: *"Some call it a sin cycle, but we don't. We call it a redemption cycle."*
- **Hebrew name variants** — the hosts note the Hebrew forms behind English names: *Zephanyahu* (Zephaniah), *Nachum* (Nahum), *Yermeyahu* (Jeremiah), *Ovadjah* (Obadiah). Marty: "Zephanyahu — we say Zephaniah."

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty's reframe is that a prophet is a **mouthpiece** with "a present-day message for a present-day audience," not a crystal-ball future-teller. How does reading the prophets as *speaking to their own moment* (rather than predicting ours) change the way you'd approach a book like Amos or Isaiah?
2. The hosts refuse to pick between **Source A** (idolatry/immorality) and **Source B** (justice/empire), insisting "both are correct." Which agenda were *you* taught to hear in the prophets — and what might you be missing by leaning on only one?
3. The prophetic era moves **warning → woe → hope**. This session's arc is the partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit. Where in your own life or community can you name a season of "warning" that went unheeded into "woe" — and what would it look like to trust that the story "doesn't end with destruction," but "moves to hope"?
4. Heschel says the prophet is more than a passive messenger — "an active partner" who has "experienced the very heart of God." If sharing God's *pathos* (his heart) is what makes a prophet, what injustice or sorrow do you sense God's heart stirring about, and what would it cost you to speak into it *now*?
5. Marty holds his five-period framework and his four-voice Isaiah *loosely* — "you can disagree with this. It's okay." Where are you tempted to treat a particular reading of Scripture as the *only* way, and what might it free up to hold a strong framework with an open hand?
6. Marty insists on calling it a **redemption cycle**, not a "sin cycle" — naming the pattern for God's rescuing action rather than human failure. How would re-naming a repeated struggle in your own story around God's faithfulness (instead of your failure) change how you live inside it?

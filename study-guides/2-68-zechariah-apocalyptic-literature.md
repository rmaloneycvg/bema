# BEMA 68 — Zechariah: Apocalyptic Literature — Study Guide

**Session:** 2 (The Prophets)
**Episode:** 68 · "Zechariah — Apocalyptic Literature"
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings
**Primary text:** [Zechariah 9:9–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah+9%3A9-10&version=NIV) (the king on a donkey), with the broader apocalyptic visions of [Zechariah](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah&version=NIV)

---

## Where This Sits in the One Story

Session 1 built the picture of **partnership**: God rescues a people out of Egypt and invites them to become a kingdom of priests who join his restoration project. Session 2 turns to the **Prophets**, and the arc of this stage is the covenant partner's (Israel's) **unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Zechariah is a **remnant-period** prophet — a contemporary of Haggai — speaking to the people who have come *back* from exile and are now trying to rebuild under Persian rule.

This is a crucial turn in the arc. The partner's unfaithfulness has already run its full course — warning, woe, exile — and yet God has not quit. The remnant has returned, but the return is not the happy ending they hoped for: the glory of the good old days is gone, and they sit under the control of an empire that is not their own. The temptation now is a *new* form of unfaithfulness — not idolatry this time, but despair, impatience, and the urge to force God's hand through revolution. Zechariah's whole burden answers that temptation: **persevere obediently under empire, and your king will come.**

Relative to Session 1's partnership, this episode shows God refusing to quit by *re-casting the partner's hope*. The deliverance will not come through armies and military might but through God's providence — a king who rises through **humility**, riding on a donkey. That is the same unexpected pattern the story has used before (David, the suffering servant, Micah's Bethlehem), and it sets the stage for Session 3's rebuilding and, eventually, for Jesus. The partner's job in this stage is simply to stay faithful on a long, slow path that leads *through* Persia.

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **Introducing Zechariah, Haggai's contemporary.** Dating puts Haggai and Zechariah as toddlers when the first waves of returnees reached Jerusalem; they grew up in the mess of the rebuild, were likely schooled together (a "prophet college"), and may even have been friends.
2. **Short ministry vs. long ministry.** Haggai's ministry was months; Zechariah's spans decades of *ongoing* encouragement — because the rebuild is a long, hard, slow season under Persia, not a quick fix.
3. **What apocalyptic literature is (and isn't).** Deliberately repeating the Ezekiel definition: apocalyptic uses symbols and images to convey hope to their present day — *not* a map of the end times or a blueprint for the future.
4. **Zechariah's burden: persevere under empire.** The prophecy is to the returned remnant, calling them to endure Persian rule; typical apocalyptic shape — images encouraging faithfulness, then a closing assurance that a new kingdom/ruler is coming.
5. **The king on a donkey (Zechariah 9:9–10).** Brent reads the famous passage; Marty notes it is quoted every Palm Sunday for the play on Jesus' triumphal entry — but Zechariah's *original* intent was to tell the remnant to live obediently within Persia.
6. **Deliverance through humility, not might.** The rescue comes through God's providence, not military revolution; the king rises through humility — a theme the hosts trace to David, the suffering servant of 3 Isaiah, and Micah's Bethlehem Ephrathah.
7. **Reading the whole passage, not half.** Brent and Marty note our habit of cherry-picking the first half and skipping the promise that God will destroy the weapons of war — chariots, warhorses, the battle bow — if his people walk obediently.
8. **Zechariah → Revelation.** Nearly all of Zechariah's apocalyptic images reappear in Revelation; the hosts run through a list (myrtle trees/lampstands, horns/trumpets, measuring line/rod, clean garments/white robes, scrolls, woman in a basket/on a beast, four horses and chariots).
9. **Why read the whole prophets.** Marty's aim is not to explain whole books but to hand listeners the tools and context to go read the prophets themselves; the podcast "turns the lights on."
10. **Session 3 preview.** God does big things for this remnant that set the stage for Jesus; the Judaism that emerges after Babylon will look nothing like the Judaism before it — groundbreaking stuff.

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 2 arc — the covenant partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **God refuses to quit by sustaining the partner through a long, slow rebuild.** The return from exile is not the finish line; it is the start of a decades-long season of struggle under Persia. God's refusal to quit here looks like *ongoing* encouragement — Zechariah's whole long ministry is given to keeping a weary remnant on the path.
   - *Marty:* "Because this is going to be a long season for them. This is not going to be a short, like, 'Oh, we need to build a temple, and then everything's okay.'... it's going to be on God's timetable. That often is full of a lot of waiting and a lot of perseverance and a lot of struggle."
   - *Marty:* "Zechariah gives consistent images that are meant to inspire the people, to push forward into the future that God has for them."

2. **The partner's faithfulness in this stage means obedience *within* empire, not revolt against it.** The temptation of the remnant is to force deliverance through military revolution. Zechariah redirects that impulse: stay obedient under Persia and let God provide a different kind of rescue.
   - *Marty:* "His original intent was insisting that God's people would find a way to live obediently within empire. Zechariah's message is, if you can persevere right now in Persia, then eventually your king will come to you riding on a donkey."
   - *Marty:* "This isn't going to be a big military revolution that they're going to undergo, but if they can walk obediently, God's providence is going to provide a different kind of revolution."

3. **God's deliverance comes through humility and unexpected beginnings — the story's consistent pattern.** The partner keeps expecting rescue by might; God keeps delivering through the lowly and overlooked. Recognizing that pattern is itself an act of faithfulness in a stage tempted to despair of a quiet God.
   - *Marty:* "their King's going to rise to power through humility—which I hope that that would sound familiar to us. The prophecy of a king rising through humility."
   - *Marty:* "Your king is going to come from very humble beginnings, from places that you wouldn't even expect. A king riding on a donkey is not the glorious coming that you think of when you think of a great king coming in power."

4. **Obedience is tied to God dismantling the machinery of oppression.** The half of the passage we skip is the promise that if the partner walks obediently, God will *destroy* the weapons of war. God's refusal to quit includes a commitment to unmake the chariots and warhorses that have ground his people down.
   - *Marty:* "If God's people are going to walk in obedience, if they'll walk in obedience, even in the midst of Persia, God's going to do something in the world that will destroy the weapons of war and the chariots of oppression."

5. **Apocalyptic hope speaks to the present, not to a crystal ball.** For a remnant tempted to despair, Zechariah's images are not predictions to decode but hope delivered *now* — the arc's "God's refusal to quit" sounds, in the rebuild, like a present assurance that the king is on his way.
   - *Marty:* "Apocalyptic literature is not trying to map out visions of the end times or lay out blueprints for the future. That is simply not how apocalyptic literature functions."
   - *Marty:* "Zechariah is quite insistent that if God's people will persevere, their future king will come to them, but for now they must walk obediently the path that lies before them."

## East/West Lens

Only categories genuinely engaged by this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Reading a passage in its original context rather than as a proof text (East).** The episode explicitly resists the Western habit of lifting Zechariah 9 out of its remnant setting to serve a familiar Palm Sunday reading, and insists on recovering what Zechariah *originally* meant.
  - *Marty:* "We love to cherry pick passages from Zechariah that fit our needs for a good proof text, but we rarely find these passages used in context."

- **Apocalyptic as present hope, not future prediction (East).** The episode recovers the ancient Eastern function of apocalyptic — symbols conveying present-day hope — against the Western impulse to treat it as a timetable or blueprint.
  - *Marty:* "apocalyptic literature utilizes symbols and images to convey hope to their present day."

- **Reading intertextually across the whole story (East).** Meaning emerges by hearing the "humble king" theme echo across David, the suffering servant, and Micah, and by hearing Zechariah's images resound forward into Revelation — rather than reading the book in isolation.
  - *Marty:* "This is a common theme in the prophets. Your rescue, your deliverance, your king is not going to come to you through loud trumpets and military might."
  - *Marty:* "Everything in Revelation has already been said centuries before. Maybe it would help us if we became familiar with the Hebrew Scriptures that John is quoting."

- **Deliverance through humility rather than power (East).** The episode frames God's chosen mode of rescue as the lowly and unexpected — a king on a donkey — over the Western-style expectation of a great king arriving in military glory.
  - *Marty:* "A king riding on a donkey is not the glorious coming that you think of when you think of a great king coming in power."

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension states the problem, tags its type, cites the transcript, links the passage, and gives a Hebraic resolution (or marks it open).

1. **Zechariah 9:9 is quoted every Palm Sunday for Jesus — but what did it *mean* when Zechariah first spoke it?**
   - *Type:* Interpretive tension (proof text vs. original context).
   - *Problem:* The passage is so bound to the triumphal entry that its original function — a word to the Persian-era remnant — is routinely overwritten.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "there's an obvious play in Jesus's triumphal entry on Zechariah 9. However, what we often fail to remind ourselves or understand sometimes is that Zechariah's original intent... was insisting that God's people would find a way to live obediently within empire."
   - *Passage:* [Zechariah 9:9–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah+9%3A9-10&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* Both readings can stand without collapsing one into the other. Zechariah's original word to the remnant — persevere under Persia and your humble king will come — is the soil out of which the Palm Sunday "play" grows. Honoring the original context does not cancel the later echo; it makes the echo intelligible.

2. **How can a people under imperial rule be told to *wait* rather than fight for deliverance?**
   - *Type:* Unstated cultural assumption (deliverance = military revolution).
   - *Problem:* The natural expectation is that rescue from empire comes through armies and might; being told to stay obedient under Persia can feel like surrender.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "This rescue wouldn't come through armies and military might, but through God's divine providence. This isn't going to be a big military revolution."
   - *Passage:* [Zechariah 9:9–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah+9%3A9-10&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The assumption that deliverance must be military is precisely what the passage overturns. The "different kind of revolution" is God's providence working through humility, and the promise that God himself will break the chariots, warhorses, and battle bow relocates the fight from the people's hands to God's.

3. **Why do we hear "your king comes… victorious" and skip the broken weapons that come with it?**
   - *Type:* Logic/selective-reading gap.
   - *Problem:* The passage is habitually halved — the glorious king is kept, the costly promise (that obedience is tied to God destroying the instruments of war) is dropped.
   - *Cited:* *Brent:* "It's really easy to read half of this passage and really get that picture." / *Marty:* "Yes, we always like to be really selective in the things we hear in these passages."
   - *Passage:* [Zechariah 9:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah+9%3A10&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* Read whole, the passage ties the king's coming to a world where "the weapons of war and the chariots of oppression" are destroyed — but conditioned on the partner walking in obedience. The king and the broken bow belong together; keeping only the first half turns a demanding promise into a comfortable slogan.

4. **If Revelation simply reuses Zechariah's images, why do we treat Revelation as hopelessly obscure?**
   - *Type:* Genre/interpretive difficulty.
   - *Problem:* Revelation is approached as uniquely vague and complex, as though its imagery were invented from nothing.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "we stand around and wring our hands and moan about how the book of Revelation is so vague and complex and hard to understand. Everything in Revelation has already been said centuries before."
   - *Passage:* [Revelation](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Revelation&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The obscurity is largely self-inflicted unfamiliarity. John is quoting the Tanakh — and Zechariah especially — so becoming fluent in those source images (lampstands, horns, measuring rod, white robes, scrolls, horses and chariots) is the way in. The full working-out is deliberately deferred to Session 4, so the detail is held **open — dance with it** for now.

## Terminology

- **apocalyptic literature** — writing that "utilizes symbols and images to convey hope to their present day"; explicitly *not* a map of the end times or a blueprint for the future. (The definition is deliberately carried over from the Ezekiel episode.)
  - *Marty:* "apocalyptic literature utilizes symbols and images to convey hope to their present day."
- **remnant** — the returned community rebuilding Jerusalem/the temple under Persian rule; the audience Zechariah addresses.
  - *Marty:* "Zechariah is a prophecy about the remnant that has returned and the need for them to persevere in the face of Imperial rule."
- **"prophet college"** — Marty's image for the schooling of prophets; prophets were trained, not spontaneously made, which is why Haggai and Zechariah may have known each other.
  - *Marty:* "There's training. Think of it like a Bible college, a prophet college. You have training and it's very, very possible that Haggai and Zechariah may have been trained together."
- **triumphal entry** — Jesus' Palm Sunday entrance on a donkey, the "obvious play" on Zechariah 9:9 the hosts flag as the passage's most familiar New Testament use.
  - *Marty:* "there's an obvious play in Jesus's triumphal entry on Zechariah 9."
- **"a different kind of revolution"** — Marty's phrase for God's providential, humility-driven mode of deliverance, set against a military revolution.
  - *Marty:* "if they can walk obediently, God's providence is going to provide a different kind of revolution."

## Illustrations & Images

- **The king on a donkey.** The governing image of the episode — a king who arrives "righteous and victorious, lowly and riding on a donkey," rising to power through humility rather than military glory.
  - *Marty:* "A king riding on a donkey is not the glorious coming that you think of when you think of a great king coming in power."
- **Haggai and Zechariah as boys in the rubble.** The human picture of two toddlers who grew up in the mess of the rebuild and may have trained together and been friends.
  - *Marty:* "these two boys grew up in the mess and the struggle that was the remnant as their families tried to rebuild. It's really possible these guys might've even known each other, been really good friends."
- **The Zechariah → Revelation image list.** A paired catalog of apocalyptic pictures that migrate from Zechariah into Revelation, used to demystify Revelation.
  - *Marty:* "Zechariah's prophecy starts with a man walking among the myrtle trees. Revelation starts the man walking among the... Lampstands."
  - *Brent (on the four horses/chariots):* "On a beast." / *Marty:* "John and Revelation is pulling an image from Zechariah centuries before, that utilizes the same picture of horses drawing chariots."
- **"Turning the lights on."** Marty's image for what the podcast does — not explaining the whole book, but giving just enough context that a listener's own reading of the prophets suddenly illuminates.
  - *Marty:* "if we have a discussion in the podcast, it will simply turn the lights on."
- **Reading your Bible on the treadmill / car dashboard.** A running comedic aside about where listeners have Zechariah open as they listen.
  - *Marty:* "I'm sure on the treadmill that you're running on, you've got your Bible open to Zechariah there in the gym." / *Brent:* "Hopefully not like on the car dashboard if you're driving..."

## Discussion Questions

1. Zechariah tells a weary remnant that deliverance will come on *God's* timetable, "full of a lot of waiting and a lot of perseverance and a lot of struggle." Where in your life are you being asked to stay faithful on a long, slow path rather than force a quick resolution?
2. The remnant wanted rescue through military might; God offered "a different kind of revolution" through humility. Where do you instinctively look for power to fix things, and what would it mean to trust deliverance that comes "from places you wouldn't even expect"?
3. Marty says we love to read "half of this passage" — the victorious king — and skip the broken chariots and the obedience they're tied to. What demanding half of a familiar promise do you tend to leave out?
4. Apocalyptic literature "conveys hope to their present day" rather than mapping the future. How does reading Zechariah (or Revelation) this way change what you're listening for in it?
5. The whole episode insists that the images of Revelation "have already been said centuries before" in the Hebrew Scriptures. What would it look like for you to become more familiar with the Scriptures the New Testament is quoting?
6. Living "obediently within empire" was the remnant's calling. What does faithful obedience look like for you inside systems and structures you did not choose and cannot overthrow?

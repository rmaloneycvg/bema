## Overview

This is a structural/overview episode that sets up the kingdom era. Before diving into the stories of Saul, David, Solomon, and the divided monarchy, Marty pauses to explain *why* a straight-through Bible reader gets disoriented here: the same period of history is told **twice**, by two different sources. **Source A — Samuel/Kings** records the era in near-real-time as "agenda-driven headlines" from "Israel's perspective." **Source B — Chronicles** retells the identical period roughly 400 years later, during the exile, from "Judah's perspective," like a documentary looking back with hindsight. Both are true; they are simply shaped differently to make different points.

The engine underneath the whole conversation is the TaNaKh. Marty shows that the Hebrew Scriptures contain the same books as the Christian Old Testament but in a different order — and that Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings sit in the **Nevi'im (the Prophets)**, not among the histories. That placement is the thesis of the episode: for the Hebrew mind, **history is prophetic**. Samuel/Kings isn't neutral reportage; it is a message meant to wake God's people up in their present.

The guide tracks how the episode advances the one story. Session 1 established the **partnership**: God rescued a people out of Empire, married them at Sinai, and planted them at the crossroads of the earth to bless all nations. Session 2 (the Prophets) now watches that partner — Israel — move into the monarchy and chronic unfaithfulness, while God refuses to quit. BEMA 40 hands the reader the interpretive tools (two sources, prophetic history) needed to read that stage honestly rather than as flat, contradictory record.

## East / West Lens

Only the interpretive categories the hosts actually engage are tagged below; each is cited.

- **History as prophecy vs. history as neutral record (Eastern vs. Western historiography)** — The West treats bias in history-writing as a defect; the ancient world wrote history *in order to teach*. Marty: *"in the Western world... we do our best... to record simply what happened without bias and agenda. But the ancient world of the Bible did not do that... if you're going to take the time to do that, you are going to take the opportunity to make sure you're learning from the history you're recording."*
- **Prophet reframed (East) — present-oriented messenger, not future-teller** — Marty: *"Prophets aren't primarily people that tell the future... They're trying to get God's people to wake up to the thing that God wants them to see in their present. They're not future oriented nearly as much as they are present oriented."*
- **Both/and over either/or (two true tellings)** — When Brent asks which source is "right," the answer is both. Brent: *"They're both right. Potentially."* Marty: *"Yeah, they're just written from different perspectives. And those perspectives have certain... limits to them."*
- **Canon order as interpretation (Hebrew TaNaKh vs. Christian OT)** — The same books in a different order teach differently. Marty: *"the book order of the TaNaKh of a Jewish Bible is different then the Old Testament order... And that's instructive. And it's not... right or wrong."*

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **The review slide.** Marty opens by noting they rarely get to review *with the review in front of them*; today a whiteboard-style slide is provided. Marty: *"there is a slide for your review if you're not driving."*
2. **Meta-narrative recap — Preface and Introduction.** Genesis 1–11 as the preface (good Creation, humanity struggles to trust, chaos multiplies into Empire); Genesis 12–50 as the introduction (Abraham's family — "a hot mess" — learning to trust the story and forgive).
3. **A Tale of Two Kingdoms.** The narrative proper begins in Exodus: Empire vs. Shalom; rescue out of Egypt, the Sinai "wedding," the tabernacle as "honeymoon suite," the call to be *"a kingdom of priests."*
4. **Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy.** Leviticus teaches priesthood, atonement, and partying ("tov meod"); Numbers is the "desert honeymoon"; Deuteronomy is "remember" — 14 calls to remember plus 5 "do not forget," centered on the **AOW** (alien, orphan, widow).
5. **Joshua and Judges.** Joshua places the people at the crossroads to bless all nations; Judges carries the **redemption cycle** (named, not re-explained) — *"God's redemption is always better than our sin is bad."*
6. **Ruth.** Elle's episode recapped — "a love story about a Moabitess, an outsider, who is righteous and who is welcomed in."
7. **The disorientation problem.** The honest confession that drives the episode: a straight-through reader hits Saul/David/Solomon in Samuel/Kings, then meets them *again* in 1 Chronicles. Marty: *"all of a sudden I turn the page in First Chronicles and I go back to Saul?"*
8. **One story, two sources (the thesis).** *"You have one story, but you have two sources that are telling the story."* Samuel/Kings = one source; Chronicles = another; same period, different shaping.
9. **A caveat about certainty.** Marty signals he will speak "in broad strokes... generalizations... loosely and poetically," naming the many historical/dating debates he is deliberately deferring, and warning against teachers who say "this is the way that it is."
10. **The TaNaKh explained.** Torah / Nevi'im / Ketuvim — same books, different order. Marty walks Brent through the surprise that **Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings are in the Prophets**.
11. **History is prophetic.** Because Samuel/Kings sit among the Nevi'im, they tell history *as prophets* — with a message, like Isaiah had a message. This also explains Marty's earlier wrestling with Joshua as prophetic conquest narrative, not flat history.
12. **David and Goliath as prophetic.** The previous episode's story didn't just report events; it provoked. Marty: *"This is asking me to be a particular kind of person, to be a particular kind of leader... It's prophetic in how it delivers the story."*
13. **Source B — Chronicles.** From "Judah's perspective," written ~400 years later during the exile, like a documentary. Chronicles is literally the **last book of the Hebrew Bible** — the final word is spoken from hindsight.
14. **The 9/11 (and Vietnam/Iraq) analogy.** Reading the headlines in real time (limited, in-the-moment) vs. watching a good documentary 20 years later (motivations, backstory). Both true, different limits.
15. **Pulling the two apart.** Marty's payoff: once you know you're reading the same period from two ages, the previously confusing section becomes legible — "now we can start to move towards a more complete understanding."
16. **Brent's testimony.** The tools made familiar-but-disconnected stories finally fit together: *"I just never knew how to fit any of it together... this conversation was super helpful."*

**Coverage verification:** The review recap (Preface → Introduction → Two Kingdoms → Torah books → Joshua/Judges → Ruth), the disorientation problem, the one-story/two-sources thesis, the certainty caveat, the TaNaKh/Nevi'im/Ketuvim structure, "history is prophetic," the David-and-Goliath example, Source B/Chronicles-as-documentary, and the 9/11 analogy are all represented. Strays (the Romans forward reference, the Deuteronomy "remember" count from the live-stream Q&A, the "Source A/B as training wheels" caution) are parked in **Other Talking Points** below.

## Key Lessons

Framed against the Session 2 arc — the partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **One story, two sources.** The kingdom era is told twice, and that is a feature, not a contradiction. Marty: *"the Samuel/Kings narrative is one source, and the Chronicles narrative is one source. Both of them are talking about the same period of history."* **Arc link:** before Session 2 watches the partner fail through the monarchy, the reader is equipped to hear two faithful tellings of that failure instead of tripping over the repetition.

2. **History is prophetic.** Hebrew history-telling is designed to wake people up, not merely to report. Marty: *"history for the Hebrew mind is prophetic... There is a message... you're telling history in Samuel and Kings as a prophet."* **Arc link:** the record of Israel's unfaithfulness is itself an invitation to repent in the present — the Prophets' whole posture.

3. **Perspective shapes the telling — and both can be true.** Agenda-driven headlines (Samuel/Kings) and a hindsight documentary (Chronicles) are both authoritative, each with limits. Brent: *"They're both right. Potentially."* **Arc link:** God's refusal to quit is testified to from two vantage points — Israel's raw real-time account and Judah's reflective exile account — strengthening, not weakening, the claim.

4. **Chronicles' placement preaches.** The last word of the Hebrew Bible is spoken from exile, looking back. Marty: *"Chronicles is literally the last book of the Hebrew Bible... written while the people of God are in exile... looking back."* **Arc link:** the canon itself ends mid-story with a people in captivity still retelling their history — a people God has not abandoned.

5. **Hold your tools loosely.** Source A/B is a helpful on-ramp, not a final doctrine; certainty about dating and construction is deferred. Marty: *"It's very helpful at the beginning and then as you keep going, learn how to loosen your grip."* **Arc link:** the humility models how to read the hard monarchy stories ahead — generously, provisionally, without claiming more than the text gives.

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension: the surfaced problem, a classification tag, the cited quote, a verse link, and a Hebraic resolution (or "Open — dance with it").

- **Why does the Bible repeat the same kings twice?** *(Structural / canon-ordering tension.)* Marty: *"all of a sudden I turn the page in First Chronicles and I go back to Saul?... Why am I reading about this again?"* Text: [1 Chronicles 10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Chronicles%2010&version=NIV) (Chronicles' retelling of Saul). **Hebraic resolution:** one story told by two sources — Samuel/Kings (near-real-time headlines) and Chronicles (an exile-era documentary) — not a duplication error but two purposeful tellings.

- **Can biased, "agenda-driven" history be trusted as Scripture?** *(Historiography / genre tension.)* Marty: *"We are used to when we read history, assuming that history is unbiased... But the ancient world of the Bible did not do that. And that might strike us sideways."* Text: [2 Kings 17:7–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Kings%2017%3A7-23&version=NIV) (Kings openly interpreting why the exile happened). **Hebraic resolution:** ancient history was written *to teach*; the "agenda" is the inspired message, so the bias is the point, not a defect.

- **How can Joshua and Judges, or Samuel/Kings and Chronicles, both be "right"?** *(Both/and interpretive tension.)* Brent: *"They're both right. Potentially."* Marty: *"they're just written from different perspectives. And those perspectives have certain... limits."* Text: [2 Chronicles 36:22–23](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Chronicles%2036%3A22-23&version=NIV) (the hindsight ending of the Hebrew Bible). **Hebraic resolution:** different perspectives with different limits can each be "exactly right, exactly authoritative, exactly inspired"; the 9/11 headlines and the later documentary are both true accounts of one event.

- **When was this written, and does that unsettle the faith?** *(Historical / dating tension.)* Marty: *"it very well could be that this is some of the oldest stuff you have in the Bible... you don't have to agree with this, if this just rocks your world way too much."* Text: [1 Samuel 9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=1%20Samuel%209&version=NIV). **Open — dance with it.** Marty deliberately defers the "fun historical" debates ("we're not going to get into that... I will later"), inviting the listener to loosen their grip rather than resolve it now.

## Terminology

- **TaNaKh** — Hebrew acronym for **T**orah, **N**evi'im, **K**etuvim; the Hebrew Scriptures contain the same books as the Christian Old Testament but in a different order. Marty: *"It refers to the three parts of what we call the Old Testament... the Torah, the Nevi'im, and the Ketuvim. It's the same books... but... they are in a different order."*
- **Nevi'im (the Prophets)** — the TaNaKh's second section, which surprisingly *includes* Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings. Marty: *"Samuel and Kings is a prophet? Indeed, because history for the Hebrew mind is prophetic."*
- **Ketuvim (the Writings)** — the "catch-all" category of wisdom books and the five scrolls (Ruth, Esther) — and **Chronicles**, the last book of the Hebrew Bible. Brent: *"It's a writing... It's the very last one."*
- **History is prophetic** — Hebrew history-telling delivers God's present-tense message, not neutral reportage. Marty: *"you're telling history in Samuel and Kings as a prophet. There is a message."*
- **Source A (Samuel/Kings)** — the near-real-time, "agenda-driven headlines" told from "Israel's perspective" (historically, not geographically). Marty: *"Source A is Samuel and Kings... written as agenda-driven headlines."*
- **Source B (Chronicles)** — the same era retold ~400 years later during the exile from "Judah's perspective," "like you're watching a documentary." Marty: *"Chronicles is Source B... It's from Judah's perspective, and it's like you're watching a documentary, rather than reading the headlines."*
- **AOW (alien, orphan, widow)** — Deuteronomy's refrain of who to remember; "the foreigner, the orphan, and the widow." Marty: *"Remember, essentially, especially what we call the AOW: the alien, the orphan, the widow."*

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty claims that for the Hebrew mind "history is prophetic" — written to wake people up, not merely to report. How does reading Samuel/Kings as a *message* rather than a neutral record change what you look for in those stories?
2. Brent says both sources are "right," just written from different perspectives with different limits. Where in your own life have you told the same true story two different ways depending on when and why you were telling it? Which telling was "more true"?
3. The ancient world wrote history with an open agenda, unlike the Western ideal of unbiased reporting. Is a declared agenda more honest than a hidden one? What agenda do you bring when you tell your own history?
4. Chronicles — the last word of the Hebrew Bible — was written in exile, looking back. What does it mean that the canon ends with a captive people still retelling their story rather than with a tidy resolution?
5. Marty urges listeners to hold the Source A / Source B framework "loosely" and to beware teachers who say "this is the way that it is." Where are you gripping an interpretive tool too tightly, and what would loosening your grip look like?

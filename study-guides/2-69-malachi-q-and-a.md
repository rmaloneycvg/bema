**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings
**Primary text:** [Malachi](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi&version=NIV) — read in this episode: [Malachi 1:6–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi+1%3A6-8&version=NIV), [Malachi 2:13–14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi+2%3A13-14&version=NIV), [Malachi 3:6–18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi+3%3A6-18&version=NIV)

---

## Where This Sits in the One Story

Session 1 built the picture of **partnership**: God rescues a people out of Egypt and invites them to become a kingdom of priests who join his restoration project. Session 2 turns to the **Prophets**, and the arc of this stage is the covenant partner's (Israel's) **unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Malachi is the **last of the prophetic voices** — only Esther remains in Session 2, and it is not counted as prophecy.

Malachi stands at the far end of the arc. The warnings, the woes, the exile, and even the return have all already happened. The remnant has come *back* from Persia — but the glorious kingdom they dreamed of was never handed to them, and they still sit under the boot of empire. In this stage the partner's unfaithfulness takes a *new* and subtler shape: not idolatry or outright rebellion, but **apathy and disillusionment**. The people are so disappointed that "things didn't turn out the way they had dreamed" that they have gone slack — offering blind and lame animals, withholding tithes, and muttering that "it is futile to serve God." Marty names the condition precisely: they have been "robbed of [their] identity as partners of God."

This is exactly where God's refusal to quit shines most clearly against Session 1's partnership. God does not walk away from an apathetic, disillusioned people. Instead he sends "my messenger" (that is what *Malachi* means) to argue with them — a back-and-forth Q&A that drags their grievances into the open and answers them. And the book does not close in despair: a **faithful remnant** hears, writes a scroll of remembrance, and God once again "had found a partner." Relative to Session 1, this episode shows the partnership surviving its lowest, flattest, most cynical moment — and God still refusing to let the covenant die.

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **Malachi, the last prophetic voice.** Marty frames the episode: one more book after this (Esther, not counted as prophecy in Jewish thought or the Gentile Bible). A listener (Emily) has been waiting months for Malachi, which makes Marty a little nervous.
2. **Recapping the remnant voices.** Brent and Marty list the remnant prophets they've covered: 4 Isaiah, Ezra–Nehemiah, Haggai, Zechariah — frustrations and struggles that have been building.
3. **The frustrations boil over.** The struggles of Haggai and Zechariah find their "fruition" in the decades after the restoration: the people are disappointed that the return did not restore a glorious kingdom.
4. **Persia as subtler empire (Brueggemann).** The "turning of the tables" was really a *reshuffling* of items on the table; Persia's rule was longer, wider, and deeper than Babylon's — less brutal, more subtle — enticing the people into a *cultural* servitude that robbed them of their identity as partners of God.
5. **Nostalgia and the lie of a better past.** Losing hope, the people long for "the good old days." Marty's extended reflection (via Rabbi Fohrman): "Do we imagine new tomorrows, or do we lie to ourselves about a better past?" You can only go forward; the past wasn't as great as remembered, and believing the lie makes us live more poorly in the present.
6. **What "Malachi" means.** The name means "my messenger" — possibly a title rather than a proper name; we don't know.
7. **The image: Q&A.** After reviewing the images (4 Isaiah = hope, Ezra–Nehemiah = passionate leadership, Haggai = Build!, Zechariah = apocalyptic literature), Marty names Malachi's nugget: **Q&A** — God accuses, the people respond with an incredulous question, God answers.
8. **First dialogue (1:6–8).** "If I am a father, where is the honor due me?" / "How have we shown contempt?" / "By offering defiled, blind, lame, diseased animals — try offering them to your governor." Brent and Marty note the irony against later Jewish scrupulousness about spotless sacrifice; here they shrug, "a goat's a goat."
9. **Nadab-and-Abihu parallel.** Marty likens the blemished offerings to the "common fire" of Nadab and Abihu in Leviticus — an apathetic approach to worship.
10. **Second dialogue (2:13–14).** "You flood the LORD's altar with tears… because the LORD is the witness between you and the wife of your youth, to whom you have been unfaithful" — echoing Hosea.
11. **Third dialogue (3:6–15).** "Return to me and I will return to you" / "How are we to return?" / "Will a mere mortal rob God?… in tithes and offerings… Bring the whole tithe into the storehouse… test me in this."
12. **The tithe passage in context.** 3:10 is routinely made into a *formula* or prosperity-gospel proof text; in context the problem is apathetic unbelief ("It is futile to serve God… the arrogant are blessed"), and the point is that disbelief and an apathetic walk carry real consequences.
13. **Giving then vs. now.** Brent contrasts frictionless electronic giving with the real labor of hauling goods and animals; "what good does it do?" bit harder for them.
14. **The scroll of remembrance (3:16–18).** A faithful remnant hears: "those who feared the LORD talked with each other, and the LORD listened… a scroll of remembrance was written… 'they will be my treasured possession'" — hearkening back to Exodus and the marriage.
15. **God finds a partner again.** Marty's closing synthesis: sometimes the partner is 7,000 who never bowed to Baal, or the few who could have saved Sodom, or a handful who put their names on a scroll — and that is enough to change everything. Malachi for a world dominated by debate and bifurcated worldviews.

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 2 arc — the covenant partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **The partner's unfaithfulness here is apathy, not idolatry — and God refuses to let it slide.** The remnant's disappointment has curdled into a lackadaisical worship: blemished sacrifices, withheld tithes, a shrug. God's refusal to quit shows up as a *confrontation* that takes their apathy seriously rather than ignoring it.
   - *Marty:* "They're not even offering unblemished sacrifices. They're falling into this lackadaisical—I think of Nadab and Abihu in Leviticus offering common fire… this apathetic approach to their worship."
   - *Marty:* "Where's my honor, where's my respect? Would your governor accept these gifts? If he wouldn't accept these gifts, why would I accept these gifts?"

2. **Empire's subtlest weapon is to rob the partner of its identity.** Persia didn't persecute them into idolatry; it enticed them into a cultural servitude that quietly dissolved their sense of being God's partners. Naming that is how the arc's "unfaithfulness" is diagnosed at this late stage — the partner has forgotten who it is.
   - *Marty:* "Instead of being robbed of your material possessions, you are robbed of your identity as partners of God."
   - *Marty:* "It's harder to actually fight the idolatry that's present in our Western American culture than it is to fight outright idolatry because it's more subtle and hidden and internal."

3. **The lie of a better past makes present faithfulness impossible.** The remnant's nostalgia is itself a form of unfaithfulness — it keeps them from walking forward with God. God's messenger comes precisely "to shake the people out of their foolish nostalgia."
   - *Marty:* "Do we imagine, do we dream about new tomorrows or do we lie to ourselves about a better past?"
   - *Marty:* "When I begin to believe this, I start to live much more poorly in the present. I get a lot more cynical… I find myself beginning to fall into sins of the past as well."

4. **The tithe passage is not a transaction but a diagnosis of unbelief.** Read as a formula, 3:10 flatters our appetites; read in context, it exposes an apathetic walk that no longer believes God's promises. The arc's refusal-to-quit is not a vending machine — it is a call back into trusting partnership.
   - *Marty:* "Not a formula of, if you tithe, God does X, Y, and Z, but this message of when you don't believe in the promises of God, when you take this apathetic approach to your walk, it has these larger consequences."
   - *Marty:* "You say that following me does nothing. You say that it's futile to serve God. That's why you're not bringing me your tithes… because you don't think any of this stuff actually works."

5. **God's refusal to quit succeeds here: he finds a partner again.** Unlike the hopeless prophets whose audiences never get it, Malachi ends with a remnant that hears, repents, and writes a scroll of remembrance — and God responds in kind. The partnership of Session 1 survives its most disillusioned hour.
   - *Marty:* "That day God, once again, had found a partner. Sometimes that partner is simply 7,000 people who haven't bowed the knee to Baal… Sometimes it's just a few people… who put their names on a scroll and set out to become people of the promise."
   - *Marty:* "It's not one of those hopeless prophets of despair where there's Q&A, but God's people just refuse to get it. In this case… they go, 'Man, okay. That's right. We're going to stand up.'"

## East/West Lens

Only categories genuinely engaged by this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Reading a passage in its original context rather than as a proof text (East).** The episode explicitly resists the Western habit of turning Malachi 3:10 into a tithing/prosperity formula and insists on recovering what the passage *meant* in the remnant's disillusionment.
  - *Marty:* "We make this really formulaic by forgetting the context, because the context of Malachi here is, God's people are falling into this apathetic… 'You've spoken arrogantly against me.'"

- **Hebrew word-meaning as interpretive key (East).** Recovering that *Malachi* means "my messenger" — and may be a title, not a name — reframes the whole book as God sending a messenger to argue his people back into partnership.
  - *Marty:* "Malachi just means 'my messenger.' It's likely that it might not even have been a proper name, but we don't know."

- **Reading intertextually across the whole story (East).** Meaning emerges by hearing Malachi echo Hosea (marital unfaithfulness), Leviticus (Nadab and Abihu's common fire), and Exodus ("treasured possession"/the marriage) rather than reading the book in isolation.
  - *Marty (on 2:13–14):* "Oh, here, that goes with Hosea there."
  - *Marty (on 3:17):* "Hearkens back to Exodus, the marriage. I like that."

- **Covenant framed as marriage rather than contract (East).** The relationship God defends is spousal — "the wife of your youth," unfaithfulness, a marriage covenant — not a legal arrangement, which is why apathy registers as *betrayal*.
  - *Brent (reading 2:14):* "the Lord is the witness between you and the wife of your youth. You have been unfaithful to her. Though she is your partner, the wife of your marriage covenant."

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension states the problem, tags its type, cites the transcript, links the passage, and gives a Hebraic resolution (or marks it open).

1. **How could the famously scrupulous people of the sacrificial system offer *blind and lame* animals?**
   - *Type:* Historical/logic gap (contradiction with later Jewish practice).
   - *Problem:* Later Jewish history went to extraordinary lengths for spotless sacrifice; the shrugging, blemished offerings of Malachi seem almost impossible by comparison.
   - *Cited:* *Brent:* "The lengths that they went to, to offer perfect, spotless sacrifice. To think that this is what they're doing, it's like, 'What? How's that even possible?'" / *Marty:* "but here a blind goat. 'Man, who cares. It's not really as good as we wanted it to be anyway.'"
   - *Passage:* [Malachi 1:6–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi+1%3A6-8&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The contradiction *is* the point. The blemished goat is a symptom of disillusionment, not of ignorance — "it's not really as good as we wanted it to be anyway." Apathy, not incompetence, degrades the worship; the later scrupulousness is what faithfulness looks like once the partner remembers who it is.

2. **Isn't Malachi 3:10 a promise that giving triggers God's blessing — the tithing/prosperity formula?**
   - *Type:* Interpretive tension (proof text vs. original context).
   - *Problem:* 3:10 is routinely lifted into a transactional formula (give → God pours out) or a prosperity-gospel slogan.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "I think we've gotten really used to using this passage as a formulaic approach… whether it's giving or tithing or prosperity gospels… We make this really formulaic by forgetting the context."
   - *Passage:* [Malachi 3:6–12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi+3%3A6-12&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* In context the passage diagnoses apathetic unbelief, not a payout mechanism. The people withhold tithes *because* they have decided "it is futile to serve God"; the "test me in this" is a summons back into trusting partnership, not a vending-machine guarantee. The consequence named is relational — "it doesn't honor me. It's not worship. It's not wholeness."

3. **Why does the book have to argue — a whole Q&A — instead of God simply declaring the verdict?**
   - *Type:* Genre/form question (why dialogue?).
   - *Problem:* God repeatedly makes an accusation only to have the people answer "How have we…?", forcing an explanation; it would seem simpler just to pronounce judgment.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "God's going to make an accusation that people are going to respond to with a question. Like, 'What? How could you say that?'… Then God's going to answer that."
   - *Passage:* [Malachi 1:6–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi+1%3A6-8&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The dialogue form *is* God's refusal to quit. He keeps the conversation open rather than ending it, drawing the apathetic partner into self-recognition. The Q&A works — unlike the "hopeless prophets of despair," this audience finally says "that's right" and acts, which is only possible because God kept talking.

4. **Can a people genuinely weeping at the altar still be the unfaithful party?**
   - *Type:* Emotional/moral tension (tears vs. unfaithfulness).
   - *Problem:* The people "flood the LORD's altar with tears" yet are told God no longer accepts their offerings because they have been unfaithful — grief and guilt sit together uncomfortably.
   - *Cited:* *Brent (reading 2:13–14):* "You flood the Lord's altar with tears… You ask, 'Why?' It is because the Lord is the witness between you and the wife of your youth. You have been unfaithful to her."
   - *Passage:* [Malachi 2:13–14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Malachi+2%3A13-14&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* Read through the marriage covenant (and Hosea's echo), the tears are real but misdirected — grief over consequences without turning from the unfaithfulness that caused them. The resolution the book offers is not more weeping but *return* ("Return to me and I will return to you"), which the remnant finally enacts in the scroll of remembrance.

## Terminology

- **Malachi** — the Hebrew name/title meaning "my messenger"; possibly a title rather than a proper name.
  - *Marty:* "Malachi just means 'my messenger.' It's likely that it might not even have been a proper name, but we don't know."
- **Q&A** — the "image"/nugget for the episode: Malachi's prophecy is structured as a dialogue — accusation, incredulous question, answer — between God and his people.
  - *Marty:* "the nugget for Malachi is going to be 'Q&A' because the structure that Malachi chooses to use in his prophecy is this dialogue."
- **remnant** — the returned community after exile; here, specifically, the faithful few within a disillusioned nation who hear Malachi's message.
  - *Marty:* "We are told that they were people who belong to a faithful remnant. Maybe not everybody, but we're told that there is a faithful remnant."
- **scroll of remembrance** — the record written by those who feared the LORD, marking their repentance; God promises they will be his "treasured possession."
  - *Brent (reading 3:16):* "A scroll of remembrance was written in his presence concerning those who feared the Lord and honored his name."
- **"treasured possession"** — God's promise over the remnant in 3:17, which Marty hears as an echo of Exodus and the marriage covenant.
  - *Marty:* "Hearkens back to Exodus, the marriage. I like that."
- **common fire** — the "unauthorized"/apathetic offering of Nadab and Abihu in Leviticus, Marty's parallel for Malachi's blemished sacrifices.
  - *Marty:* "I think of Nadab and Abihu in Leviticus offering common fire."

## Illustrations & Images

- **"A goat's a goat."** The shrug of apathetic worship — a blind or lame animal offered because nothing feels worth the effort anymore.
  - *Brent:* "A goat's a goat, whatever." / *Marty:* "A goat's a goat. Temple is a temple; path is a path."
- **Reshuffling the items on the table.** Marty's image for the return from Persia: it looked like the tables had turned, but it was only a rearrangement — the people were still under empire.
  - *Marty:* "What appeared to be the turning of the tables with the kingdom of Persia, happened to become a simple reshuffling of the items on the table."
- **The lie of "the good old days."** Marty's extended personal illustration about friends who were miserable in a context, left it, then remembered it fondly — and his own confession of doing the same "this very week."
  - *Marty:* "'You were miserable in those days. Those days were horrible. You couldn't wait to get out of those days, but now it's like, 'oh man, wasn't that the ticket.'' We do this all the time, longing for a yesterday that really wasn't what we remember it to be."
- **Electronic giving vs. hauling animals.** Brent's picture of how costless modern giving is compared with the real labor of gathering goods and animals and traveling to the temple.
  - *Brent:* "your giving is electronic most of the time. It's not really much difference in work one way or the other, but these people, they have to travel… 'What? It doesn't even do any good, so why go to all this effort?'"
- **Names on a scroll.** The closing image — a few people who put their names on a scroll and take a stand, with "the ability and the power to change everything."
  - *Marty:* "There are a few people that put their names on a scroll. It takes a stand to make a difference. It has the ability and the power to change everything."

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty says the remnant was "robbed of [their] identity as partners of God" — not by brutal persecution but by a subtle, enticing cultural servitude. Where do you see that subtler kind of idolatry at work in your own context, and why is it "harder to identify and fight"?
2. Rabbi Fohrman's question anchors the episode: "Do we imagine new tomorrows, or do we lie to ourselves about a better past?" Where are you tempted by a nostalgic "better past" that, honestly examined, "really wasn't what [you] remember it to be"?
3. The hosts insist Malachi 3:10 is not a tithing formula but a diagnosis of apathetic unbelief. How does reading it as "when you don't believe in the promises of God… it has these larger consequences" change the way you hear it?
4. Malachi is a *dialogue* — God accuses, the people ask "How have we…?", God answers. Where in your life would an honest Q&A with God surface a "How have we…?" you'd rather not ask?
5. The book turns on a faithful remnant who "talked with each other" and wrote their names on a scroll of remembrance. What would it look like for you and a few others to "put [your] names on a scroll and set out to become people of the promise"?
6. Marty confesses to wanting to "go back to how it was four years ago." You can only go forward, not backward. What is one forward step God may be inviting you into that nostalgia is keeping you from taking?

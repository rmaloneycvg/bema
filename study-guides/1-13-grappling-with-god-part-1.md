# Grappling with God, Part 1

BEMA 13 · Session 1
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/1-13-grappling-with-god-part-1.md`
Book: Genesis (Genesis 25:19–34; 28:10–22; 31:43–55; walking through chs. 25–31)

> **Session 1 overarching arc: partnership.** This is **part one of a two-part, deliberately open-ended survey of the life of Jacob** — the third of four patriarchs. The hosts refuse "thunderous conclusions" and leave Jacob "hanging" at a cliffhanger (part two follows; a deeper character study is promised in Session 6). The driving question is a partnership question: *why does God keep choosing to work through this chaotic, deceptive mess of a man?* The answer the episode gropes toward — God would rather partner with a Jacob who has fire than an Esau who has no care — is the arc in miniature: partnership turns not on having it all together, but on wanting in.

## Key Lessons

- **God keeps showing up for Jacob out of "stupidly gracious" grace, long before Jacob deserves it or is transformed.** At Bethel, after Jacob has graduated from opportunism to "outright deception," God meets him in a dream with a promise. Reed: *"this is a stupidly gracious response on God's part... it is a ridiculously beautiful gracious response on God's part."* Marty voices the tension the whole episode sits in: *"Why would God show grace here? Why would God be stupidly gracious in His response?... He certainly doesn't deserve it, and He's certainly not Abraham, and He's certainly not Isaac."* Grace precedes transformation — God travels with the mess.
- **God prefers the one who *wants it* (fire/chutzpah) over the one who has it but doesn't care.** Marty's central takeaway: *"if God has to choose between a guy that doesn't have it all together, but he wants it, versus the guy who has it alright, but just doesn't care... I think God chooses the Jacob."* And: *"it's easier to steer the momentum of Jacob than it is to light a fire under an Esau."* Jacob's defining trait across the chaos is his fire: *"the one thing we can point out in the life of Jacob... is his fire. We call it chutzpah... he wants it."*
- **Jacob's life is a compounding, unsustainable chaos of his own making — the opposite of the shalom his father built.** Reed names the keyword: Jacob is *"always doing whatever he has to do in the moment, not thinking about... how that's going to spiral out,"* a *"chaotic sort of life."* Marty contrasts it sharply with Isaac: *"This is not the kind of world that I want to be. It's not what Isaac was building with the quarreling herdsmen... Isaac's bringing shalom to chaos. This is just pure chaos that Jacob has in his wake... Isaac was blessing the Canaanites... Jacob can't even get along with his cousins."*
- **The birthright (bechor) is weight before it is wealth — a double portion of responsibility, not just inheritance; Esau despises it, Jacob craves it.** Marty: *"he gets that double portion of the inheritance because he has a double portion of responsibility... he's responsible for... shouldering that and carrying that on."* The chosen son must "realize the weight of this calling and the weight of this partnership that he's called to." Esau sells it for stew — *"he despises it... He just doesn't care, but Jacob wants it."*
- **The holy breaks in at the "middle of nowhere's nowhere."** The unremarkable "certain place" where Jacob sleeps is, in the Hebrew sense, maximally ordinary and unholy — and that is exactly where God shows up. Marty: *"in these very mundane, very normal, very in-between, very unholy places, we can have these quite holy experiences... God was in this place the whole time and I wasn't even aware of it."* Reed ties it to not-knowing: *"Only once you admit that you don't know... only then do you actually experience the mystery of God."*

## East/West Lens

- **Words (power of the spoken blessing vs. abstract/powerless speech).** The episode's strongest Eastern move: words *do* things. Marty: *"they even saw... a really efficacious... covenantal, we would say contractual reality. There's a binding reality when these words went out,"* which is why Isaac cannot simply take the mis-given blessing back — *"once they're gone, in their world, these words go to work... He can't just... do flipsies."* Reed sharpens the contrast with trivial speech: *"That kind of speech is really different from... 'what time is it? It's 3-15'... That kind of speech is powerless compared to this kind of speech."*
- **Community / lineage vs. individual (the beit av and patrilineal legacy).** The birthright only makes sense inside a household-carrying-a-mission frame: *"this firstborn son is the chosen person in this patrilineal society to carry on dad's lineage... The other sons... can choose to go start their own beit av's."* The question is who carries the family's covenant mission forward, not individual advantage.
- **Names carry destiny / dual nature (word-picture vs. flat label).** A name is a loaded word-picture, often with "positive and negative rub." *tahm* ("even-tempered / mild / blameless") ironically describes a man who is anything but — Reed: *"Jacob is not peaceful. He is not mild. He's definitely not content."* Marty wonders if the name holds "the dual nature... He has the tools, the ingredients to be these things. And yet we see the other side of the coin."
- **Discovery / mystery vs. cognitive mastery.** Reed's icon illustration frames knowing God as *un*-knowing: the darker center means *"the more you experience God, the less you understand God,"* and genuine encounter begins when *"you don't have it all together, mentally, cognitively."* This is the Eastern preference for lived mystery over Western explanatory certainty.
- **Eating together as covenant (relational-legal act vs. mere meal).** At the Galeed heap, sharing food is a public, legal declaration of restored relationship. Marty: *"you eat with someone... what it tells the world that would be watching is that you and this person are in fellowship... legally right relationship."* (He links it forward to *"Jesus eating with sinners and tax collectors."*)
- **Tragedy and messiness as true-to-life vs. clean good/bad boxes.** Reed: *"maybe it is well-intentioned. Maybe they just wanted what God wanted, but they went about it maybe in the wrong ways. And then the destruction kind of gets out of control."* Marty affirms the Eastern comfort with unresolved complexity: *"we would love it if everything... fit so cleanly in a good box and a bad box. And yet they're kind of this weird mixture of the two. It's very true to our experience."*

## Recurring Through-lines

- **Partnership (Session 1 arc) — God partners with fire, not with pedigree.** The whole episode is a meditation on who God chooses to carry the mission forward. The partnership is not forfeited by failure but by indifference: God *"loves to work with Jacob's,"* and reaches even reluctant partners before they are cleaned up. Jacob's craving for the birthright — the weight of the covenant partnership — is what makes him usable.
- **Chutzpah / holy audacity.** Explicitly named and tied back to Abraham: *"We call it chutzpah. We've talked about it with Abraham."* Jacob's fire is the same God-pleasing audacity seen earlier in the patriarch arc; here it is the single redeeming thread in an otherwise troubling life. (Flag for cross-reference with earlier chutzpah moments; do not re-explain the term's full backstory here.)
- **The mission handed down generation to generation.** The birthright/blessing is the baton of Abraham's mission: *"Isaac carried his father's legacy really well... now the story needs to be passed on from him to his son... from generation to generation."* This episode shows the baton passing *crookedly* — through deception rather than clean blessing — raising whether good ends justify "underhanded things."
- **Shalom vs. chaos.** Jacob is the deliberate foil to the previous episode's "mission realized": where Isaac brought *shalom* to the quarreling herdsmen and blessed outsiders, Jacob leaves "pure chaos... in his wake" and "can't even get along with his cousins." (Callback to the Isaac episode, 1-12.)
- **"God was in this place and I did not know it" — holiness in the ordinary.** The Bethel moment plants a motif the corpus returns to: the mundane in-between place as the site of divine encounter, recoverable only in hindsight.
- **Foreshadowing the wrestling / "not ready yet."** The name "heel-grasper" and Jacob's wheeling-dealing vow both point ahead to the night wrestling of part two. Reed: Jacob *"is still not at a point yet where he is ready for that kind of wrestling"*; the grasping at Bethel is bargaining, not the clinging, won't-let-go wrestling to come.

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat in the episode, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Framing the survey.** Marty sets up Jacob as the third of four patriarchs, admits he has "always struggled with Jacob," credits Rabbi David Fohrman's material for reshaping his reading, and announces a deliberately macro, two-part, conclusion-light treatment with a Session 6 deep dive to come. *(Marty: "we're gonna talk about Jacob on kind of a larger macro level here today, not a ton of... thunderous conclusions.")*
2. **Reading Gen 25:19–28.** Brent reads the birth of the twins and the womb-struggle; the prophecy that "the older will serve the younger."
3. **Jacob's name.** Discussion of *Ya'akov* / heel-grasper — "supplanter," "over-reacher," and the NET footnote's possible positive "may he protect." Marty notes he gave it as his own son's middle name for its "always want more" sense; Reed hears foreshadowing of the later wrestling-and-not-letting-go.
4. **"tahm" / content in the tents.** The ambiguous word (even-tempered / mild / simple / blameless); the irony versus Jacob's striving; the rabbinic reading that staying in the tents = devotion to God's way; Brent's question about why "tents" is specified.
5. **Reading Gen 25:29–34.** The stew-for-birthright sale; "Esau despised his birthright."
6. **The bechor explained.** Firstborn = "chosen"; double portion = double responsibility to carry the father's legacy; worked example of splitting an estate. Esau despises the weight; Jacob wants it — Jacob's "fire"/chutzpah introduced.
7. **Shared responsibility for the loss.** Reed puts "as much" blame on Esau's apathy as on Jacob's opportunism (not yet trickery); Brent asks whether Esau was genuinely near death and whether Esau even has the *right* to give away a birthright that is Isaac's to pass on.
8. **Jumping over Gen 26 to Gen 27 — the stolen blessing.** Rebekah's plot; the deception; why Isaac can't reverse it (power of spoken, covenantal words); whether Rebekah told Isaac the prophecy; whether good ends justify "underhanded" means; the story as a tragedy whose "destruction kind of gets out of control."
9. **Reading Gen 28:11 — Bethel / the dream.** "Middle of nowhere's nowhere"; the holy in the mundane; "God was in this place and I did not know it."
10. **Grace and the icon of unknowing.** Reed's "stupidly gracious" response; the orthodox icon whose dark center = the more you experience God the less you understand; encountering mystery only when you admit you don't have it together.
11. **"He's not ready for wrestling yet."** Jacob's bargaining vow ("if you protect me, then I'll serve you") as small, transactional thinking — not the later wrestling; no concern for his own transformation, only "I don't want to die."
12. **Gen 29–30 — Laban, Leah, Rachel, the "deceive-off."** Poetic justice: the deceiver deceived into marrying Leah; fourteen-plus years for Rachel; the flock-breeding competition and Jacob's dream-driven method; "is God in that?"; the relationship becomes "completely unsustainable."
13. **Fleeing again; Rachel steals the idols.** Marty links Abraham's refusal to send Isaac back to Nahor's house — a hint that living in Laban's household means "his gods go with you."
14. **Reading Gen 31:43–55 — the Galeed heap.** Two men with "legitimate beef" seeking peace; eating together as covenant fellowship; two stones, two names (Jegar-sahadutha / Galeed), two gods invoked — they "can't even agree on what to call the thing"; the Mizpah terms weighted to Laban's side; a second meal to finally settle it; Brent spots "one last element of trickery" in Laban's phrasing.
15. **Closing reflection.** The contrast with Isaac's shalom; the central question "why does God keep wanting to work through this guy?"; Marty's Jacob-vs-Esau answer (fire over compliance), with pastoral care for both "Jacobs" and "Esaus"; Reed's connection to the parable of the two sons (Matthew 21); book recommendations (Fohrman/Aleph Beta, Kushner).

## Terminology

- **Jacob / Ya'akov** (יעקב) — from the word for "heel"; "heel-grasper / supplanter / over-reacher." Marty: *"that's what his name actually means... it seems to imply more of heel, grasper, so, supplanter."* Reed offers *"over-reacher... whose reach exceeds their grasp"*; Brent cites the NET footnote's possible positive sense *"'may he protect,' as in a rear guard dogging the heels of everyone else."* Foreshadows the later wrestling where Jacob grasps and won't let go.
- **Esau / Edom** (עשו / אדום) — "red"; named for coming out "reddish all over, like a hairy garment," and called **Edom** ("red") for trading his birthright for the red stew. Brent reads: *"That is why he was also called Edom."*
- **tahm / tam** (תם) — the adjective for Jacob "living in tents": "even-tempered / mild / simple / content," and in Job "blameless." Reed: *"the Hebrew word is tahm... I've seen it translated... mild or simple. The word in Job actually gets used and it means blameless."* Ironic given Jacob's striving.
- **bechor** (בכור) — "firstborn / chosen." Marty: *"the word simply means 'chosen'... this firstborn son is the chosen person in this patrilineal society to carry on dad's lineage. This means that he gets a double portion of the inheritance... because he has a double portion of responsibility."*
- **chutzpah** — holy audacity / fire; the "wants it" drive. Marty: *"the one thing we can point out in the life of Jacob... is his fire. We call it chutzpah. We've talked about it with Abraham."*
- **"middle of nowhere's nowhere"** — the Hebrew sense of the unremarkable "certain place" where Jacob dreams; a mundane, unholy spot that becomes holy. Marty: *"the Hebrew here insinuates the middle of nowhere's nowhere... there is nothing holy about the place... And yet in these very mundane... places, we can have these quite holy experiences."*
- **Bethel** (בית אל) — "house of God"; the name Jacob gives the dream-place (formerly Luz). Marty: *"a place that he calls and returns to — Bethel, house of God. He meets God."*
- **beit av** — "house of the father," the patrilineal household; non-firstborn sons may leave to start their own. Marty: *"The other sons... can choose to go start their own beit av's if they want to."*

## Illustrations & References

- **The orthodox Christian icon with the darkening almond behind the Christ figure (Reed).** *"it actually gets darker as you go towards the center... the more you experience God, the less you understand God"* — the image for encountering divine mystery only when you admit you don't know.
- **Marriage/promise as reality made with words (Reed).** To explain binding covenantal speech: *"marriage is created with what? With words. And that actually creates a whole new reality"* — versus powerless speech like *"what time is it? It's 3-15."*
- **The "deceive-off" / game-show image (Marty).** Jacob and Laban's flock competition as *"like a game show, deception competition"* — poetic justice compounding the chaos.
- **God's "stupidly gracious" response (Reed).** The theological heart-image of the episode: grace lavished on an undeserving deceiver asleep on a stone.
- **Rabbi David Fohrman / Aleph Beta / the Parsha Companion.** Marty's reshaped reading of Jacob: *"he did some work on the life of Jacob that really, really helped."* Brent points listeners to Aleph Beta ("plenty of stuff on Jacob there"); a deeper dive is promised in Session 6.
- **_God Was in This Place & I, I Did Not Know_ by Harold Kushner.** Recommended; its title is drawn from Jacob's Bethel line (Gen 28:16). Marty: *"Kushner wrote a book, God Was in This Place & I, I Did Not Know... expands on some more Jewish wisdom."*

- **The NET translation footnote.** Brent cites it for the alternative "may he protect" reading of Jacob's name.
- **Matthew 21:28–32 — the parable of the two sons (Reed).** The reluctant son who nonetheless goes vs. the compliant son who doesn't: *"The tax collectors and the sinners are entering the kingdom ahead of you"* — a Jesus teaching behind the Jacob-over-Esau point ("God would rather find those people who are gonna go along, even if it's difficult").

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty keeps asking why God is "stupidly gracious" to a man who "certainly doesn't deserve it." Where in your own life has grace shown up *before* any change on your part — and what did it do to you that earning it never could?
2. Marty frames the choice as "a guy who wants it but doesn't have it together" (Jacob) versus "a guy who has it together but doesn't care" (Esau). Which are you more often — and does the claim that it's "easier to steer the momentum of a Jacob" sit comfortably or uncomfortably with you?
3. The birthright is "a double portion of responsibility," not just a double inheritance. How does seeing the chosen role as *weight* rather than *reward* change how you think about being "chosen" or called?
4. In their world, a spoken blessing could not be taken back — words "go to work." If you took the binding power of your own words that seriously, what would you say differently this week?
5. Reed asks whether knowing "what God wants to have happen" ever justifies "doing underhanded things to make it happen." Rebekah's plot "backfires" even as it "works." Where are you tempted to force a good outcome by crooked means?
6. Jacob meets God in the "middle of nowhere's nowhere" and only realizes it afterward — "God was in this place and I did not know it." Where might God have been present in an ordinary, in-between stretch of your life that you only recognize now?
7. The hosts deliberately leave Jacob "hanging" with few conclusions, insisting the mess is "true to our experience." What is lost when we rush a messy story into a clean "good box / bad box" — and what might be gained by sitting in the tension?

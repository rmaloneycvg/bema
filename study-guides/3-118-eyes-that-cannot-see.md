# BEMA 118: Eyes That Cannot See — Study Guide

**Session 3 (The Gospels) · Hosts: Marty Solomon & Brent Billings**
**Primary texts:** [Matthew 15:29–39](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2015%3A29-39&version=NIV); [Matthew 16:1–12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2016%3A1-12&version=NIV); [Mark 8:14–26](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208%3A14-26&version=NIV)

## Overview

This episode closes a long string of connected stories by reading the feeding of the 4,000, Jesus' warning about the "yeast of the Pharisees," and Mark's two-stage healing of a blind man as one sustained rabbinic lesson. Marty argues that the numbers in both feeding miracles are deliberate (a "gigantic remez"): the feeding of the 5,000 in the Jewish Triangle uses Jewish numbers, while the feeding of the 4,000 in the Gentile Decapolis switches to Gentile numbers. The point driven home is that Jesus is "enough" not only for the lost sheep of Israel but for the outsider — the mamzer, the Gentile, the one everyone assumed was out. The disciples' repeated confusion ("where will we get bread?") provokes Jesus to half-heal a blind man as a living prop: *do you have eyes but fail to see?*

Within Session 3's arc — the king/Messiah embodying Israel's story — this episode shows the Messiah actively **expanding the boundaries of the covenant partnership in action**, dragging his disciples (and us) toward a Kingdom that includes everyone they assumed was excluded.

1. **Recap and geography** — Jesus returns from Tyre and Sidon (Phoenicia), walks along the Sea of Galilee, and heads back into the Decapolis for the second and final recorded time (the first was the demoniac and the pigs).

4. **The feeding of the 4,000 ([Matthew 15:29–39](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2015%3A29-39&version=NIV))** — in the Decapolis, Gentile numbers appear: 7 loaves (completion / seven nations), unspecified fish, 7 baskets left over, 4,000 people (4 = Gentiles).
5. **The disciples' repeated food crisis** — the same "remote place" problem as before, now compounded because they are in unclean pagan territory.
6. **The demand for a sign & the sign of Jonah ([Matthew 16:1–4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2016%3A1-4&version=NIV))** — Pharisees and Sadducees ask for a sign; Jesus points to the sign of Jonah, read here as a Gentile reference, not the resurrection.

9. **Mark's "cherry on top" ([Mark 8:14–26](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208%3A14-26&version=NIV))** — "do you have eyes but fail to see?" leads directly into the two-stage healing of the blind man at Bethsaida, read as a deliberate teaching prop.
10. **Closing** — the lesson's weight sets up the "hike" (Caesarea Philippi) coming next; podcast housekeeping (Facebook page, newsletter, group map).

## Key Lessons

### 1. The numbers are intentional — a "gigantic remez"
Marty insists the symbolic numbers in the feeding stories are not accidental but a deliberate interpretive game pointing to a larger teaching.

> **Marty:** "This all fits the same story in the Text. It's like a gigantic remez. I think Jesus is getting at, 'When you let me interpret the law and completely trust me with it, there is more than enough to go around for all of God's people.'"

In the feeding of the 5,000 ([Matthew 14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2014&version=NIV), reviewed here): 5 loaves (books of Moses) + 2 fish (tablets) = 7, the complete Law; 5,000 (complete community × books of Moses = the Jews); 12 baskets (tribes of Israel) left over.

### 2. The geography changes the numbers — Jesus is enough for the Gentiles too
When the setting shifts from the Jewish Triangle to the Gentile Decapolis, the numbers shift from Jewish to Gentile.

> **Marty:** "If the number 5 was a Jewish number, and the number 4 is a Gentile number, we have to realize that we're now in the Decapolis... We have a community of pagan Gentiles. Hence the number is 4."

> **Marty:** "Not only am I enough for the Jewish people, I am also enough for the Gentile nations."

The Gentiles have neither the books nor the tablets of Moses — so the Spirit "doesn't remember" the fish count; what remains is 7 loaves (completion / seven nations from [Joshua 3:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joshua%203%3A10&version=NIV)) and 7 baskets.

### 3. The "yeast of the Pharisees" is their sin — refusing the outsider
Yeast is a consistently negative symbol (sin), and the specific sin named is the Pharisees' failure toward the mamzer.

> **Marty:** "Beware of their teachings, beware of their sin, because they are going to teach you to not do the thing that I'm trying to impress on you. Which is going to the Decapolis, which is finding the Gentiles, which is feeding the 4,000."

Brent presses the nuance and Marty concedes it:
> **Brent:** "I do have to say that Jesus does relate yeast to teaching here. I feel a little bit vindicated from earlier." **Marty:** "Absolutely." **Brent:** "But sinful teaching."

### 4. Miracles as teaching tools — the blind man is a prop
Marty's hardest claim: Jesus half-heals the Bethsaida blind man on purpose, to dramatize the disciples' blindness.

> **Marty:** "I think Jesus half heals this guy and stares at his disciples in the eyeballs and goes, 'You've got to be kidding me.' ... Then, Jesus completes the healing because it'd be really lame to make the guy blind again and send him back to the town."

> **Marty:** "This is a perfect example of Jesus's miracles being a teaching tool, and not about the content of the miracle itself. This blind man is nothing more than a prop for Jesus's teaching."

### 5. The disciples are partners, not lackeys
A key read of the feeding miracles: Jesus hands the Law *to the disciples* to distribute, giving them a critical role.

> **Marty:** "Jesus doesn't wander around and hand out bread and fish. Jesus says, 'I'm going to take the law and give it to you disciples, and you disciples play a critical role in this mission of God and the bringing of Kingdom.'"

> **Brent:** "I feel like most of the time you read that, it's like, oh, well, the disciples are just the lackeys, but that's not what Jesus is doing with it."

## How This Episode Advances the One Story

- **Session 1 (partnership):** God sought a human partner to carry blessing to the whole world. Here that partnership motif is explicit — Jesus does not do the work solo but puts the "complete Law" *into the disciples' hands* to feed God's people, insisting they "play a critical role in this mission of God." The second-Moses framing (groups of fifties at Sinai, [Exodus 18–19](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Exodus%2018-19&version=NIV)) ties the Messiah back to the founding partnership story.
- **Session 2 (the partner's unfaithfulness; God refusing to quit):** The "yeast of the Pharisees" is this episode's naming of the partner's failure — the religious leadership has drawn the covenant lines too tightly, excluding the mamzer and the Gentile. Jesus refuses to accept that failure as final; he physically returns to the Decapolis to enact a wider mission.
- **Session 3 (the king/Messiah embodying the story):** The Messiah embodies Israel's vocation by *becoming* the point of the story — "I am enough" for Jews and Gentiles alike. He doesn't merely teach the covenant's global scope; he performs it (feeding 4,000 Gentiles) and demands his partners see it. The episode advances the arc from *diagnosis* of the partner's blindness toward the Messiah forcibly opening his partners' eyes to a Kingdom bigger than they imagined — explicitly setting up the next, harder leg of the journey (the hike toward Caesarea Philippi) without yet getting there.

## East/West Lens

*Only categories actually engaged by this episode are tagged, each with a citation.*

- **Block logic / associative reading over linear chronology (Eastern).** The hosts read a chain of stories as thematically interwoven rather than strictly sequential, and note the Gospels themselves order material thematically.
  > **Marty:** "Matthew does change this... Matthew is trying to put things, I think, in a particular order here with the Pharisees and the encounter and the yeast and all of that stuff."

- **Symbol and number as meaning-bearing (Eastern/Hebraic) vs. flat literalism (Western).** The entire reading hinges on numbers carrying covenantal meaning, not just arithmetic.
  > **Marty:** "All these numbers make a difference... I suggested that in the feeding of the 5,000, Jesus takes the law which is 5 loaves."

- **Honor/shame and insider/outsider categories (Eastern).** Clean/unclean geography and the mamzer category drive the plot — the disciples' food "problem" is really a purity problem.
  > **Marty:** "They can't go into a pagan city that's all unclean. They can't eat there... They have a food problem this time."

- **Rabbinic pedagogy — the teacher provokes the student to "heavy lifting" (Eastern).** Jesus expects interpretive labor, not spoon-feeding.
  > **Marty:** "I'm a rabbi trying to teach you things and you know how to do basic life stuff like discern the weather patterns. Work with me here... Do some heavy lifting."

## Narrative Tensions

### 1. "Did they go back across the lake?" — the mushy chronology
**Problem:** The sequence of boat trips and the yeast conversation in Matthew is hard to follow.
**Classification:** *Time-jump / sequencing ambiguity.*
> **Brent:** "I'm actually a little unclear here. Did they go back across the lake after he had the conversation?"
> **Marty:** "Matthew does change this... Matthew tells us in such a way it's a little mushy and there's a bunch of argument about how to harmonize this section of the Gospels and where to put it."
Related text: [Matthew 16:4–5](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2016%3A4-5&version=NIV).
**Hebraic resolution:** The hosts resolve it by turning to Mark, which they judge to be chronologically cleaner ("Mark does this chronologically much, much better"), and by treating Matthew's ordering as thematic arrangement rather than a reporting error. The "contradiction" dissolves once you stop demanding Western linear chronology.

### 2. Did Jesus "fail" to heal the blind man the first time?
**Problem:** The two-stage healing in [Mark 8:22–26](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208%3A22-26&version=NIV) looks like Jesus couldn't complete a miracle.
**Classification:** *Apparent theological problem (a seemingly deficient Messiah).*
> **Marty:** "Jesus didn't get enough of his little mini-sabbatical. You're right, because he can't even complete his miracles. He's having a hard time getting the whole miracle mojo going." **Brent:** "What are you going to do?" **Marty:** "Or is he? Is there something going on here that Jesus is doing on purpose when he heals but doesn't heal this blind man?"
**Hebraic resolution:** Read inside the rabbinic lesson, the staged healing is intentional pedagogy, not weakness — Jesus dramatizes "do you have eyes but fail to see?" ([Mark 8:18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208%3A18&version=NIV)) by half-healing the man in front of the disciples, then completing the healing.

### 3. "I'm struggling with the whole numerology thing"
**Problem:** The symbolic-number reading feels like a stretch to some listeners.
**Classification:** *Interpretive-method objection (reader resistance).*
> **Marty:** "If you're like, 'Oh man, you're just doubling down on this whole numbers thing.' Yes, but consider the story that follows next."
> **Marty:** "For any of us being, 'Man, I'm struggling with the whole numerology thing,' brothers and sisters, there you go, Jesus just let his disciples have it by saying, 'Do you really not get this? Do you not understand? This was about the numbers.'"
**Hebraic resolution:** Marty grounds the method in the text itself — in [Matthew 16:9–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2016%3A9-10&version=NIV) / [Mark 8:19–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208%3A19-20&version=NIV) Jesus *recites the numbers back* ("the five loaves for the five thousand... the seven loaves for the four thousand") and rebukes the disciples for missing the point, so the numerical reading is the text's own argument, not an imposed one.

## Terminology

- **Remez** — a Hebrew interpretive "hint"; a layer of meaning pointed to by allusion. Marty calls the number scheme "a gigantic remez."
- **Mamzer / mumzer** — the outsider / outcast (one of ambiguous or illegitimate standing); Matthew's recurring agenda is God's care for the mamzer.
- **Yeast = sin** — a consistently negative symbol; "the yeast of the Pharisees/Sadducees/Herod" is their *sinful teaching*, specifically the refusal to welcome the outsider.
- **Sign of Jonah** — here read as a Gentile reference (Jonah sent to Nineveh), *not* the resurrection; Marty notes Matthew omits the "three days and three nights" framing in this instance.
- **The Decapolis** — the Gentile region east of the Sea of Galilee; Jesus' field-trip destination for the feeding of the 4,000 (previously the site of the demoniac and the pigs).
- **The Triangle** — shorthand for the Jewish region around the Sea of Galilee where Jesus primarily ministered ("we were in the triangle of Jesus").
- **Number symbolism (reviewed):** 1 = God; 2 = tablets of Moses; 3 = community; 4 = Gentiles (four corners); 5 = books of Moses; 6 = sinful man; 7 = completion / the seven pagan nations ([Joshua 3:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joshua%203%3A10&version=NIV)); 8 = new creation; 10 (3+7) = complete community; 12 = tribes of Israel; 40 = testing/trial; 50 = the groups Moses organized at Sinai on Jethro's counsel.

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty claims the feeding numbers are "a gigantic remez" and that even the switch from Jewish numbers (5,000) to Gentile numbers (4,000) is intentional. Where do you land on the "I'm struggling with the whole numerology thing" tension — and does Jesus reciting the numbers back in [Mark 8:19–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%208%3A19-20&version=NIV) change your read?
2. The "yeast of the Pharisees" is defined here as the *sin* of refusing the outsider. Where might a modern faith community draw its covenant lines too tightly, teaching people *not* to go to its "Decapolis"?
3. Jesus treats the Bethsaida blind man as a teaching "prop." Does reading miracles primarily as teaching tools (rather than as proofs of power) change how you hear the Gospel healing stories? What's gained or lost?
4. The disciples are read as partners entrusted with "the complete Law," not lackeys. If you take that seriously, what does it imply about your own role in "bringing Kingdom" rather than waiting for it to be handed to you?
5. The episode ends by diagnosing the disciples' blindness ("eyes but fail to see"). Where do you suspect you have "eyes that cannot see" about who is actually *in* the Kingdom?

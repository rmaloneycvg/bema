# A Love Story

BEMA 37 · Session 2
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/2-37-a-love-story.md`
Book: Ruth 1–4 (the whole scroll). Guest Elle Grover Fricks reads from her own translation (transliterating/translating every Hebrew name); the NET is given in the transcript for reference.
Co-host: Brent Billings · Guest co-host: Elle Grover Fricks · (Marty Solomon does **not** appear in this episode)
Core question leaned on throughout: *What do you do when the story doesn't go the way it's supposed to?* — a Moabite outsider from a lineage of shame learns to "flourish in the midst of trauma."

> **Session 2 overarching arc: the prophets — the partner's (Israel's) unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Session 1 built the *partnership*: God and a people bound in covenant, "you create the space and I'll fill it." Session 2 sits in the long era when the partner fails — "the days when the judges judged," an era "not known for being super full of great stuff and holy people" (Elle). This episode advances the one story by showing where God's faithfulness actually surfaces when the national partnership is in collapse: **on the margins, through an outsider.** The whole family embodies the breakdown — famine in the "House of Bread," death in a line called "fruitful," a God named "my God is king" who seems absent (*Elimelech*). Yet God's refusal to quit the partnership runs *underneath* the failure: *chesed* flourishes through Ruth and Boaz "along the margins" of a dark age, and the Moabitess is drawn into the line of David and of Jesus. Where Session 1 asked whether the partner would say *yes*, this episode asks what God does when the partner has largely said *no* — and answers: He keeps redeeming, even "in the messiness," even "in the gray."

## Key Lessons

- **When "the story isn't going the way it's supposed to," you can still pursue flourishing — the book of Ruth is a toolkit for it.** Brent frames the aim up front: *"gather tools for flourishing in the midst of trauma."* Elle: *"What do we do when God is supposed to be king — Elimelech, right? — and instead we find tragedy?"*
- **Tool 1 — catch sight of something good and cling to it with all your might.** Elle: *"Rut catches sight of something that is good and she holds on to it with all of her might... cling to the good thing... dabaq to it, as it says in Hebrew."* By the end she makes it Christological: *"catch a hold of something good... something of Jesus, something that actually feels like life."*
- **Tool 2 — ignore the other voices, even discouraging ones from inside the community.** Even when Na'ami freezes Ruth out and tells the whole town "God has done evil to me," Ruth doesn't quit. Elle: *"This is Na'ami saying that God isn't good. But Rut chooses to hold on to that ball, to ignore all of these voices."*
- **Tool 3 — seek out a beacon of strength in a context you already know.** Ruth goes to a *field* (her known context) and to *Boaz* ("in fierce strength"). Elle: *"Some of us are called to find additional strength beyond our own in the gifting, in the calling that we've already found ourselves."*
- **Tool 4 — pursue a changed destiny with chutzpah instead of going on autopilot.** Elle names the many points where Ruth could have coasted: *"Rut doesn't go on autopilot... She chases that thing down."*
- **Coda — flee to worship.** The outsider "neighbor" (*Rut*) runs to "fierce strength" (*Boaz*) — the name of a temple pillar. Elle: *"we can also flee to worship, flee to the community of God in worship, the abiding place of God."*
- **God keeps redeeming through people who "color outside the lines," meeting them in the gray.** Of the threshing-floor scene Elle says: *"isn't it a more beautiful story to talk about how God uses people even when they color outside the lines of what we expect 'biblical womanhood' to look like... God still is doing a work to redeem this family of women."*
- **Blessing can be a reflex — Boaz is "a man of blessing."** Woken at midnight by a woman in the dark, Boaz's instinct is to bless: *"May our mouths also be full of life as that, where we wake up at midnight, somebody's in our room and we bless them."*
- **This grace flowers "along the margins" of a dark age.** Brent: *"the time of the judges isn't known for being super full of great stuff and holy people. And yet, this is happening along the margins."*

## East/West Lens

*(Only categories the episode actually engages, each with a citation.)*

- **Names carry identity and destiny (Eastern naming vs. Western label).** Elle translates every name because *"God sovereignly works with your parents to choose your name and it's linked to your identity, but also your destiny."* The pericope's meaning sits "right on the surface" for the original listener — *Beyt Lechem* ("House of Bread") in a famine, *Elimelech* ("my God is king") in a tragedy.
- **Story read as a unified whole with long echoes (Eastern intertext vs. Western proof-text).** Boaz's words to Ruth are deliberately *"right out of Genesis 12,"* God's call to Avraham; *Moav* reaches back to Lot's incest in Genesis 19. The scroll is heard against the whole Tanakh, not in isolation.
- **God revealed through familiar cultural imagery (Eastern accommodation).** *Shaddai* is "a name for a spiritual being, which is thought of as a family protector," depicted in "the cultures of the north"; God "meets Avraham" using an image "familiar... from the culture of his home country."
- **Grace as patronage, not a vague feeling (Eastern honor/patron vs. Western sentiment).** *Chen* is "not vague grace" but "a sponsor: somebody who will say, 'I choose you and I'll give you everything you need to walk out your calling.'" Elle names how English flattens "grace" into "elegance" or mere forgiveness.
- **Embodied, physical covenant language.** The *wing* (*kanaf*) is at once the corner of Boaz's garment, the *tallit* wing drawn over bride and groom, and the "wings of refuge" he blessed her under — covenant said with cloth and body, not abstraction.
- **Honor and reputation as communal realities (Eastern collectivist frame).** Ruth's identity as "a Moabitess" is the first thing said of her by the overseer; Boaz protects her name publicly — "the gates of my people will know that you are a powerful woman" — rather than exposing her.

## Recurring Through-lines

*(Motifs genuinely surfaced by this episode — explicit callbacks the hosts name, plus genuinely-implied threads. No motif forced. Registry not consulted per task overrides.)*

- **God's refusal to quit the partnership (Session 2 arc).** With the national partnership in collapse "in the days when the judges judged," God's *chesed* persists on the margins, carrying a Moabite outsider into the line of David. Elle: Na'ami's own turn — *"Blessed be Adonai who has not abandoned his compassion to the living and the dead."*
- **The outsider drawn in / blessing to the nations (callback to Avraham, Genesis 12).** Boaz names Ruth in Avraham's own call-language — *"you left your father, your mother, the land of your birth... that sounds like my people to me."* The Moabitess becomes true family of Avraham.
- **Chesed / covenant loyalty.** The whole book is lived loyalty — Ruth to Na'ami, Boaz to the dead, God to the family — crowned by Na'ami's confession that God "has not abandoned his compassion."
- **A legacy of shame redeemed (Moav → the Messianic line).** *Moav* = "from father," the memory of Lot's incest (Genesis 19); yet the woman "carrying that family story... of shame" ends as great-grandmother of King David and an ancestor of Jesus.
- **"Trust the story" (Session 1, Episode 1).** Elle's repeated counsel to cling, ignore the discouraging voices, and refuse autopilot is trusting-the-story applied to personal trauma: the ending isn't yet in view, so hold on.
- **The Shema / "love your neighbor."** Elle's closing "frosting": *Rut* means "neighbor," and "neighbor love... is part of the Jesus Shema, part of what we're called to."
- **Refuge "under the wings" of God.** Boaz blesses Ruth as one "under whose wings you have come for refuge" (2:12), later echoed when she asks him to "spread your wing over me" (3:9) — human covenant mirroring divine shelter.
- **Fruitfulness vs. barrenness.** A family "of the tribe of Ephrathah... fruitful" meets famine, disease, weakness, and death — the subverted-fruit motif the series keeps pulling on — until God "enabled her to conceive" and the line continues.

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Framing.** Brent introduces Elle Grover Fricks to "zoom in on the mini-narrative of Ruth and gather tools for flourishing in the midst of trauma"; notes Ruth is the smallest book covered so far and urges listeners to read the whole thing.
2. **Why translate the names.** Elle explains she renders every Hebrew name by meaning (as Marty did with *Ya'akov*): names carry "identity... but also your destiny." Her own name means "torch" — she can be illumination or an arsonist. The literary payoff: like "Sloak Butcher" or "Professor Hellsfire," Hebrew names tell you on the surface who's who.
3. **Ruth 1:1–5 — a triple brokenness.** Elle reads her translation and unpacks the names: *Beyt Lechem* ("House of Bread") in a famine (irony), *Yehuda* ("praise"), *Moav* ("from father," Lot's incest = a legacy of shame), *Elimelech* ("my God is king" — is he?), *Na'ami* ("pleasant"), *Machlon* ("diseased"), *Khilion* ("weak"), *Ephrathah* ("fruitful"), *Rut* ("neighbor"). "A lineage of brokenness marrying into a family brokenness and a time of national brokenness." The existential questions these raise are "not totally foreign... for the church today."
4. **Brent on geography.** Moav is "on the other side of the Dead Sea," modern Jordan; the journey from Beyt Lechem (just south of Yerushalayim) wasn't far — raising Elle's question of whether the family even sickened on the move.
5. **Ruth 1:6–17 — the road back and Ruth's vow.** Brent reads (with the Na'ami pronunciation bit). Na'ami urges both daughters-in-law home with a blessing; Orpah kisses her goodbye; **Ruth clings** ("Your people, my people. Your God, my God").
6. **Tool 1 — cling to the good (*dabaq*).** Elle: Ruth "catches sight of something good... and holds on with all her might," embodied in Na'ami and her God. The **"ball, ball, ball!" basketball** illustration from her childhood. "Be like Rut. Cling to that thing."
7. **Ruth 1:18–22 — arrival in Beyt Lechem; "call me Mara."** Brent reads. The whole city is "in a hubbub"; Na'ami says *"Shaddai has dealt much bitterness to me... Adonai has done evil to me."*
8. **Tool 2 — ignore the other voices.** Courage in Hebrew = "fast feet." Na'ami "freezes her out" ("left off speaking with her"); the city reacts; Na'ami's "theology" leans dark ("God has done evil to her"). Elle on *Shaddai* — a northern "family protector" guardian image God used with Avraham, now invoked bitterly. Ruth clings anyway.
9. **Brent's question — why no mention of Ruth in the hubbub?** Elle: that's "the experience of the outsider" — noticed but unnamed, the community unwilling to confront the Moabitess problem (intermarriage, *Moshe*). Brent offers an alternate reading (the town too shocked by Na'ami's reply to ask). Both agree it's likely a mix. Aside: "hubbub" is onomatopoeia in English *and* Hebrew.
10. **Ruth 2:1–13 — the field of Boaz.** Brent reads. Boaz = "in fierce strength," "a hero of force" (*gibor chayil*, like David's mighty men). Ruth seeks *chen* ("patronage") in his eyes. Boaz blesses his workers; the overseer identifies her first as "a Moabitess."
11. **Tool 3 — seek a beacon of strength in a known context.** Ruth is "strategic," not a "damsel in distress"; she goes to fields she knows and to a man of *chayil*. Boaz is "not Moabite blind" — he sees her shame-history *and* names what is good, in Avraham's own Genesis-12 language, and carves out extra space for her (beyond Torah's gleaning requirement).
12. **Brent on the gleaning mechanics.** The overseer has clearly talked with Ruth; she's keeping to "the corners" (leave-the-corners law) — doing what's allowed — before Boaz widens the space further by telling the men to drop extra grain.
13. **Tool 4 — pursue a changed destiny with chutzpah (not autopilot).** Elle lists every exit ramp Ruth refuses (after widowhood, after Na'ami's "no," after Beyt Lechem, after being noticed). "That's not the example that Rut is about to set for us."
14. **The TL;DR of chapters 2–3.** Lunch/pita with "first date energy"; extra grain and no rebuke ("a form of love I can hold on to"); Ruth brings grain to Na'ami; **Na'ami's repentance** ("Blessed be Adonai who has not abandoned his compassion"). Then Na'ami's threshing-floor plan (Ruth 3:3): wash, anoint (only King David also self-anoints — "theological fun"), dress, go down at **Shavuot** (why Ruth is read then).
15. **The threshing floor — the R-rated reading of *gala*.** Elle translates "uncover **from** his feet," arguing *gala* is the nakedness word (Noah's drunkenness; Leviticus) and the scene is a provocative proposition — "the majority of scholarship" agreeing — while explicitly noting BEMA does **not** commend it "as a pursuit of marriage strategy," and that the cultural stakes (a widow needing a household's protection) are real, not greed. "If you choose not to read it that way, that's okay. We can still high-five in heaven."
16. **Chutzpah and risk.** Ruth is "putting herself in danger"; Boaz could ruin her reputation. The beauty: God "meets them there" in the gray and "chooses to continue to be faithful."
17. **Ruth 3:8–13 — midnight; "spread your wing over me."** Boaz wakes ("Who are you?"); Ruth escalates from "tent servant" to **bondservant** (sworn to the family) and asks him to "spread out your wing over me, for you are my redeemer" — *wing* (*kanaf*) as garment-corner, *tallit* wing, and refuge.
18. **The excellence of Boaz.** He declines the proposition, "executes a stunning reversal," blesses her again, names her an *eshet chayil* ("woman of force" — matching his own *gibor chayil*), guards her reputation ("the gates of my people know that you are a woman of force"), and promises the "uno reverse": *"All that you ask for, I will do for you"* — mutual self-giving, "a happily-ever-after postured situation."
19. **Chapter 4 TL;DR.** They marry; a baby is born — grandfather of King David. (The read-aloud NET of ch. 4 supplies the gate/sandal redemption and the genealogy to David.)
20. **The four tools recapped + made Christological.** Catch something good (now: "something of Jesus"), ignore the voices, seek beacons of strength, pursue a changed destiny with chutzpah. "May we all be like Rut. May we all be like Boaz. May we have the light of Na'ami."
21. **Brent's "time of the judges" button — and Elle's coda.** Brent marvels at this "beautiful little story" in the dark era; Elle adds the "frosting": *Rut* = "neighbor" (the Jesus Shema), and *Boaz* is also the name of one of the **two temple pillars** (the other, *Yakin*, "He has established") — so the outsider "flees to the temple of God for strength": a final tool, *flee to worship*.

## Terminology

- **dabaq** (דָּבַק, "cling") — Ruth *clings* to Na'ami (Ruth 1:14). The first tool for flourishing. Elle: *"cling to that thing — dabaq to it, as it says in Hebrew — cling to the good thing."*
- **Shaddai** (שַׁדַּי) — a divine name evoking a "great winged protector" / "family protector," an image from the northern cultures of Avraham's home country that God used in meeting Avraham. Na'ami invokes it with bitter irony. Elle: *"Shaddai is a name for a spiritual being, which is thought of as a family protector... God meets Avraham and He says, 'Hey, I'm a Shaddai.'"*
- **chen** (חֵן, "favor / grace / patronage") — not vague grace but *sponsorship*. Elle: *"she's looking for a sponsor: somebody who will say, 'I choose you and I'll give you everything you need to walk out your calling.' And she says... I'm gonna try and go get patronage from him."*
- **chayil / gibor chayil → eshet chayil** (חַיִל / גִּבּוֹר חַיִל / אֵשֶׁת חַיִל) — "force": strength, military might, resources, valor. Boaz is a *gibor chayil* ("hero of force," the term used of David's mighty men); he later calls Ruth an *eshet chayil* ("woman of force"). Elle: *"he uses the same words about her except for woman of force. Instead of gibor chayil, it's eshet chayil."*
- **courage = "fast feet" (chutzpah)** — the Hebrew for courage connotes quick feet, "willing to run fast to the front lines." Ruth repeatedly refuses "autopilot." Elle: *"Courage in Hebrew means fast feet... You're not second-guessing."*
- **gala** (גָּלָה, "uncover / denude") — the "nakedness" verb (first used of Noah's drunkenness; repeatedly in Leviticus's "do not uncover nakedness"). The basis for Elle's R-rated reading of the threshing floor: *"it doesn't say gala his feet... It says uncover from his feet."*
- **kanaf / "wing"** (כָּנָף) — at once the corner of Boaz's garment, the *tallit* wing drawn over bride and groom, and the "wings" of refuge Boaz blessed her under (2:12). Elle: *"wing in a marriage ceremony, the wing of the prayer shawl goes over both the man and woman... 'Spread out your wing over me, for you are my redeemer.'"*
- **Shavuot** (שָׁבוּעוֹת) — the barley/wheat-harvest holiday that is the setting of the threshing-floor scene; Ruth is the scroll read in the Jewish community at Shavuot. Elle: *"it's a holiday, which is the context of this: Shavuot... that's why this scroll is read in the Jewish community around Shavuot."*
- **the translated names** — *Beyt Lechem* "House of Bread," *Yehuda* "praise," *Moav* "from father," *Elimelech* "my God is king," *Na'ami* "pleasant/good," *Machlon* "diseased," *Khilion* "weak," *Ephrathah* "fruitful," *Rut* "neighbor," *Boaz* "in fierce strength." Elle: *"God sovereignly works with your parents to choose your name and it's linked to your identity, but also your destiny."*
- **Boaz & Yakin (the two temple pillars)** — *Boaz* appears elsewhere only as one of Solomon's two temple pillars; the other, *Yakin*, means "He has established." The outsider flees to "fierce strength" — i.e., to the temple/worship. Elle: *"there's the pillar of Yakin, which means 'He has established'... and then there's the pillar of Boaz, and she's an outsider and she flees to the temple of God for strength."*

## Illustrations & Stories

- **"Ball, ball, ball, ball!" — childhood basketball (Elle).** A tall, stick-armed pee-wee player taught by her dad to throw herself on a loose ball "with all your might and possibly your teeth," curled over it in the fetal position until the ref calls it hers — the picture of *dabaq*, clinging to the good thing (Na'ami and her God).
- **"First date energy" at lunch (Elle).** Boaz inviting Ruth to share pita "definitely has first date energy," even if "it can be hard to pick that up from the text."
- **A love "I can hold on to": not getting rebuked (Elle).** Boaz ensuring Ruth gets extra grain *and* isn't rebuked — "ugh. That's a form of love I can hold on to right there."
- **"Uno reverse" (Elle).** Na'ami tells Ruth to do whatever Boaz says; Boaz flips it — "All that you ask for, I will do for you." "That's a marriage... two people laying their life down."
- **"High-five in heaven" (Elle).** How she holds the disputed threshing-floor reading with humility: "If I get to heaven and I'm totally wrong about this, that is okay... We can still high-five in heaven."
- **"It would be a bummer to be Boaz in heaven" (Elle & Brent).** A wry aside about everyone in heaven knowing the midnight threshing-floor story — the human awkwardness inside a holy text.

## Discussion Questions

1. Elle's four tools for flourishing in trauma are: cling to something good, ignore the other voices, seek a beacon of strength in a context you know, and pursue a changed destiny with chutzpah. Which of the four is hardest for you right now, and why?
2. The names announce a gap between what the family is *supposed* to be ("House of Bread," "my God is king," "fruitful") and what they're living (famine, death, weakness). Where is that gap most honest in your own life or community — and does naming it help?
3. Na'ami says, "God has done evil to me," and later confesses, "Blessed be Adonai who has not abandoned his compassion." How do you hold space for someone whose trauma has them speaking from the first place without rushing them to the second?
4. Ruth is "noticed but unnamed" by the whole city — "the experience of the outsider." Where have you seen (or been) the person everyone is aware of but no one will actually name and welcome? What would Boaz-style welcome have looked like?
5. Elle reads the threshing floor as a risky, "color-outside-the-lines" proposition that God still redeems — and explicitly allows the other reading. How does it change (or not change) the story for you if God works His redemption "in the messiness, in the gray"?
6. In Session 1 the question was whether the human partner would say *yes*; in Session 2 the partner has largely failed. What does it mean to you that God's faithfulness surfaces here through a *Moabite outsider* "along the margins" rather than through the center of the nation?
7. Boaz is "a man of blessing" whose reflex, even when startled awake, is to bless and protect. Whose reputation could you guard this week the way Boaz guarded Ruth's?

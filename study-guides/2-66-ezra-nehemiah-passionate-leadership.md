# BEMA 66 — Ezra/Nehemiah: Passionate Leadership — Study Guide

**Session:** 2 (The Prophets)
**Episode:** 66 · "Ezra/Nehemiah — Passionate Leadership"
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings
**Primary text:** [Ezra 7:6–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezra+7%3A6-10&version=NIV) and [Nehemiah 13:23–25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Nehemiah+13%3A23-25&version=NIV), read as one continuous story

---

## Where This Sits in the One Story

Session 1 built the picture of **partnership**: God rescues a people out of Egypt and invites them to become a kingdom of priests who join his restoration project. Session 2 turns to the **Prophets**, and the arc of this stage is the covenant partner's (Israel's) **unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Most of the session has traced that unfaithfulness through warning, woe, and exile. This episode moves into the **remnant period** — the aftermath — when a chastened people begins, in difficult waves, to come *home*.

That is the shape of God's refusal to quit at this stage: Babylon has fallen to **Persia**, **Cyrus** decrees the return, and the partner — not the "A+ star players" but the "below-average, C-minus" remnant — starts the long, grinding work of rebuilding. The partner was unfaithful enough to be carted off; God is faithful enough to open a road back. But the return is not a triumphant parade. "Very few people find themselves filled with joy on their way back home," and the rebuilding "is going to be a struggle all the way through."

Relative to Session 1's partnership, this episode advances the one story by showing *what partnership looks like after the collapse* — not restoration of a golden age, but a bruised people needing **leaders** to carry them forward. The theme Marty names is **passionate leadership**, and three figures (Zerubbabel, Ezra, Nehemiah) model three utterly different ways God raises up people to keep the partnership moving when it would be easier to quit. The story is explicitly left unfinished: the temple will be rebuilt and struggled over "four times, five times" and will not reach Jesus-era grandeur until Herod the Great, centuries later.

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **Why one episode for two books** — the Jewish tradition of reading Ezra and Nehemiah as close companion volumes; the Masoretic text treats them as a single book; a continuous (not merely parallel) narrative of the same return.
2. **Marty's posture toward the text** — still studying it; the real value is stepping back for "big observations" and historical context rather than diving into every passage; it "can be somewhat problematic at times."
3. **Setting up the remnant: Babylon falls to Persia** — Babylon overthrown by Persia; Persia, shaped by the Greeks, rules by partnership rather than brute force; the suzerain-vassal relationship evolved; the movie *300* named (not recommended) as the opposite portrait.
4. **Cyrus's decree and the letters** — Cyrus lets the Jews return as Persian *method* ("a strong people that bow the knee to Persia"), not mere generosity; this explains the barrage of loyalty-testing letters in Ezra; the returnees are now called *Jews* (Judeans), not Israelites.
5. **Where Ezra/Nehemiah sit in the canon** — in the Ketuvim (Writings), immediately before Chronicles, not among the Nevi'im.
6. **Not a joyful mass homecoming — waves of return** — the childhood image of a big celebration corrected; return came in difficult waves: Zerubbabel, then Ezra, then Nehemiah, likely more.
7. **Zerubbabel the pioneer** — the apostle-like one who goes first; "somebody always needs to go first."
8. **Ezra the shepherd/exhorter (Ezra 7)** — Brent reads Ezra 7; Ezra 7:10 as the "pursue, model, teach" verse treasured at Impact Campus Ministries; Talmudic asides (would have given the law like Moses; disciple of Baruch, scribe of Jeremiah); leads with his *voice* — letters, a town-hall meeting over intermarriage, a shared list.
9. **Nehemiah the prophet (Nehemiah 13)** — Brent reads Nehemiah's "pulled out their hair/beards"; no town-hall meeting, fiery Elijah-like chutzpah; Marty's careful caveat that this is *not* a model for leadership today, given real abusive-leadership problems in the church.
10. **Closing observation 1 — hardship always comes** — resistance and trouble are the one constant; the Exodus-then-desert and baptism-then-wilderness testing pattern; good leaders help people endure.
11. **Closing observation 2 — the leaders could not be more different** — Ezra encouragement and tact, Nehemiah fire and inspiration; both blessed because each works under his own anointing.
12. **Closing observation 3 — all of them were passionate** — Nehemiah's passion is obvious; Ezra's quiet daily passion (another letter, another wait on a Persian king) and Zerubbabel's are just as real.
13. **The charge to the listener** — everyone has some capacity for leadership (boss, parent, mentor, the person someone is quietly watching); be the leader God made you to be, especially in a jaded, cynical season.
14. **The theme and the long road ahead** — theme = "passionate leadership" / "rebuilding God's people"; 6th century BC (c. 586–530s BC); the temple rebuilt and struggled over repeatedly until Herod the Great.

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 2 arc — the covenant partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **God's refusal to quit looks, in the remnant period, like a road home that still has to be walked.** The unfaithful partner was exiled; now Cyrus's decree opens the return — but God does not airlift anyone back. The partnership resumes as *hard work* done by an unimpressive remnant.
   - *Marty:* "Cyrus... made a decree that allows the Jews to go back home. This is the Persian way. This isn't just a stroke of generosity on the part of Cyrus."
   - *Marty:* "It is not going to be easy to go back and rebuild... It wasn't one big mass Exodus back home."

2. **Rebuilding the partnership is defined by hardship, and that is to be expected, not resented.** The single constant across every leader is struggle; Scripture builds the pattern that mountaintop moments are followed by testing. The arc's "God refuses to quit" is matched by a people who must refuse to quit.
   - *Marty:* "please note that resistance, hardship, and troubles will come. If you want to go rebuild anything in your life, it is not going to be easy."
   - *Marty:* "In each story, the one constant piece is hardship... Good leaders help people overcome obstacles."

3. **God advances the one story through radically different leaders, each working under his own anointing.** Ezra the tactful shepherd and Nehemiah the fiery prophet could not be more opposite, yet both are blessed — because the point is not a leadership template but faithfulness to how God made you.
   - *Marty:* "these leaders couldn't be more different in their leadership styles and personalities... both leaders are blessed in their work by God. Why? Because they are working as God has created them to work."
   - *Marty:* "Ezra leads with encouragement and tact; Nehemiah leads with fire and inspiration."

4. **Passion — loud or quiet — is what keeps the partnership moving when it would be easier to quit.** The arc hinges on refusal to quit; here that refusal is embodied as passion, including the unglamorous passion to write "another letter only to wait for a delayed response from some Persian king."
   - *Marty:* "they were passionate. This went for all of them. It's easy to see the passion of Nehemiah, but don't think for a second that Ezra didn't lead each and every morning with passion."
   - *Marty:* "You don't lead a charge of the remnant without passion and an undying commitment to persevere."

5. **The remnant's rebuilding is real but unfinished — the story is explicitly not resolved here.** The partner is home, but the restored temple of Jesus' day is centuries off; the episode refuses to tie a triumphant bow, keeping the one story moving forward rather than declaring the arc complete.
   - *Marty:* "they're not going to rebuild some great world. It's going to be a struggle all the way through. They're going to rebuild this temple four times, five times."
   - *Brent:* "They have quite a ways to go before we get to what we know of Jerusalem in Jesus's day."

## East/West Lens

Only categories genuinely engaged by this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Reading for the big idea over systematizing every detail (East).** Marty deliberately steps back for "big observations" of the whole story instead of dissecting individual passages, trusting the shape of the narrative to teach.
  - *Marty:* "I've always been moved by just stepping back and making some big observations of the story on Ezra and Nehemiah."

- **Reading the text in its historical and cultural moment (East).** The intermarriage material and Nehemiah's violence are read as a different point in "the evolution of human consciousness," not lifted out of context as a timeless rule.
  - *Marty:* "This is a different period of history, this is a different period of the evolution of human consciousness. Let's remember that as we look at this. This is not a justification for how a leader should act today."

- **Hebrew names and categories carry meaning (East).** The placement of Ezra/Nehemiah in the **Ketuvim** (Writings) rather than the **Nevi'im** (Prophets) is treated as significant canonical information, not trivia.
  - *Brent:* "Ezra and Nehemiah are still prophets though, right?" / *Marty:* "I'm pretty sure Ezra and Nehemiah end up in the Ketuvim. They're writings and not seen as Nevi'im."

- **Receiving tradition's midrashic imagination (East).** Marty leans on Talmudic asides — Ezra "would have given the law" like Moses, and the Baruch→Jeremiah discipleship chain — as meaningful lenses on who Ezra was, rather than dismissing them as non-literal.
  - *Marty:* "The Talmud says he had such a passion for the law that if Moses had not given the law on Mount Sinai, Ezra would have given the law here at the return of the exiles."

- **Truth carried through the whole-story pattern (East).** The lesson that hardship follows breakthrough is established intertextually — Exodus-then-desert and baptism-then-wilderness — rather than by proposition.
  - *Marty:* "The Bible affirms this experience from the Old Testament, all the way through Jesus's ministry and the gospels."

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension states the problem, tags its type, cites the transcript, links the passage, and gives a Hebraic resolution (or marks it open).

1. **How can Nehemiah's violent, hair-pulling, curse-calling response be in Scripture as "leadership"?**
   - *Type:* Moral/ethical difficulty (abusive-looking behavior in an exemplar).
   - *Problem:* Nehemiah beats men and pulls out their hair over intermarriage — behavior that reads as straightforwardly abusive, especially in an age wrestling with abusive church leadership.
   - *Cited:* *Brent (reading Neh. 13):* "I rebuked them and called curses down on them. I beat some of the men and pulled out their hair." / *Marty:* "I don't want to make light of that... This is not a justification for how a leader should act today."
   - *Passage:* [Nehemiah 13:23–25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Nehemiah+13%3A23-25&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* Read the account in its own moment, not as prescription. Marty resolves it by locating it "a period 2,500 years ago" in a different stage of human consciousness — the text *reports* Nehemiah's fiery temperament (and names its anointing) without *endorsing* the method for today. The constructive takeaway is the passion and refusal to quit, not the beard-pulling.

2. **Why does intermarriage matter so much that both leaders confront it?**
   - *Type:* Unstated cultural assumption (modern readers "get hung up on the intermarriage thing").
   - *Problem:* To modern ears the intermarriage crackdown sounds like ethnic exclusion; it is unclear why it would be the flashpoint for the whole rebuilding project.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "people get hung up on the intermarriage thing, but if you're going to go back and rebuild, intermarriage is probably not a great way to get started... if you're trying to get back and reestablish lineage and genealogy."
   - *Passage:* [Ezra 7:6–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezra+7%3A6-10&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* Framed as a rebuilding problem, not a purity-of-blood boast. The remnant is trying to *re-establish* an identity and lineage that exile nearly erased; wholesale intermarriage at the very start would dissolve the thing they came home to restore. The episode names the tension but does not fully exposit it — held as **open — dance with it** beyond this rebuilding rationale.

3. **If Cyrus "graciously" sent the Jews home, why is the book of Ezra so full of suspicious, loyalty-testing letters?**
   - *Type:* Apparent contradiction (generosity vs. surveillance).
   - *Problem:* A benevolent decree of return sits oddly next to constant accusatory correspondence questioning the Jews' loyalty to Persia.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "This isn't just a stroke of generosity on the part of Cyrus. This is his method... Persia will not stand for a little rebellion beginning at the crossroads of the earth."
   - *Passage:* [Ezra 7:6–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezra+7%3A6-10&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The two fit once Persian strategy is understood. The return is a suzerain-vassal arrangement, not sentiment — Persia wants a loyal, productive people at a strategic crossroads. Hence the relationship must be constantly managed through letters, with opponents stoking doubt and the Jews writing to "keep him happy."

## Terminology

- **Ketuvim** — the "Writings," the third division of the Hebrew canon; where Ezra and Nehemiah land, immediately before Chronicles.
  - *Marty:* "I'm pretty sure Ezra and Nehemiah end up in the Ketuvim. They're writings."
- **Nevi'im** — the "Prophets," the canonical division Ezra and Nehemiah are *not* in, despite being prophetic figures.
  - *Marty:* "They're writings and not seen as Nevi'im."
- **suzerain-vassal** — the ancient covenant/treaty form (a dominant lord over a subordinate people), recalled here as the evolved template for how Persia ruled by partnership.
  - *Brent:* "Suzerain-vassal." / *Marty:* "If you can establish all of the suzerain-vassal relationships where you're the clear suzerain with the upper hand."
- **remnant** — the returning portion of the people in this period of history; not the elite but the "below-average, C-minus" survivors.
  - *Marty:* "we got into the remnant period of history last episode."
- **Jews / Judeans** — the term now used for the returning people, distinguished from "Israelites" because the returnees are Judeans come home.
  - *Marty:* "they're now Jews, they're no longer Israelites... Judeans have become the reference of Jews."
- **chutzpah** — brazen, fiery audacity; the quality Marty attaches to Nehemiah's "Elijah-like fire."
  - *Marty:* "Nehemiah is a prophet. He's got that fiery chutzpah, Elijah-like fire."
- **midrash / midrashic** — Jewish interpretive tradition; the source of the Talmudic "tidbits" about Ezra.
  - *Marty:* "a couple of midrashic tidbits from the Talmud."

## Illustrations & Images

- **Three leaders, three archetypes: pioneer, shepherd/exhorter, prophet.** The organizing image of the episode — Zerubbabel goes first, Ezra gathers and teaches, Nehemiah brings fire.
  - *Marty:* "If Zerubbabel is a pioneer, Ezra is the exhorter... then there's Nehemiah."
- **Ezra 7:10 — "pursue, model, teach."** The treasured verse read as a three-fold pattern: study the law, walk it out, teach it.
  - *Marty:* "we talk about pursuing, modeling, and teaching. If we need a verse to go to, we go to Ezra 7:10."
- **Ezra the town-hall shepherd who "leads with his voice."** Ezra confronting intermarriage not by force but by gathering everyone, talking it through, and compiling a shared list.
  - *Marty:* "he calls a town hall meeting, essentially. We all get together and we all talk about it." / *Brent:* "As a shepherd though, he uses his voice."
- **Nehemiah pulling out hair/beards.** The jarring image of Nehemiah's confrontation — the older translation's "I pulled out their beards" — as the opposite of a town-hall meeting.
  - *Marty:* "The old translation used to say, 'I pulled out their beards.' Nehemiah does not take the same approach that Ezra does."
- **Ezra as second-generation disciple of Jeremiah.** The Talmudic discipleship chain — Ezra under Baruch, Baruch the scribe of Jeremiah — picturing where Ezra's passion to restore the God–people relationship came from.
  - *Marty:* "They say that Ezra is a second-generation disciple of Jeremiah, which is why he has the passion he has to restore the relationship between God and his people."
- **Exodus-then-desert / baptism-then-wilderness.** The pattern-image that breakthrough is immediately followed by testing, used to normalize the hardship of rebuilding.
  - *Marty:* "Jesus gets baptized and he immediately goes out to the desert... Well, what did the people of Israel do? They get this amazing Exodus experience and they go out to the... desert."
- **"The one man who doesn't realize that his colleague is watching him."** The closing image that everyone is a leader of *something*, often unawares.
  - *Marty:* "You might be the one man who doesn't realize that his colleague is watching him. You are a leader of something."

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty names three leaders — the pioneer (Zerubbabel), the shepherd/exhorter (Ezra), and the fiery prophet (Nehemiah) — and insists all three were "blessed... because they are working as God has created them to work." Which of the three is closest to how God made *you* to lead, and where have you tried to lead like someone you're not?
2. The one constant across every leader was hardship: "if you want to go rebuild anything in your life, it is not going to be easy." Where are you in a rebuilding season right now, and how does it reframe things to expect the testing instead of being surprised by it?
3. Ezra "leads with his voice" — letters, a town-hall meeting, a shared list — while Nehemiah leads with fire. Marty is careful that Nehemiah's hair-pulling is "not a justification for how a leader should act today." How do you discern what to carry forward from a biblical figure and what belongs to its own moment in history?
4. Marty points to the *quiet* passion of Ezra — getting up each morning to "write another letter only to wait for a delayed response from some Persian king." Where is God calling you to an unglamorous, un-applauded passion that nobody will put on a highlight reel?
5. "You are a leader of something" — a parent, a mentor, the colleague someone is quietly watching. Who is watching you that you may not have noticed, and what would it look like to lead them "as the leader God created you to be"?

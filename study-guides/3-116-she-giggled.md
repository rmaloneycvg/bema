# BEMA 116: She Giggled — Study Guide

**Session 3 (The Gospels) · Episode 116**
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings
**Primary text:** Matthew 14:22–36 (Peter walking on the water)

---

## Overview

This is a format-break episode. Rather than the usual back-and-forth exegesis, Marty reads aloud a chapter he wrote years ago for a private book he penned for his daughter Abigail — *When Two-Year-Olds Preach* — titled "She Giggled." The chapter braids two things together: Jesus's teaching that we must have "faith like a child," and the story of Peter walking on the water, read through the first-century lens of rabbi-and-*talmid* discipleship. Marty's governing claim is that Jesus's "you of little faith, why did you doubt?" is not a scolding of a failed task but an affirmation of Peter's ability — the rabbi believes his disciple can do what the rabbi does. He grounds the reframe in an extended personal illustration: watching his newly-walking daughter fall again and again on a park slope and, instead of crying, *giggle* and get back up.

**Where this sits in the one story:** Session 1 established the partnership — God calling human partners to walk with him and bear his image. Session 2 traced the partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit on that partnership. Session 3 shows **the King/Messiah embodying the story in person**. This episode advances the arc by locating the renewed partnership *inside the rabbi-disciple relationship*: the Messiah who embodies the story calls disciples not because they are flawless but because he genuinely believes they can become like him. Where Session 2 dwelt on human failure and divine faithfulness, this episode shows the Messiah pressing that same faithfulness one step further — he not only refuses to quit on his partner, he actively has *faith in* his partner, and asks the partner to risk the water.

## Episode Flow

A segment-by-segment backbone of the conversation. Coverage is verified below.

1. **Framing the format-break** — Brent sets up that this is "a little bit of a different episode"; Marty will interact with the Peter story by reading his own written chapter.
2. **The backstory of the book** — Marty explains writing *When Two-Year-Olds Preach* (for Abigail) and *When Five-Year-Olds Preach* (for Ezekiel), drawn from observing his toddlers and reading Scripture through that lens.
3. **Reading the passage** — Brent reads Matthew 14:22–36 (the end of Matthew 14).
4. **"She Giggled," part 1 — faith like a child reconsidered** — Marty challenges the flannelgraph/trivial reading of "faith like a child"; a rabbi who stages a visible lesson means it to carry depth.
5. **Re-imagining the "become like children" scene** — Marty reconstructs a possible all-day scene: Jesus and disciples spending a whole day with village children before delivering the teaching.
6. **The park illustration — "She giggled"** — the extended story of his daughter learning to walk up a slope, falling ~20–30 times, giggling instead of crying, adjusting, and finally making it to the top.
7. **The Peter story and Western expectation** — our Western minds are amazed, then skeptical, then satisfied when Peter "fails as our representative."
8. **Naming sources (so as not to plagiarize)** — Ray Vander Laan, Rob Bell (*Velvet Elvis*, NOOMA "Dust").
9. **First-century discipleship explained** — Torah study, becoming a *talmid*, the rabbi's call as a vote of confidence ("I believe you can become just like me").
10. **The reframed sinking** — Peter wants to be like his rabbi; when he sinks, the question is *faith in whom?* — not Jesus (who's fine) but Peter's faith in himself.
11. **The reframed rebuke** — "you of little faith, why did you doubt?" read as "I believe in you; I'd never have called you if you couldn't do it."
12. **The Rob Bell quote** — God has a high view of people; "Jesus believes in me."
13. **Marty's personal application** — losing faith in oneself; innocence tied to confidence; not narcissism or humanism but *imago Dei* worth.
14. **"I want to learn how to giggle"** — getting up, falling, getting up; not trivializing sin; working out salvation; God as a father on a park bench, not a judge in a boat.
15. **Closing** — Brent notes it lands differently now that he's a parent; show-notes mention of the 2015 blog posts and the 2011–2012 origin; Marty recommends *Prototype* by Jonathan Martin.

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 3 arc — the Messiah embodying the one story and re-forming his partner through discipleship — and backed by a transcript citation.

### 1. "Faith like a child" is not trivial — a staged rabbinic lesson carries depth
Marty refuses the flannelgraph reading. A rabbi who sets up a visible scenario intends weight, not cuteness.
> **Marty:** "If a rabbi such as Jesus takes the time to set up a scenario, and then uses it as a pitcher of spiritual formation, the listeners had better take note. Visible lessons from the rabbi are never intended to be empty or easy for that matter."

In the Session 3 frame, the Messiah's smallest staged moments are kingdom pedagogy; nothing he does on-camera is filler.

### 2. The rabbi's call is itself a vote of confidence
First-century discipleship means the rabbi has already judged you capable of becoming like him. Being chosen *is* the affirmation.
> **Marty:** "If a rabbi chose you as a disciple, he was in essence saying to you, 'I believe you have what it takes to become just like me.'"
> **Marty:** "You know that you can do it because if you could not have done it, the rabbi would have never called you. The rabbi's call is his affirmation in your ability and your potential."

This is the renewed partnership of the one story made concrete: the King does not merely tolerate his partners; he stakes his movement on their ability.

### 3. Peter sinks because he loses faith in *himself*, not in Jesus
The pivotal reframe. Jesus is not sinking; the failure is Peter's loss of confidence in his own calling.
> **Marty:** "Whom does he lose faith in, Jesus? Jesus is not sinking. Jesus is doing just fine. Does Peter lose faith in Jesus's ability to help him walk on water or does Peter lose faith in himself?"

### 4. The "rebuke" is actually the rabbi driving his belief in the disciple
"You of little faith, why did you doubt?" is read not as a scolding of failure but as an insistence on Peter's capacity.
> **Marty:** "Is this actually a rabbinical scolding of Peter's failure to accomplish his task, or is this question of Jesus actually driving his belief in Peter? 'Peter, if you didn't have what it takes to walk on water, I never would have called you out... I will never ask you to do something you cannot do.'"

### 5. God believes in us — not humanism, but *imago Dei* worth
Marty guards the claim carefully: this is not self-salvation but the recognition that God judged his partner worth saving.
> **Marty:** "I have been told that I need to believe in Jesus, which is a good thing, but what I'm learning is that Jesus believes in me."
> **Marty:** "...faith in myself that recognizes that I'm made in the image of God, the kind of faith that might actually be willing to believe there must be something worth loving and worth saving if God was willing to save it for the story of the cross."

This directly advances the arc: the hope rests "in the power of the resurrected Christ," yet the Messiah's embodiment of the story includes entrusting the mission to fallible partners.

### 6. Faithfulness looks like falling, getting up, and giggling
The chapter's thesis: maturity in the kingdom is not the absence of failure but the refusal to be immobilized by it.
> **Marty:** "I have some falling to do, and I have plenty of getting up to do. I have a salvation that needs to be worked out with fear and trembling, and it's going to take some effort..."
> **Marty:** "I want to learn how to giggle."

### 7. The disciple's duty is to mimic the rabbi's every move
The reason Peter steps out at all: a *talmid* imitates the rabbi in everything. If the rabbi walks on water, the disciple wants to walk on water.
> **Marty:** "The path of a disciple was a path of memorizing the rabbi's teachings, taking on the rabbi's set of interpretations, and most importantly, becoming just like the rabbi."
> **Marty:** "If Jesus is walking on the water, what does Peter want to do? He wants to be just like his rabbi."

## East / West Lens

Only the interpretive categories actually engaged in this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Rabbi/*talmid* discipleship as the reading key (Eastern):** The whole reinterpretation depends on recovering the first-century cultural institution of discipleship rather than reading the scene with modern assumptions.
  > **Marty:** "The first-century concept of discipleship was such that the most successful thing you could do in the Jewish culture, the thing they valued the most was the study of Torah."

- **Naming and critiquing the Western reading reflex (East/West contrast):** Marty explicitly diagnoses how "our Western minds" process (and secretly expect to fail) the Peter story.
  > **Marty:** "Our Western minds are blown away as we consider the possibility of a man pursuing Jesus out onto the waves of the Sea of Galilee... Then just as we're beginning to become skeptical, Peter meets all of the worldly expectations and succumbs to the failure we've all been wishing he would defeat as our representative."

- **Lived observation / narrative formation over propositional lesson (Eastern):** Truth is derived by *watching* — children, a daughter on a slope — not by abstracting a doctrine; the day-long imagined scene reinforces embodied, time-soaked teaching.
  > **Marty:** "Perhaps this wasn't merely a two-minute lesson that day... I have discovered there are great lessons to be learned from watching children."

## Narrative Tensions

Surfaced problems, each classified, cited, verse-linked where relevant, and resolved Hebraically (or left open).

### 1. How long did the "become like children" teaching actually take?
**Classification: time-jump / unstated information.** The Gospel does not tell us how long the gathering lasted; Marty's Western instinct had filled the gap with a 20-second children's-sermon.
> **Marty:** "How long does this gathering and discussion take place? We're not told. I have always assumed that Jesus takes about 20 seconds to gather some kids up..."
**Verse:** [Matthew 18:1–4 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+18%3A1-4&version=NIV)
**Hebraic resolution:** Marty re-reads the silence through rabbinic pedagogy — a staged, possibly day-long immersion with the children — so the "faith like a child" lesson becomes substantial formation, not a throwaway.

### 2. Whom does Peter actually lose faith in?
**Classification: logic gap / contested interpretation.** The default reading says "Peter lost faith," but never specifies the object of that faith.
> **Marty:** "The answer for many of us is that Peter loses faith. He sees the wind and the waves and he loses faith. That is correct, but as Bell asks, 'Whom does he lose faith in, Jesus?'"
**Verse:** [Matthew 14:28–31 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+14%3A28-31&version=NIV)
**Hebraic resolution:** Within the rabbi/*talmid* frame, the object is Peter's confidence in *himself* and his calling — not Jesus's power — so the sinking is a crisis of self-doubt, not of christology.

### 3. Is Jesus's question a rebuke or an affirmation?
**Classification: contested interpretation / tone ambiguity.** The same words ("you of little faith, why did you doubt?") can read as scolding or as encouragement.
> **Marty:** "Is this actually a rabbinical scolding of Peter's failure to accomplish his task, or is this question of Jesus actually driving his belief in Peter?"
**Verse:** [Matthew 14:31 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+14%3A31&version=NIV)
**Hebraic resolution:** Read through the logic of the rabbi's call (the rabbi would never have called Peter out if Peter couldn't do it), the question functions as the rabbi *insisting on* his disciple's capacity.

### 4. Doesn't "God believes in me" slide into narcissism or humanism?
**Classification: theological guardrail / potential misreading.** Marty anticipates the objection and heads it off.
> **Marty:** "I'm not talking about a narcissistic faith that seems to elevate my standing in God's created order and lacks humility. I'm certainly not trying to promote some humanistic worldview that seems to assert that the answer to all of our ills somehow lies within us."
**Resolution:** The faith he means is anchored in *imago Dei* and in the cross — the hope lies "in the power of the resurrected Christ" — so human worth is a gift of the Creator's valuation, not a bootstrap.

### 5. Doesn't celebrating "falling and giggling" trivialize sin?
**Classification: ethical tension / potential misreading.** The giggle imagery could be heard as cheap grace.
> **Marty:** "I would never suggest that we trivialize sin for even a moment. I'm not saying that failure is somehow okay and God doesn't care about our success. I'm not one of the Jesus followers who seems to whip God's grace around like it's a get out of jail free card..."
**Verse:** [Philippians 2:12–13 (NIV)](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Philippians+2%3A12-13&version=NIV)
**Resolution:** The point is not that failure doesn't matter but that being *immobilized* by failure is itself the problem; the salvation "worked out with fear and trembling" still requires getting back up.

## Terminology

- **talmid** (pl. **talmidim**) — a disciple; a student a rabbi has chosen because the rabbi believes "you can become just like me." The call itself is the rabbi's vote of confidence in the student's ability and potential.
  > **Marty:** "One of the pinnacle experiences of the Jewish school system was being given the opportunity to become a talmid, or as we say it, a 'disciple.'"

- **Rabbi's call as affirmation** — the technical idea that selection into discipleship is not neutral enrollment but a declared judgment that the student has "what it takes."
  > **Marty:** "The rabbi's call is his affirmation in your ability and your potential."

- **Faith like a child** — reframed from sentimental cuteness to the toddler's innocence-tied-to-confidence: the willingness to fall, giggle, and get up because dad and mom are there and you are loved.
  > **Marty:** "One of the things that I'm noticing about children is they have incredible faith in themselves. They know that dad is there, and they know that mom is there. They know they are loved..."

- **"Covered in the dust of the rabbi"** — the discipleship image (sourced from Vander Laan) that a disciple follows so closely he is covered in the dust kicked up by the rabbi's feet.
  > **Marty:** "Ray Vander Laan has an excellent treatment of this subject... The lesson is called In the Dust of the Rabbi."

## Illustrations & Images

- **The daughter on the park slope ("She Giggled")** — the governing illustration: a one-year-old learning to walk uphill, falling ~20–30 times, giggling rather than crying, adjusting her technique, and finally reaching the top.
  > **Marty:** "She fell. She got up. She fell. She giggled... Without exaggerating, I can honestly say this process repeated itself a good 20 times before anything changed."

- **Learning to walk as the great parental milestone** — Marty contrasts the thrill of first steps with teeth or weight, naming the "pure thrill of those first five or six steps."
  > **Marty:** "There's nothing that compares to the ability of walking... nothing compares to the pure thrill of those first five or six steps."

- **The re-imagined all-day scene with the village children** — a reconstructed scenario in which Jesus and the disciples spend an entire day playing with children before the teaching lands.
  > **Marty:** "I imagine Jesus and his disciples spending the day with the children, telling stories, playing games, maybe even taking naps..."

- **Rabbi followed into the restroom** — the vivid image of disciples mimicking the rabbi so completely they trail him everywhere.
  > **Marty:** "Some Jewish scholars say that they have seen a rabbi proceed into a restroom... and in his wake are 10 or 12 young disciples."

- **God on a park bench vs. God in the boat** — the closing contrast between a God who scolds "you of little faith" from the boat and a Father who watches his child learn to walk from a bench.
  > **Marty:** "I wonder if God would rather sit in a boat and say, 'You of little faith,' or if He'd rather sit on a park bench and watch His child learn how to walk."

- **Jesus grabbing the chin** — the image of the rabbi lifting the downcast disciple's face to meet his gaze and saying, "You can do this."
  > **Marty:** "I picture Jesus grabbing me by the chin and jerking my head up... he looks into my soul... He says to me, 'You can do this.'"

- **The life preserver and climbing back into the boat** — the posture of defeated resignation that stays "safe" rather than risking the water again.
  > **Marty:** "We grabbed for our life preserver, we climb back into the boat, and we hold our gaze on the floor as we take the scolding that we knew we had coming."

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty reframes "you of little faith, why did you doubt?" from a scolding into the rabbi *insisting* that Peter is capable. How does hearing that line as belief-in-you rather than disappointment-in-you change the way you read the Peter story — and the way you imagine God speaking to you after a failure?

2. In first-century discipleship, being *called* was itself the rabbi's declaration that "you have what it takes to become just like me." Where in your life have you assumed God is only tolerating you, when the invitation might actually be a vote of confidence?

3. Marty asks whether Peter lost faith in Jesus or in *himself*. When you "start to sink," which faith tends to fail first for you — and what difference does it make to name the right one?

4. The daughter on the slope falls twenty-plus times and *giggles* instead of crying. Marty guards this against trivializing sin while still saying he's "tired of being immobilized by [his] failures." What's the difference between taking failure seriously and being paralyzed by it, and where do you need to "learn how to giggle"?

5. Marty contrasts a God who says "you of little faith" from the boat with a Father who watches his child learn to walk from a park bench. Which image has shaped your picture of God — and how would relating to the park-bench Father change the risks you'd be willing to take?

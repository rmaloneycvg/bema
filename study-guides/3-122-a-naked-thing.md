# BEMA 122: A Naked Thing — Study Guide

**Session 3 (The Gospels) · Episode 122**
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings
**Primary text:** Matthew 19:1–30 · with Genesis 2:24, Deuteronomy 24:1, and allusions to Genesis 24 & 26

---

## Overview

This episode walks the whole of Matthew 19: Jesus is tested on divorce, blesses the children the disciples try to turn away, and meets the rich young man — ending with the promise of the "renewal of all things." Marty's throughline is that chapter 19 has an agenda: it keeps insisting on the **value of the people a religious and worldly culture pushes aside** — women, children, and the one who feels he still lacks something despite having everything. The famous divorce teaching is re-read not as a weapon of shame but as a shield for the vulnerable.

> **Brent Billings:** "Today, we find our understanding of divorce challenged by Jesus and an ancient discussion taking place between the rabbis about the importance and the value of people."

> **Marty Solomon:** "It feels like Chapter 19, Jesus has an agenda of sorts, where he is trying to talk about the importance of people that a religious culture and a worldly culture puts less value on."

---

## Where This Sits in the Story (Arc Context)

**Session 3 arc stage: the king/Messiah embodying the story.**

Session 1 established the story God is telling: a God who invites humanity into **partnership** to be a blessing, to put a chaotic world back toward *shalom*. Session 2 traced the **partner's unfaithfulness** and God's stubborn refusal to quit on that partnership. Session 3 shows the Messiah stepping into the middle of the story and **living it out in his own body and teaching** — not founding a new religion but embodying what Israel was always meant to be.

This episode advances that one story in a specific way: Jesus takes a live rabbinic debate (divorce) and a cultural blind spot (the disposability of women, children, and the discontented wealthy) and **re-centers the whole thing on the people the covenant was always supposed to protect.** The partnership of Session 1 was never abstract — it was always about blessing real, vulnerable neighbors. Here the King demonstrates that the Kingdom is *made up of* exactly the people the powerful discount, and he explicitly ties the entire teaching back to the Session 1 premise: **trust the story.**

> **Marty:** "If you want to be about the Kingdom of God, it is not going to have anything to do with your bank account... you better be about radical hospitality if you want to sit on those thrones and join me."

> **Marty:** "All of this is rooted in something that we started the whole journey out at the beginning of Session 1... Trusting the story is what allows us, frees us, releases us to do [these things]."

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **Framing the test** — Pharisees come to "test" Jesus; the rabbinic art of testing vs. trapping. ([Matthew 19:1–3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A1-3&version=NIV))
2. **The Eight Great Debates and the two yokes** — Shammai (yoke of obedience) vs. Hillel (yoke of love); the two greatest commandments as a rabbi's "lens."
3. **Jesus answers from creation** — "the two will become one flesh"; what God joins, let no one separate. ([Matthew 19:4–6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A4-6&version=NIV); [Genesis 2:24](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Genesis+2%3A24&version=NIV))
4. **The certificate of divorce (the *get*)** — "Why did Moses command it?" "Because your hearts were hard." ([Matthew 19:7–9](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A7-9&version=NIV); [Deuteronomy 24:1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Deuteronomy+24%3A1&version=NIV))
5. **The textual variant** — *ervat davar* ("a naked thing" = anything) vs. *davar ervat* ("a thing of nakedness" = immorality); why Shammai and Hillel landed where they did.
6. **Jesus sides with Shammai — for a Hillel reason** — divorce isn't about the man's convenience; it's about protecting the woman left with no provision. ([Matthew 5:31–32](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+5%3A31-32&version=NIV))
7. **The hard saying on eunuchs** — "not everyone can accept this word." ([Matthew 19:10–12](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A10-12&version=NIV))
8. **Let the little children come** — the disciples rebuke; Jesus says the Kingdom belongs to such as these. ([Matthew 19:13–15](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A13-15&version=NIV))
9. **The rich young man** — "What good thing must I do?"; Jesus lists commandments 6–9, then jumps to 5 + "love your neighbor." ([Matthew 19:16–20](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A16-20&version=NIV))
10. **Fohrman's two parallel tablets** — 5 ("honor father and mother") parallels 10 ("do not covet"); both = be satisfied with the life you have.
11. **"Go, sell your possessions"** — the man goes away sad. ([Matthew 19:21–22](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A21-22&version=NIV))
12. **Camel and the eye of the needle** — debunking the "small gate" legend; it means what it says. ([Matthew 19:23–26](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A23-26&version=NIV))
13. **Peter's question and the renewal of all things** — twelve thrones, the hundredfold, *mishpat* as putting the world back together. ([Matthew 19:27–30](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+19%3A27-30&version=NIV))
14. **Return to the premise** — 122 episodes in: *trust the story*, rooted in Shabbat.

**Coverage verification:** All fourteen segments above are drawn from the episode's walk through Matthew 19 and its supporting discussion. No major teaching block in the transcript is left unplaced. Minor asides are parked under *Other Talking Points*.

## East / West Lens

The podcast repeatedly contrasts a Hebraic (Eastern) reading with the Greek-Western habits most listeners bring. Only the categories actually engaged in this episode are tagged below, each with a citation.

- **Interpretation as communal debate (East) vs. single "correct" answer (West).** The Hebraic world *expected* rabbis to argue; a "test" could be collaborative critical thought, not only a trap.
  > **Marty:** "There's just the rabbinical art of engaging in critical thought. There's a potential here that the Pharisees are just simply wanting to test Jesus."

- **A rabbi's "yoke" as an interpretive lens (East) vs. doctrine as a checklist (West).** Scripture is read *through* a governing love or obedience, not applied flatly.
  > **Marty:** "A rabbi's yoke is how they interpret the Text... That prescription lens is made up of two commandments."

- **Law as protection of persons (East) vs. law as moral scorekeeping (West).** The divorce teaching protects the vulnerable rather than ranking the righteous.
  > **Marty:** "[Jesus] was not trying to establish a moral standard of holiness, but a measure of protection for those pushed to the margins."

- **Judgment as restoration / *mishpat* (East) vs. judgment as condemnation (West).** "Judging the twelve tribes" means putting the world back together, not sentencing.
  > **Marty:** "If you want to put the world back together, that's what we're going to be doing in the age to come."

- **Hyperlinked allusion / *remez* (East) vs. flat literalism (West).** "Brothers, sisters, father, mother, wife" and the hundredfold point back to Isaac — a hint, not a ledger of literal thrones.
  > **Marty:** "Again, we always make this so mechanical, and so literal... Not what the picture Jesus is painting is."

## Narrative Tensions

**1. Jesus always sides with Hillel — but here he sides with Shammai.**
*Classification: apparent contradiction in the teacher's pattern.*
> **Marty:** "This is the one time where Jesus takes Shammai's perspective. What's going to give here?"
**Hebraic resolution:** Jesus adopts Shammai's *narrow grounds* for divorce but for Hillel's *reason* — love and protection of the woman. The ruling is strict precisely because the point is not obedience-for-its-own-sake but shielding the vulnerable party.
> **Marty:** "Jesus has taken this position about what men are called to do for women in this ancient patriarchal world."

**2. A teaching meant to protect the vulnerable is used to shame them.**
*Classification: ironic reversal / pastoral tension.*
> **Marty:** "This teaching of Jesus gets used to actually commit the very sin that Jesus is actually fighting against with the Pharisees, a mistreatment of the women."
**Hebraic resolution:** Reading the law as *protection* (not scorekeeping) returns the text to its purpose — healing and restoration for the one pushed to the margins, not fresh guilt.

**3. "Which commandments?" — Jesus skips the tenth and jumps to the fifth.**
*Classification: puzzling omission / interpretive gap.*
> **Marty:** "I would expect 6-7-8-9-10... Yet he doesn't."
**Hebraic resolution:** Using Fohrman's parallel-tablet structure, commandment 5 ("honor father and mother") mirrors 10 ("do not covet") — both are about *being satisfied with the life you have*. The rich man's hidden issue is coveting a different lot, so Jesus names the fifth to touch the tenth.

**4. The rich young man goes away sad — and we never learn what he did.**
*Classification: open ending.*
> **Marty:** "I always wanted to know what the ending to his story was... Maybe he didn't. It's this interesting interaction."
**Open — dance with it.** The text deliberately leaves the outcome unresolved; the invitation ("come, follow me") still stands for the reader.

**5. "Is it lawful to divorce for *any and every* reason?" treats the wife as a prop.**
*Classification: unstated cultural assumption surfaced.*
> **Marty:** "The woman is like a prop in their question. 'When can we get rid of our wives?' Jesus is like, 'No, this isn't about you and your wives, this is actually about the women in your life.'"
**Hebraic resolution:** Jesus refuses the frame of the question and relocates the moral weight onto the man's duty toward a full-fledged human being.

## Terminology

- **The Eight Great Debates** — the set of rabbinic debate topics (divorce among them) that revealed how a rabbi interpreted Scripture.
- **Yoke** — a rabbi's interpretive lens; how they read the whole Text. "A rabbi's yoke is how they interpret the Text."
- **Shammai** — rabbi (c. 40 years before Jesus) with a *yoke of obedience*; second commandment ≈ keeping the Sabbath/obeying. Narrow grounds for divorce.
- **Hillel** — rabbi with a *yoke of love*; second commandment = "love your neighbor as yourself." Broad grounds for divorce ("if she burns your biscuits").
- **get** — a certificate of divorce (Hebrew G-E-T).
- **ervat davar vs. davar ervat** — the Deuteronomy 24:1 textual variant. *davar ervat* = "a thing of nakedness" (sexual immorality; Shammai's narrow grounds); *ervat davar* = "a naked thing" (any thing at all; Hillel's broad grounds).
- **eunuchs** — in Jesus's hard saying, those who forgo marriage, including "for the sake of the kingdom of heaven."
- **olam haba** — the age to come / eternal life (what the rich man seeks).
- **shaphat / mishpat / shofetim** — to judge / justice-restoration / judges; "judging the twelve tribes" = joining God in putting the world back together.
- **mamzer** — the devalued outsider; here extended to the women, children, and marginalized the Kingdom is *for*.
- **remez** — a hint/allusion back to an earlier text (e.g., Isaac and Rebecca behind "brothers, sisters, father, mother, wife").

## Illustrations & Images

- **"Burn your biscuits" (Hillel's broad grounds).** Hillel's recorded quip that a man may hand his wife a *get* "if she happens to burn your biscuits in anger" — vivid shorthand for the lax position Jesus rejects.
  > **Marty:** "'If she happens to burn your biscuits in anger, you may hand her a get.'"
- **The divorce court on the opposite side of town.** Hillel's rule forcing a man to walk clear across town — so neighbors could talk him out of it — complicates the "flippant" reading of his position.
  > **Marty:** "'The divorce court that you have to use is the one on the opposite side of town.'"
- **The camel and the eye of the needle.** The popular "small gate in the city wall" devotional is debunked; the saying means near-impossibility.
  > **Marty:** "A wonderfully poetic teaching. It's just not built in any historicity."
- **The child as more than a prop.** Brent and Marty's read that in chapter 18 the disciples may have heard the child as a mere illustration ("about us"), and in chapter 19 Jesus insists the child was a *child* — one of the actual citizens of the Kingdom.
  > **Marty:** "'The child was not just a prop, the child was a child and that's why I chose the child.'"

## Key Lessons

1. **The Kingdom is composed of the people power discounts.** Chapter 19's agenda — women, children, the one who still lacks — shows the Messiah embodying a story that was always about blessing the vulnerable neighbor. This advances Session 1's partnership from principle to practice: the King demonstrates *whom* the partnership was for.
   > **Marty:** "If you can't see the value of women, if you can't see the value of children, you're missing the whole point of the Kingdom of God."

2. **Law read as protection, not as a weapon.** Jesus takes a strict ruling but for a merciful reason; the strictness exists to shield the woman, not to score the man's holiness. The embodied-Messiah stage reinterprets Torah by its *purpose*.
   > **Marty:** "The issue of divorce was about protecting the woman, who, in a patriarchal culture, would be left with no provision, belonging, or care."

3. **Discontent, not just immorality, blocks the Kingdom.** The rich man kept the commandments yet coveted a different life; satisfaction with your lot (commandment 5 ↔ 10) is Kingdom-shaped. Against Session 2's portrait of a faithless partner, Jesus names the subtle unfaithfulness of a heart that always wants a different story.
   > **Marty:** "'Don't wish for a different life. Be satisfied with the lot that you have right now in this life.'"

4. **Judgment means putting the world back together.** The twelve thrones are *mishpat*/restoration, not condemnation — the renewal of all things is the mission the King invites the disciples into. This is the Session 1 vision (shalom from chaos) carried forward by the Messiah.
   > **Marty:** "There is going to come a day where we are going to put the world back together."

5. **None of it works without trusting the story.** Selling possessions, protecting a vulnerable spouse, forgiving — every hard command is downstream of the founding premise. The embodied Messiah does not replace Session 1; he grounds his hardest teachings in it.
   > **Marty:** "We'll never be able to do any of the things that Jesus calls us to do if we haven't learned how to trust the story."

## Discussion Questions

1. Jesus adopts Shammai's *strict* position on divorce but for Hillel's *merciful* reason — protecting the woman. Where in your own life or community do you see rules enforced with the right letter but the wrong heart? What would it look like to keep the rule *for the sake of the vulnerable* instead?

2. Marty argues that this teaching has often been used to shame the very people (especially women) it was meant to protect. How should a community hold both the seriousness of covenant *and* the restoration of those who have been through divorce?

3. The rich young man "kept all the commandments" yet went away sad. If his hidden issue was *coveting a different life* (commandment 5 ↔ 10, "be satisfied with your lot"), where might that same discontent be hiding in a life that looks obedient on the surface?

4. Jesus says the twelve will "judge" the tribes — but judgment here means *mishpat*, putting the world back together. How does reframing judgment as restoration change the way you imagine the "age to come" and your own participation in it?

5. 122 episodes in, Marty insists every hard command traces back to "trust the story." Pick one command from Matthew 19 (protect the vulnerable, be satisfied with your lot, be radically generous). What would you have to *trust* about God's story in order to actually do it?

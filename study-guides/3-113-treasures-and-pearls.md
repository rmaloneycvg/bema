# BEMA 113 — Treasures and Pearls — Study Guide

**Session 3 (The Gospels) · Episode 113**
**Speakers:** Marty Solomon, Brent Billings, Corey Knadler (guest)
**Primary texts:** Matthew 13:44–46; Proverbs 2:1–8; Ezekiel 16:1–7
**Secondary texts referenced:** Luke 15; Psalms (God imaged as father, nursing mother, shepherd)

---

## Overview

This episode works through the next two parables of Matthew 13 — the treasure hidden in a field and the merchant seeking fine pearls — using the same PaRDeS (Jewish hermeneutic) road map the hosts have applied to the whole parable discourse. Guest Corey Knadler joins Marty and Brent. The surface reading (p'shat) is that the kingdom of heaven is "weird," counterintuitive, and worth selling everything to obtain. The obvious remez is Proverbs 2 — searching for wisdom "as for hidden treasure." But Marty flags a problem: in every other parable of Matthew 13, the main actor is God, not us. That drives him to a second, preferred remez — Ezekiel 16, where God finds a castaway infant in a field and raises her into a jeweled treasure. Read that way, the man and the merchant are God, and the treasure/pearl is God's people.

Within the Session 3 arc — the king/Messiah embodying the one story — this episode advances the gospel claim at the heart of the Gospels: that God is the seeker. Session 1 opened with God inviting humanity into partnership and shalom; Session 2 traced the partner's unfaithfulness and God's stubborn refusal to quit. Here Jesus, teaching in parables, reframes the kingdom so that God is the one who stumbles upon his people as treasure and "sells it all" to acquire them — "which is obviously the story of the Gospel." The King embodies the finder-God of Ezekiel 16, enacting in person the pursuit that the whole story has been building toward.

> **Marty:** "When God found us, he found a treasure, a true gem, a pearl in a field. You are important enough to God that when he found you, there's nothing he would not do to acquire you. He would sell it all, which is obviously the story of the Gospel."

## East/West Lens

BEMA's recurring interpretive contrast between Hebraic (Eastern, concrete, relational, story-shaped) and Greek-Western (abstract, systematized) ways of reading. Only the categories actually engaged in this episode are tagged below.

- **PaRDeS as a wrestling match, not a system (Hebraic / process).** Marty resists treating interpretation as a tidy procedure, insisting it is meant to be a struggle with the text and the tradition.
  > **Marty:** "It's not supposed to be a podcast where we just go through this systematically. It's supposed to be a wrestling match. It's supposed to be you and biblegateway.com... but we shortcut the process, so it always feels a little awkward to me."

- **Text in conversation with text (Hebraic / intertextuality).** The parable is understood by its echoes — "hidden treasure" pulls up Proverbs 2, "pearl" pulls up the Hebrew idiom and ultimately Ezekiel 16. Meaning travels along a chain of allusions.
  > **Marty:** "One of the first passages that I'm going to think of, and I think most Jews are going to think of when you think of a treasure hidden in a field, is going to be Proverbs 2."

- **Concrete image over abstract concept (Hebraic).** The kingdom is carried by physical images — buried treasure, a pearl, a plant growing in a field, a found child — rather than stated doctrine. Even "entered puberty" in Ezekiel is literally a concrete image, "excellent ornaments."
  > **Marty:** "'I made you grow like a plant of the field.' First of all, come on now, with all the growing of plants that we've had in the parables, tell me that's not just fantastic."

- **Translation softening the Hebrew (Hebraic vs. Western).** The provocative, embodied Hebrew of Ezekiel is flattened in English; recovering the literal "excellent ornaments / full adornment" restores the jewel/pearl connection.
  > **Marty:** "The language of Ezekiel was so unbelievably sexual throughout the whole thing... and we translate it away in Ezekiel all the time, all the time. It's so frustrating."

- **Hebrew-first text over the Septuagint (Hebraic).** Marty argues Matthew's remez logic only works from the Hebrew, not the Greek — the Septuagint "does not help us in this situation."
  > **Marty:** "Matthew wasn't written in the Greek... I just can't make the Septuagint work hardly ever with Matthew."

- **Block logic / holding two readings in tension (Hebraic).** Rather than choosing Proverbs 2 *or* Ezekiel 16, Marty lets both stand — the first-thought remez and the preferred remez — as a deliberate layering.
  > **Marty:** "Notice this additional remez allows us to see the man and the merchant as God characters. This also adds a significant layer to the teaching."

## Episode Flow

The transcript's teaching, in order. Coverage verification follows.

1. **Intro and guest welcome.** Brent frames the episode as "the next two parables of Jesus in Matthew 13, Treasures and Pearls"; guest Corey Knadler is introduced (WSU/BEMA alum, biochemist, newly married to Maria).
2. **Two short parables, like last time.** Marty notes this is a second pair of very small parables, echoing the mustard seed and yeast — and that in the previous episode Jesus was understood to be "telling a whole story," reiterating earlier teaching rather than introducing new themes.
3. **Parallelism vs. chiasm.** Brent names "a parallelism, if not a chiasm"; Marty agrees it is at least a parallelism but declines to assert a chiasm ("I'm no literary scholar... I'm no Ken Bailey").
4. **Reading the parables (Matthew 13:44–46).** Brent reads the treasure and the pearl.
5. **P'shat (surface reading).** Corey's takeaways: both are "weird stories," and the characters are extremely devoted — "getting rid of everything they have to get that thing." Marty links "weird" back to the mustard seed's "counterintuitive" kingdom. Brent raises the oddity of a man digging in a field he doesn't own.
6. **Remez (hint).** Proverbs 2:1–8 read aloud; the line "search for it as for hidden treasure" plus the fact that a "pearl" is a Hebrew idiom for a proverb/teaching makes Proverbs the first-thought remez.
7. **Derash (derived teaching) from Proverbs 2.** If Proverbs is the remez, wisdom/understanding of the kingdom must be sought and dug for; it is buried like treasure, like the seed, like yeast in dough. This ties to the parable of the soils — the good soil is the one who "hears the word and understands."
8. **The glaring problem — the characters are off.** In the soils, the weeds, the mustard seed, and the yeast, the main actor is God. If we are consistent, the man and the merchant must also be God — not us searching for wisdom.
9. **Bonus aside: three human images of God in the Psalms.** Father, nursing mother, shepherd — echoed in Jesus's Luke 15 trio (lost sheep / lost coin / lost son). The woman baking becomes an image of God.
10. **The preferred remez — Ezekiel 16:1–7.** Read aloud: God finds a castaway infant abandoned in a field, says "Live!", and raises her into a jeweled treasure. The man/merchant can now be read as God the finder.
11. **The "beautiful jewel" word study.** Ezekiel's "entered puberty" is literally "come to excellent ornaments / full adornment" (KJV, ESV compared) — a beautiful jewel in verb form; jewel and pearl are interchangeable in biblical Hebrew.
12. **The payoff.** The parables are about God finding his people as treasure/pearl and selling everything to acquire them — "the story of the Gospel."
13. **Septuagint caveat.** The Greek Septuagint doesn't support the reading; Marty uses this to reinforce that Matthew's remez logic is Hebrew-native.
14. **Close.** Brent: "Two parables with some bonus material." Corey is thanked.

## Key Lessons

Framed against the Session 3 arc — the King embodying the one story — and how this episode advances it.

### 1. The kingdom is hidden, small, and counterintuitive — so it must be dug for

On the surface the parables are "weird." Marty ties that to the mustard seed's "counterintuitive" kingdom: not loud and trumpeting (the John the Baptist expectation) but buried and requiring work to uncover.

> **Marty:** "This stands in contrast to this John the Baptist narrative, which says it's loud, it's coming with trumpets, it's showing up. Jesus said, 'No, it's not. It's buried and you have to go digging for it.'"

**Arc advance:** The King who embodies the story proclaims a kingdom that looks nothing like the political, powerful messiah Israel expected after Session 2's exile and longing. It is small and hidden — continuous with the mustard-seed and yeast kingdom, and setting up the whole Session 3 reversal of what "king" means.

### 2. Reading Proverbs 2 alone makes *us* the hero — and that breaks the pattern

The Proverbs 2 remez yields a true derash: wisdom must be sought "as for hidden treasure," and the good soil is the one who *understands*. But if we stop there, we become the man searching — which contradicts every other parable in the chapter, where God is the actor.

> **Marty:** "We read those parables, we think, 'Oh, it's like me. I have to go out and search for hidden treasure.' If all I sit there and see is Proverbs 2, then I'm the man, which I pause and I go, 'Wait, but I'm not the man in these other parables.'"

**Arc advance:** The episode guards against a self-centered reading of the Gospels. The one story is not primarily about human spiritual achievement; it is about the God who acts — the same God who kept the partnership alive in Session 2.

### 3. The real remez is Ezekiel 16: God is the finder

Marty's preferred reading sends the hearer to Ezekiel 16, where God finds a castaway infant in a field and raises her into a jeweled treasure. Now the man and the merchant are God, and the "plant of the field" and "beautiful jewel" language links straight back to the parable's imagery.

> **Marty:** "Is the parable of the treasure and the parable of the pearl... a parable of what God found, and it's not about us primarily finding wisdom. The parable actually speaks about God finding his people."

**Arc advance:** This is the gospel in miniature and the center of the Session 3 stage. The King embodies the finder-God of Ezekiel 16 — the God who refused to abandon his people (Session 2) now personally seeking and buying them back.

### 4. You are the treasure God sells everything to acquire

The payoff turns the parable outward to the listener: when God finds his people, he finds a treasure worth everything. He "sells it all" to acquire them — the Gospel storyline.

> **Marty:** "He sees your true value and is willing to sell everything in order to buy you as his own."

**Arc advance:** Session 1's invitation to partnership and Session 2's refusal-to-quit culminate in a King who pays everything for the partner. The embodiment of the story is costly pursuit, not conquest.

### 5. Matthew's remezes live in the Hebrew, not the Greek

Because the "jewel/pearl" link and the Ezekiel 16 allusion only work from the Hebrew text, Marty argues Matthew's parables presuppose a Hebrew-literate audience and a Hebrew source.

> **Marty:** "Matthew wasn't written in the Greek. And there's just so many reasons why and that's one of them... I just can't make the Septuagint work hardly ever with Matthew."

**Arc advance:** Hearing the King's teaching rightly requires standing inside the Hebrew story — reinforcing the whole BEMA premise that the Gospels are the climax of one continuous, Hebraic narrative, not a Greek departure from it.

## Narrative Tensions

Surface problems the episode raises, each with a classification tag, a cited quote, the relevant text, and either a Hebraic resolution or an honest "Open — dance with it."

**Tension 1 — A man digging in a field he doesn't own.** *(Classification: cultural/ethical oddity.)* Brent flags that the treasure-finder behaves strangely and perhaps improperly.
> **Brent:** "Speaking of normal, is it normal for a guy to wander around and dig around in fields that he doesn't own?"
> **Resolution (Hebraic / intertextual):** The oddity is a cue, not a problem. "Wandering... and finding hidden treasure" is exactly the picture of Proverbs 2 ("search for it as for hidden treasure"), and once Ezekiel 16 is in view the finder is God, who comes upon the castaway "in the open field." The strangeness points past p'shat toward the remez. See [Proverbs 2:1–8](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Proverbs+2%3A1-8&version=NIV) and [Ezekiel 16:1–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+16%3A1-7&version=NIV).

**Tension 2 — The characters in these parables "appear to be off."** *(Classification: internal inconsistency across the discourse.)* If the man/merchant is us, the parable contradicts the rest of Matthew 13, where God is the actor.
> **Marty:** "It's that the characters and the parables appear to be off. Notice that with every parable told in this teaching, who is the man, Brent?... It's God."
> **Resolution (Hebraic):** Read Ezekiel 16 as the controlling remez. The finder is God, which makes the man and the merchant God-characters, restoring consistency with the sower, the field owner, the farmer, and the baking woman. See [Matthew 13:44–46](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+13%3A44-46&version=NIV).

**Tension 3 — "Pearl" in a Hebrew text when Hebrew barely has the word.** *(Classification: translation/linguistic gap.)* A parable turning on a "pearl" is awkward if ancient Hebrew lacks a word for pearl.
> **Marty:** "A pearl is not a really present idea in Hebrew language, ancient biblical Hebrew language. If you want to say pearl... you'd really be stuck with the idea of jewel."
> **Resolution (Hebraic):** Jewel and pearl are effectively interchangeable; Ezekiel 16's "beautiful jewel" (the literal sense of "entered puberty" / "excellent ornaments") supplies the pearl imagery, and the Hebrew idiom already uses "pearl" for a proverb/teaching. The linguistic scarcity becomes the bridge between the two parables. See [Ezekiel 16:1–7](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+16%3A1-7&version=NIV).

**Tension 4 — Parallelism or chiasm?** *(Classification: literary-structure disagreement.)* Brent proposes a chiasm; Marty will only commit to a parallelism.
> **Brent:** "A parallelism, if not a chiasm."
> **Open — dance with it:** Marty holds it loosely — "Definitely a parallelism for sure, at least in my mind. I'm no literary scholar. I'm no Ken Bailey, but if I had to give my two cents, that's what I think." No chiastic structure is laid out, so the question is left genuinely open. (No chiasm is asserted; see note under *Chiasm* omission below.)

**Tension 5 — The Septuagint doesn't support the reading.** *(Classification: textual/translation disagreement.)* Brent notes the Greek undercuts the Hebrew word-play.
> **Brent:** "Unfortunately, the Septuagint does not help us in this situation."
> **Resolution (Hebraic):** Marty treats this as confirming rather than defeating the reading — Matthew's remez logic is Hebrew-native, so a Greek translation that loses the "jewel/pearl" link is expected. "Just bolstering your theory." See [Matthew 13:44–46](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+13%3A44-46&version=NIV).

## Terminology

- **PaRDeS** — the four-level Jewish reading: p'shat (surface), remez (hint to another Hebrew text), drash/derash (derived truth), sod (God-given mystery). *(Marty: "We're going to use the same Jewish hermeneutic road map... The first thing we're going to look at is what, Brent? / P'shat.")*
- **P'shat** — the plain, surface-level reading. *(Marty: "P'shat would be surface level reading.")*
- **Remez** — a hint pointing to another text. *(Marty: "Now we're looking for a remez... one of the first passages that I'm going to think of... is going to be Proverbs 2.")*
- **Derash / drash** — the derived teaching built from the remez. *(Marty: "If Proverbs is a remez, then this would mean that at least part of Jesus's drash... is that wisdom and understanding of the Kingdom is something that must be sought after and dug for.")*
- **Pearl / jewel** — in Hebrew idiom a "pearl" is a proverb/teaching/nugget of wisdom; biblical Hebrew has few gem words, so "jewel" and "pearl" are effectively interchangeable. *(Marty: "A pearl is actually a Hebrew idiom for a proverb, for a teaching, for a nugget of wisdom. They call those things pearls.")*
- **"Excellent ornaments" / "full adornment"** — the literal Hebrew behind Ezekiel 16's "entered puberty," a beautiful jewel in verb form. *(Marty: "The phrase there, 'entered puberty,' is literally excellent ornaments, 'come to excellent ornaments.'")*
- **The man / the merchant = God** — across Matthew 13 the main actor is God; so the one who finds and buys the treasure/pearl is God finding his people. *(Marty: "Who does the merchant and who does the man have to be if we're consistent, Brent? / Seems like it has to be God.")*
- **Understanding (the mark of good soil)** — the good soil "hears the word and understands," echoing Proverbs 2's repeated "understanding." *(Marty: "I think about the one who hears the word and understands it.")*

## Illustrations & Images

- **Selling everything for one thing.** Both characters liquidate all they own for the single treasure/pearl. *(Corey: "Basically getting rid of everything they have to get that thing." / Marty: "This thing that they found is so valuable that everything else pales in comparison.")*
- **The weird, counterintuitive kingdom.** The kingdom as strange and backwards, not matching normal assumptions. *(Corey: "The Kingdom of Heaven is weird because they're both weird stories." / Marty: "It's counterintuitive. It's backwards.")*
- **Treasure, seed, and yeast all buried.** The kingdom hidden in the ground and in the dough, requiring digging. *(Marty: "The wisdom is again, buried just like what, Brent? / Like treasure... The seed... Yeast, buried in dough.")*
- **Loud trumpets vs. buried treasure.** John the Baptist's loud, arriving kingdom set against Jesus's hidden one. *(Marty: "It's loud, it's coming with trumpets, it's showing up. Jesus said, 'No, it's not. It's buried and you have to go digging for it.'")*
- **God as nursing mother.** One of only three human images God takes in the Psalms, alongside father and shepherd. *(Marty: "God is talked about as a mother who nurses at her breasts.")*
- **The castaway infant in the field.** Ezekiel 16's abandoned baby whom God finds, says "Live!" over, and raises into a jewel. *(Corey, reading Ezekiel 16: "you were thrown out into the open field... I said to you, 'Live!' I made you grow like a plant of the field.")*
- **"Blossoming into womanhood" / Victorian adornment.** Corey's analogy for the softened puberty language, which Marty affirms. *(Corey: "like an author in the 1800s writing about somebody blossoming into their womanhood." / Marty: "Like a Victorian Age type, language type situation.")*

## Discussion Questions

1. Corey's p'shat was that both parables are "weird" and that the characters give up everything. Where in your own life has the kingdom felt counterintuitive or "backwards" — small and hidden rather than loud and arriving "with trumpets"?
2. Marty says that if we read only Proverbs 2, "then I'm the man" — and that quietly makes the Gospel about *our* search for wisdom. How does the reading change when God, not you, is the one searching the field? What do you gain and what do you lose?
3. Ezekiel 16 pictures God finding a castaway infant "thrown out into the open field" and saying "Live!" If this is the controlling image behind the treasure and the pearl, how does being *the found treasure* rather than *the finder* reshape how you hear "the kingdom of heaven is like..."?
4. Marty claims the parables are "obviously the story of the Gospel" — God selling everything to acquire his people. Within the Session 3 arc of a King who embodies the story, what does it mean that the Messiah's pursuit looks like costly buying rather than conquest?
5. The hosts hold two remezes (Proverbs 2 and Ezekiel 16) together instead of choosing. When is it faithful to let two readings of a text stand in tension, and when do you need to decide between them?
6. Marty argues the Septuagint "does not help us" and that Matthew's word-play only works in Hebrew. How much should the original language shape how we read the Gospels, and what might non-Hebrew readers miss or need help recovering?

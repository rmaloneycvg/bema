# BEMA 61 — Ezekiel: Strength — Study Guide

**Session:** 2 (The Prophets)
**Episode:** 61 · "Ezekiel — Strength"
**Hosts:** Marty Solomon & Brent Billings
**Primary text:** [Ezekiel 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+1&version=NIV) (the chariot/throne vision), with [Ezekiel 4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+4&version=NIV), [Ezekiel 10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+10&version=NIV), and [Ezekiel 16–17](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+16-17&version=NIV)

---

## Where This Sits in the One Story

Session 1 built the picture of **partnership**: God rescues a people out of Egypt and invites them to become a kingdom of priests who join his restoration project. Session 2 turns to the **Prophets**, and the arc of this stage is the covenant partner's (Israel's) **unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Ezekiel opens a brand-new column on the diagram — the **exilic** prophets — after the pre-Assyrian, Assyrian, and Babylonian periods. The conquest is over; the people now sit in Babylon with the temple destroyed and their old way of life gutted.

That new reality produces new questions, and they are the questions of this exact stage of the story: *Will we be here forever? What did we do to deserve this? Has God forsaken us? Is God even real?* The prophetic voice shifts accordingly — from **woe** to **hope**. Warnings and preventive repentance are too late; the only way the partner comes back to the path is to **overcome** the circumstances it is in. For that, the people need **strength** — which is exactly what the name Ezekiel (*Chazak-iel*, "God strengthens") means.

Relative to Session 1's partnership, this episode shows the humbled, exiled partner at its lowest — and shows that God has not quit. Through apocalyptic imagery, Ezekiel strengthens the people with a single staggering claim: the glory of the LORD was not trapped in the temple when it fell. It departed *eastward* — toward Babylon — because **God went with them into exile**, and when they return, his presence returns from the east with them. God's refusal to quit here takes the form of a God who has *wheels*.

## Episode Flow (Coverage Backbone)

1. **Entering the exilic period** — the people sit in Babylon ("by the rivers of Babylon we sat and wept"); temple destroyed, homes gutted; the new questions of exile; the voice shifts from woe to hope.
2. **The word for Ezekiel: strength** — *chazak* / *Chazak-iel*, "God strengthens"; Marty's son Ezekiel; the dual sense — Ezekiel needs strength for a hard calling, and his message *is* strength to a people tempted to despair.
3. **Apocalyptic literature defined** — Marty's carefully-worded definition (repeated): "apocalyptic literature uses images and symbols to convey hope to their present day"; "apocalyptic" means "to uncover / to reveal"; a *present*, not future, implication; the damage done to prophecy and eschatology by misreading it.
4. **Guerrilla theater (Ezekiel 4)** — Ezekiel the thespian: model of Jerusalem, burning hair, hacking the model with a sword; cooking over excrement, bargained down to animal dung; being a prophet is rarely fun.
5. **Reading Ezekiel 1 with wide eyes** — Brent reads the chariot/throne vision; four creatures with four faces, wheels within wheels, rims "high and awesome" full of eyes, the throne of lapis lazuli and the glory of the LORD; the "high and awesome rims" running joke.
6. **The big ideas, not the details** — four = the four directions/corners, the most expansive language; eyes everywhere = nothing goes unseen; and a whole paragraph on **movement** — this God is *mobile*.
7. **The glory departs eastward (Ezekiel 10)** — the people believed God was located in the temple; the temple is destroyed; the glory departs *east*, toward Babylon — "God's going with you."
8. **The explanation and the assurance (Ezekiel 16–17, closing chapters)** — a poetic (not exhaustive) explanation of the people's unfaithfulness; and the assurance that the glory returns *from the east* as the people return, pictured in an apocalyptic new Jerusalem/new temple.
9. **The message of strength (closing notes)** — press forward, learn from mistakes, hold the promises, believe things are bigger than present circumstances, help put the world back together; the innovator/artist/storyteller; stubbornness repurposed from stiff-necked sin into endurance.
10. **Brent's Tower of Babel parallel** — eastward movement and Babel/Babylon; a tower built vs. a temple rebuilt; God consistently calling his people back from the east; "God knows where he wants His people."

## Key Lessons

Each lesson is framed against the Session 2 arc — the covenant partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.

1. **God refuses to quit by going into exile *with* the unfaithful partner.** The central move of the episode: the glory of the LORD was not destroyed with the temple; it departed east and traveled with the people into Babylon. The partner's unfaithfulness landed it in exile, and God's refusal to quit means he is *in the exile too*.
   - *Marty:* "Ezekiel says when the glory of the Lord departed, he departed with you. You went off to exile, the Lord did not leave you. The Lord went with you."
   - *Marty:* "God has not deserted you, God has not forsaken you, and God was not stuck in Jerusalem... he goes where his people go."

2. **Strength — not warning or woe — is the word the exilic partner now needs.** The time for preventing the disaster is gone; the only way back to the path is to overcome the present circumstances. The arc's "God's refusal to quit" sounds, in exile, like empowerment to endure.
   - *Marty:* "It's too late for warnings and the time for preventive repentance is long gone... they're going to need to be able to overcome their current circumstances... They are going to need strength."
   - *Marty:* "His message is also a message of strength. Ezekiel has been sent to strengthen God's people who sit in exile."

3. **Apocalyptic hope is about the present, not a crystal ball.** Ezekiel's bizarre imagery is not a blueprint for end-times events but an *uncovering* of what is actually true *now* for a despairing people — that is how God speaks hope into this stage of the story.
   - *Marty:* "apocalyptic literature uses images and symbols to convey hope to their present day... It's not this big thing about the future."
   - *Marty:* "Ezekiel is trying to talk to the Jewish people about their current world and their current situation."

4. **The exile is explained *and* bounded — unfaithfulness has a reason and an end.** Ezekiel gives just enough poetic explanation of why the discipline came (chapters 16–17) and a firm assurance that it will not last forever; the partner's unfaithfulness is named, but the story does not end in Babylon.
   - *Marty:* "He's going to say, Listen, you got carted off, God went with you, and here's why you got punished. Here's why you're being disciplined."
   - *Marty:* "there will come a day, Ezekiel says, where you will go back home and you will rebuild. ... God will come with you."

5. **The stubbornness that made Israel sin is the same stubbornness that can carry it home.** The arc's hope is not a different people but the *same* people, their defining trait redeemed — "stiff-necked" rebellion repurposed into determined faithfulness.
   - *Marty:* "The very thing that makes us a stiff-necked people in the desert is the very thing that's going to get us through these tough times as well, but we must endure, we must overcome, we must be strong."

## East/West Lens

Only categories genuinely engaged by this episode are tagged; each is cited.

- **Reading for the big idea over systematizing every detail (East).** Ezekiel 1 is approached by stepping back with "wider eyeballs" for what it is *communicating*, rather than decoding each creature and wheel — explicitly resisting a Western impulse to pull it all apart.
  - *Marty:* "just looking at this apocalyptic vision with a step back, with these wider eyeballs... what are some things that stood out to you as far as the big ideas that Ezekiel seemed to be trying to communicate?"

- **Apocalyptic as present revelation, not future prediction (East).** The episode names a specifically Western misreading — treating apocalyptic as a crystal ball — and recovers the ancient Eastern sense of "uncovering" the present.
  - *Marty:* "We have done so much damage to our understanding of prophecy and eschatology... because of a rampant misunderstanding of how apocalyptic literature functions in the ancient Eastern world."

- **Truth communicated as art and story, not only proposition (East).** Ezekiel is a *thespian* performing guerrilla theater and an artist using image and imagination; the message of strength comes through creativity, not a lecture.
  - *Marty:* "Ezekiel uses the mind of an innovator, the creativity of an artist, and the imagination of a storyteller to communicate hope to the people of God."

- **Hebrew names carry meaning and theology (East).** The whole episode hangs on the name *Ezekiel* = *Chazak-iel*, "God strengthens," with the root traced through Pharaoh's heart and Joshua's "be strong and courageous."
  - *Marty:* "Ezekiel means 'God strengthens you.' ... It is Chazak-iel. His name means 'God strengthens' or 'strengthened by God.'"

- **Reading intertextually across the whole story (East).** Meaning emerges by hearing Ezekiel's eastward movement echo the Tower of Babel and Babylon rather than reading the vision in isolation.
  - *Brent:* "there's some parallels here to the Tower of Babel story where the people are moving east and God wants them to come back west."

## Narrative Tensions

Each tension states the problem, tags its type, cites the transcript, links the passage, and gives a Hebraic resolution (or marks it open).

1. **If God lives in the temple and the temple is destroyed, is God gone — or dead?**
   - *Type:* Theological crisis / apparent contradiction (unstated cultural assumption about sacred space).
   - *Problem:* The exiles assumed God was *located* in the tabernacle/temple; its destruction seems to mean God is defeated, absent, or unreal.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "Does God care about us? Is God even real? Where did God go?" / "The temple has been destroyed. This idea of God moving not only is an odd concept to them, but this is going to have huge implications."
   - *Passage:* [Ezekiel 10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+10&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The vision of a *mobile* glory dissolves the assumption. God was never trapped in the building; the glory departs the temple under its own power and travels east with the exiles. The destruction of the temple is not the death of God but the revelation that God goes where his people go.

2. **Why would the glory of the LORD move *toward* Babylon — the place of judgment?**
   - *Type:* Apparent contradiction (direction of divine presence).
   - *Problem:* One would expect God's glory to flee the enemy; instead it moves east, the very direction of the conquering empire that carried the people off.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "the presence of God departs from the temple and goes east... East is where Babylon's from, right?" / *Brent:* "God's going with you."
   - *Passage:* [Ezekiel 1:28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+1%3A28&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* The surprising direction *is* the point. God moves toward Babylon because that is where his people are. Exile is not God abandoning the people to the enemy's territory but God accompanying them into it — and the return of the glory "from the east" later confirms the whole journey was with them.

3. **How is a vision this bizarre and impenetrable supposed to *comfort* a despairing people?**
   - *Type:* Genre/interpretive difficulty (how apocalyptic conveys hope).
   - *Problem:* Ezekiel 1 is "one of the hardest books" even in Jewish conversation — wheels within wheels, eyes everywhere — so it is unclear how it functions as encouragement at all.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "This is super confusing when you read this. What in the world does this have to do with anything? How do you ever begin to pull this apart?"
   - *Passage:* [Ezekiel 1](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+1&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* Read for the big ideas, the vision is not confusion but comfort: four faces = God sees in every direction; eyes everywhere = nothing goes unseen; wheels = God is mobile and can go anywhere. A glory too big to fit our "bucket" of understanding is precisely the point for a people who feared God was small enough to be destroyed with a building.

4. **Do the closing chapters predict a literal rebuilt temple?**
   - *Type:* Open interpretive question (how to read apocalyptic blueprints).
   - *Problem:* The detailed temple of Ezekiel's closing chapters invites a literal, architectural, future-construction reading.
   - *Cited:* *Marty:* "The closing chapters of Ezekiel are not this literal rebuilding of some future temple. It is this apocalyptic vision just like in Revelation of what this new home, this new Jerusalem, and this new temple is going to look like."
   - *Passage:* [Ezekiel 40](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ezekiel+40&version=NIV)
   - *Hebraic resolution:* Consistent with the episode's definition of apocalyptic, the closing vision uses images and symbols to convey present hope — a picture of restored dwelling-with-God, like Revelation's new Jerusalem — rather than construction specs. The episode does not exhaustively unpack it, so the detail is held as **open — dance with it**, read as hopeful imagery rather than a timetable.

## Terminology

- **chazak** (חזק) — "to be strong / to strengthen"; the root behind Ezekiel's name and the episode's one-word summary for the book.
  - *Marty:* "The word for strength or to be strong or to strengthen is the word chazak... you see it in there. Chazak-iel."
- **Chazak-iel / Ezekiel** — "God strengthens (you)" / "strengthened by God"; the name carries the book's dual sense (Ezekiel needs strength; his message is strength).
  - *Marty:* "His name means 'God strengthens' or 'strengthened by God.'"
- **kavod** — "to be heavy / to make heavy," the second word for "harden" paired with *chazak* in the Pharaoh narrative, recalled here to locate the root.
  - *Marty:* "kavod, it was 'to heavy' or 'to strengthen.' That word chazak that you mentioned was the word to strengthen."
- **chazak va'amatz** — "be strong and courageous"; the Joshua phrase recalled to show *chazak* elsewhere in the story. (Transcript renders it "chazak va'chamatz.")
  - *Marty:* "It showed up again in Joshua when we talked about chazak va'chamatz... to be strong and courageous."
- **exilic** — the new prophetic period Ezekiel opens (after pre-Assyrian, Assyrian, Babylonian); the hosts cheerfully disagree on how to pronounce it.
  - *Marty:* "Now we transition into what we're calling exilic or exilic. We can't figure out which one it is."
- **apocalyptic literature** — a form of prophetic writing that "uses images and symbols to convey hope to their present day"; the word means "to uncover / to reveal."
  - *Marty:* "the word apocalyptic means 'to uncover' or 'to reveal.' It has a very present implication, not so much a future implication."
- **eschatology** — "the study of the end times"; named as the field most damaged by misreading apocalyptic.
  - *Marty:* "our understanding of prophecy and eschatology—which means the study of the end times."
- **guerrilla theater** — impromptu prophetic street performance; Ezekiel enacts a message publicly, then leaves the audience gaping.
  - *Marty:* "He takes his apocalyptic imagery and uses it to perform what I heard one teacher called guerrilla theater."

## Illustrations & Images

- **The glory of the LORD with wheels / "rims high and awesome."** The governing image of the episode: a *mobile* God who can go anywhere, repurposed by Marty into the running "lifted vehicle" joke.
  - *Marty:* "This God has wheels, this God has rims, high and awesome. It is lifted and he goes where his people go."
- **Four faces, eyes everywhere.** The expansive imagery of Ezekiel 1 read for its big ideas — God sees in all four directions and nothing goes unseen.
  - *Marty:* "They have four faces, meaning that they're looking in all four directions. They can see everywhere."
- **Guerrilla theater: the burning Lego Jerusalem (Ezekiel 4).** Ezekiel building a model of Jerusalem, burning his cut hair on it, and hacking it with a sword to enact the city's destruction.
  - *Marty:* "My son loves Legos. I can imagine Ezekiel building a big Lego model of Jerusalem... he takes a sword and he chops up the model because Jerusalem's going to be destroyed."
- **Cooking over dung — bargained down to animal feces.** The uncomfortable sign-act of Ezekiel 4 and Ezekiel's negotiation with God over it, underscoring that prophecy is "rarely fun work."
  - *Marty:* "'Can I at least cook it over animal feces?'' To which God says, 'Alright, I'll let you cook it over animal feces.'"
- **The rivers of Babylon.** The lament-image of the exiles sitting and weeping by Babylon's rivers, used to set the emotional scene of the exilic period.
  - *Marty:* "'By the rivers of Babylon, we sat and wept,' to the old Psalm."
- **"Wheel in the Sky" (Journey).** Marty's confession that the wheels-within-wheels imagery left him unable to stop singing the Journey song — a human marker of how impenetrable the book first felt.
  - *Marty:* "I couldn't stop singing Journey in my head... 'Wheel in the Sky.'"

## Discussion Questions

1. The exiles assumed God was *located* in the temple — so its destruction felt like the end of God. Where do we quietly tie God's presence to a building, an institution, or a season of life, and what would it mean to discover that his glory "has wheels"?
2. Ezekiel's one word is *strength* for people who can no longer prevent the disaster, only endure it. Where are you past the point of "warning" and "woe," needing instead the strength to overcome circumstances you can't undo?
3. Marty insists apocalyptic literature "uses images and symbols to convey hope to their present day," not to predict the future. How does reading Ezekiel (or Revelation) this way change what you look for in it?
4. The glory of the LORD moved *toward* Babylon — into exile *with* the people. Where in your own exile might God already be present, in the very place you assumed he had abandoned?
5. "The very thing that makes us a stiff-necked people... is the very thing that's going to get us through." What stubborn trait in you — one that has gotten you into trouble — might God be repurposing for endurance and faithfulness?

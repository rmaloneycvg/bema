# Wisdom and Meaning

BEMA 45 · Session 2
Source transcript: `~/workspace/bema/transcripts/2-45-wisdom-and-meaning.md`
Book: Proverbs 2:21-22; 10:3; 20:21; 15:22; 19:21; 26:4-5; 3:5-6 · Ecclesiastes 7:13-14; 7:15; 7:1-4
Co-host: Brent Billings · Guest co-host: Reed Dent · (Marty Solomon does **not** appear in this episode)
Core question leaned on throughout: *what does it mean to live well — moment to moment — and does any of it mean anything?* A "flyover" of the wisdom literature, pairing Proverbs' confident baseline ("wise sayings that are generally true") with Ecclesiastes' honest "post-wisdom," landing on **satisfaction and meaning** as the aim of wisdom.

> **Session 2 overarching arc: the prophets — the partner's (Israel's) unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit.** Session 1 built the *partnership*: God and a people bound in covenant, learning to "trust the story." Reed's own opening recap tracks the partner's slide — Joshua's crossroads calling, the Judges "redemption cycle" seen through "God's long suffering, patience, lovingkindness," the demand for a king, Saul, David, and then **Solomon's "lust for empire"** until "the people start to look a little anti-story... the very thing that they were intended to overthrow," followed by "the split" into "full-on... anti-story" — "the situation that the prophets are going to show up and speak into." This episode is the deliberate *pause before the prophets*: the wisdom literature "largely credited to... David and to Solomon." It advances the one story not by narrating the collapse but by equipping the partner to *live well inside it* — zooming from the grand communal narrative down to "the family's caravan along the way," and refusing to pretend the covenant always "pays out" on schedule (Ecclesiastes). Where Session 1 asked whether the partner would say *yes*, this episode asks how a battered, failing partner keeps "faithfully living before and loving the God who is there above and beneath and through it all" when the paths come out crooked.

## Key Lessons

- **The Scripture shifts scope — from the grand communal epic to the *quotidian*, and God cares about both.** Reed: *"if the story up to this point has been concerned with this incredible journey of God's people from Egypt to the Promised Land and beyond, then the wisdom books are dealing with what happens in your family's caravan along the way."* How you handle work, conflict, time, and money "is just as much a part of 'spirituality' as your prayer life... your worship life."
- **Proverbs are "wise sayings that are *generally* true" — and *generally* is the key word.** Reed: *"we get sayings that are most of the time a good guide... but maybe not always."* The righteous *sometimes* go hungry; a good person is sometimes poor.
- **Wisdom is "not knowing *what* is true, but *when* it's true" (Rachel Held Evans).** Reed makes it the hinge of the whole episode: *"what's wise is not always wise. It's not what is true, but when it's true."*
- **Reframe "is it a sin?" as "is it *wise* or *foolish*?" — and the whole context opens up.** Reed with his CCF students: *"let's set aside the question of sin and ask instead: Is it wise to do this? Or is it foolish? And suddenly what opens up is like all of that context surrounding the situation."*
- **Wisdom is a *dance*, not a static catalog — the rules free you to live well, not just "by the book."** Reed: *"what we're meant to be doing with wisdom is not just sitting back and knowing in a vacuum right answers, but actually living well, dancing."* Brent extends it (waltz vs. free-flowing); Reed's over-coached soccer formation makes the same point: *"the best coaches know how to say, here's the basic rules, and yet this will enable you to be creative."*
- **Ecclesiastes is "post-wisdom" — wisdom *after* wisdom.** Having learned the baseline logic, you've lived long enough to watch it *not* play out. Reed: *"you've had some life experience and realize that there are definitely times when that is not true... it didn't work out that way at all."*
- **`hevel` ("meaningless") is *smoke*, not "nothing matters."** Reed: *"if you think you can explain the meaning of everything, then you are going the wrong direction. That's like trying to catch smoke in your hand."* The way through the obscuring mist "is through."
- **Wisdom = holding two opposites at once; "living in the tension."** Reed: *"this idea of wisdom being the ability to hold two seemingly opposite things in your mind at the same time. Like to hold grief and hope... and walk between them at the same time."*
- **The aim of the wisdom books is not pleasure or pain-avoidance but **satisfaction** and **meaning** — and those come only *through* difficulty.** Reed: *"what we want is satisfaction. And the only way we get to satisfaction..."* — the "85-year-old on the porch" thought experiment: teleported there, it would mean nothing; the meaning is in the costly journey. *"Better to go to the house of mourning because it's all going to go away."*

## East/West Lens

*(Only categories the episode actually engages, each with a citation.)*

- **Dynamic, lived wisdom vs. static propositional knowledge (Eastern vs. Western).** The episode's spine. Reed: wisdom is *"something that is lively, something that is dynamic, that responds and adapts to the situations that you find yourself in. It's not like a dusty, static thing of just like some... detached sort of catalog of courses of action."* The Western instinct ("don't give me Psalms, give me Proverbs... we don't need emotions," young Reed) is precisely what the episode corrects.
- **Wisdom personified / embodied — a *woman* calling out and a *dance* (Eastern concreteness vs. Western abstraction).** Reed: Wisdom is portrayed *"as a woman... who is calling out to you... come this way and listen to me,"* not "a crystal ball... scroll or tome on a shelf." Brent names the embodied payoff with dance forms (waltz's rigid frame vs. free-flowing styles).
- **`hevel` as a concrete image, not an abstract concept (Eastern image-thinking).** Reed invokes Tim Mackie/The Bible Project and Rob Bell's spray-bottle to make "meaninglessness" tangible: *"hevel being smoke or like a mist... he spritzes... little mists into the air... to give people a picture of the concrete image that is behind what is kind of an abstract concept."*
- **Holding tension vs. resolving it (Eastern both-and vs. Western either-or).** Reed explicitly resists the Western demand to pick a winner between the texts: *"We don't have to resolve these and figure out which one is the right one and which one is the wrong one."* Wisdom walks between grief and hope.
- **"Not karma" — an ordered creation, not a cosmic payout machine.** Reed guards against a Western/Eastern-religion misread of cause-and-effect: *"which is not the same thing as karma... we're talking about just like a natural sort of cause and effect by the way that God has set up things to be."*

## Episode Flow (coverage backbone)

A sequential walkthrough of every teaching beat, in order. Used to verify full coverage.

1. **Framing + post-Torah recap.** Brent introduces Reed to "continue our study of wisdom literature... the collection of Proverbs and the teaching of Ecclesiastes." Reed does "a little post-Torah review": Torah → Joshua's crossroads calling → Judges as "redemption cycle" (God's long suffering) → Samuel and the request for a king → Saul → David → Solomon's "lust for empire" → the split (Rehoboam/Jeroboam) into "full-on... anti-story" — "the situation that the prophets are going to show up and speak into." The wisdom literature is the pause before the prophets, "credited to... David and to Solomon."
2. **"Are you a Proverbs guy?" — the Joe assignment.** Reed loved Proverbs in high school for being "straightforward, rational, cause-and-effect" (anti-emotion). His college campus pastor Joe assigned him to read Proverbs daily all summer and "never tell anyone what you learned" (a cure for showing off) — which he now, twenty years later, "unveil[s]."
3. **The shift in scope (v1.0 callback) — Proverbs as *tools*; the *quotidian*.** The Scripture moves from the "grand overarching narrative... epic" to the small and daily. The nickel word *quotidian*: work, family, money, speech, emotions, annoying people — new vocabulary like "hotheaded," "spendthrift," "sluggard," "nagging," "quarreling." The "family's caravan along the way." Eugene Peterson (*As Kingfishers Catch Fire*) on religious people getting "disinterested in their jobs and families." Meta-message: the daily stuff is "just as much a part of 'spirituality.'"
4. **"Wise sayings that are *generally* true" (v1.0 line).** *Generally* is the key word. Four examples read by Brent, each affirmed as sensible but not always true: **Prov 2:21-22** (upright dwell / wicked cut off — "not the same thing as karma"); **Prov 10:3** (the righteous won't go hungry — but is it always? the "reliable network" reading); **Prov 20:21** (quick inheritance / lottery-winner logic); **Prov 15:22** (plans fail without counsel — Brent's paraphrase: "many advisors... I can get it done").
5. **Why we *need* the baseline.** Without dependable cause-and-effect everything is "cynical... unsettled... chaotic." It's what "we begin when we teach our kids." But "generally true, but not always."
6. **"Not *what* is true, but *when* it's true" (Rachel Held Evans, *Inspired*).** The hinge. Illustrated by the **Prov 15:22 vs. 19:21** tension (make plans with many advisors vs. "the Lord's counsel that is fulfilled") — should we make plans, hold them loosely, both-and, or does it matter since "God is going to get what God wants"?
7. **The back-to-back fool verses — Prov 26:4-5 (Robert Alter's "dolt").** "Do not answer a dolt by his folly" / "Answer the dolt by his folly" — "a lovely little puzzle." Brent: "To dolt or not to dolt." Which applies depends on context: *who is this dolt? your history? the stakes?* "What's wise is not always wise."
8. **"Is it a sin?" → "Is it wise or foolish?"** Reed's CCF reframing opens up "all of that context surrounding the situation" — "sometimes it's more a matter of wise and foolish" than right and wrong.
9. **Wisdom personified as a woman / a dance (v1.0 callback).** Not a crystal ball or tome but "a woman... calling out." Wisdom as a dance — structure that frees improvisation. Brent on waltz (rigid frame) vs. free-flowing dances. Reed's **over-coached soccer formation**: he boxed kids into 20-square-yard zones and "hampered" their play; the best coaches give rules that "enable you to be creative... doing it well, not just doing it by the book."
10. **Enter Ecclesiastes / *Kohelet* — "post-wisdom."** "Some people call it Kohelet, if they're smart academic types" (Brent: "or if they are farther along in The BEMA Podcast" — Josh Bossé's later series). The jarring flip from 31 chapters of Proverbs to "everything is meaningless." "Wisdom after wisdom": the baseline logic doesn't always play out.
11. **Proverbs vs. Ecclesiastes, pairing 1 — Prov 3:5-6 vs. Eccl 7:13-14.** "Trust in the Lord... he will make your paths straight" (the "classic high school graduation" / *Oh, the Places You'll Go* verse, maybe the most well-known proverb) set against "who can straighten what He has made crooked?... no one can discover anything about their future." Brent's running gag: "See also Ecclesiastes 7."
12. **Pairing 2 — Prov 2:21-22 vs. Eccl 7:15.** "The upright will live in the land" (the "George Bailey effect," *It's a Wonderful Life*) vs. "the righteous perishing in their righteousness, and the wicked living long in their wickedness" ("Only the good die young," Billy Joel).
13. **Don't resolve the tension — hold both.** Reed: we needn't pick a winner; wisdom holds "grief and hope... despair and hope" at once — "living in the tension." Brent reflects it back ("not what but when").
14. **Proverbs vs. Ecclesiastes in temperament.** Proverbs is "concerned with how we act and what we ought to do"; Ecclesiastes is more "philosophical" — legacy, "what's going to live on after you," "how does one find satisfaction."
15. **Eccl 7:1-4 — "a good name... the house of mourning."** Brent reads; "not real positive." Can the wise not take part in pleasure? Only if pleasure is your "highest aim" — then this is "a necessary corrective."
16. **`hevel` — smoke/mist.** Tim Mackie/The Bible Project; Rob Bell's **spray-bottle** in *Introduction to Joy*. Smoke obscures, is impermanent, un-graspable — "the only way through is through." Not "nothing means anything," but you can't catch meaning in your hand.
17. **"A good name" and the chapters 6–7 thread.** The teacher asks "what will be left? Who's going to get the benefit of your work... because everybody will die." Better the house of mourning: accept temporariness and mortality rather than "only ever insist on having a good time."
18. **The payoff — satisfaction and meaning come *through* difficulty.** The **85-year-old-on-the-porch** thought experiment: teleported into that life with grandkids, it would mean nothing; the meaning is in "what it has cost." Wisdom's goal isn't "a big country club in the sky" or "the total absence of... pain" but "meaning... satisfaction of life well lived."
19. **Resources + close.** Brent flags original-episode resources kept in show notes (Rachel Held Evans's *A Year of Biblical Womanhood* ch. on Proverbs 31; a Chris McNaught book; a TED Talk; a Nooma video). Reed adds his CCF wisdom-lit sermon series and re-recommends Eugene Peterson's *As Kingfishers Catch Fire*.

## Terminology

- **the quotidian** — the "very daily humdrum stuff": work, family, money, speech, emotions, annoying people. The wisdom literature shifts the Scripture's scope from the "giant and communal and systemic" to the small and daily. Reed: *"Here's a fun nickel vocabulary word for you, the quotidian... how do you do your job? And how do you treat your family? And what do you do with your money?"*
- **"wise sayings that are *generally* true"** (v1.0 line) — the working definition of a proverb; *generally* is "the key word." Reliable baseline cause-and-effect, "not the same thing as karma," "but maybe not always." Reed: *"we get sayings that are most of the time a good guide... but maybe not always."*
- **"not knowing *what* is true, but *when* it's true"** (Rachel Held Evans) — the nature of wisdom as dynamic and context-dependent. Reed: *"what's wise is not always wise. It's not what is true, but when it's true."*
- **wise vs. foolish (not just right vs. wrong)** — Reed's reframe of "is it a sin?" into "is it *wise* or *foolish*?", which surfaces context. Reed: *"let's set aside the question of sin and ask instead: Is it wise to do this? Or is it foolish?"*
- **Wisdom personified as a woman / "a dance"** — in Proverbs, Wisdom is "a woman who is calling out to you," not "a crystal ball... scroll or tome on a shelf"; wisdom is "like a dance" whose structure frees improvisation. Reed: *"the more you know that, the more also then you are free to express yourself and even improvise."*
- **Kohelet / Ecclesiastes ("post-wisdom")** — "wisdom after wisdom": having learned the baseline logic, you've lived long enough to see it not play out. "Some people call it Kohelet, if they're smart academic types." Reed: *"where you've sort of figured out, generally, that sort of baseline... and yet you've had some life experience and realize that there are definitely times when that is not true."*
- **hevel** (הֶבֶל, "meaningless / vapor / smoke / mist") — Ecclesiastes' refrain. Not "nothing matters" but *smoke*: obscuring, impermanent, un-graspable. Reed (citing Tim Mackie / The Bible Project and Rob Bell's spray-bottle): *"hevel being smoke or like a mist... if you try to catch smoke in your hand, you cannot do that... the only way through is through."*
- **"better... the house of mourning"** — the Ecclesiastes 7 posture: wisdom comes from diving into the places where things fall apart and accepting mortality, rather than only ever "insisting on having a good time." Reed: *"better to go to the house of mourning because it's all going to go away."*
- **satisfaction / meaning** — the stated goal of the wisdom books: "not just to... a big country club in the sky, not to just the total absence of any kind of pain, but what we want is meaning... satisfaction." It comes only *through* difficulty (the "85-year-old on the porch").
- **holding two opposites at once / "living in the tension"** — wisdom as the ability to hold grief *and* hope, despair *and* hope, the straight path *and* the crooked. Reed: *"to hold grief and hope or despair and hope and to be able to hold on to both of those and walk between them at the same time."*

## Illustrations & Stories

- **Joe's "never tell anyone what you learned" summer (Reed).** His college campus pastor, sensing Reed was "over-obsessed with making sure everybody thought I was a smart person," assigned him to read Proverbs every day all summer and never report what he learned — a wisdom-formation exercise Reed only now "unveils."
- **The "family's caravan along the way" (Reed).** The image for the scope-shift: the big story goes "from Egypt to the Promised Land," but the wisdom books deal with "what happens in your family's caravan along the way as you're traveling through."
- **Dance forms — waltz vs. free-flowing (Brent).** In a waltz "your upper body structure is so rigid that if you couldn't see the feet, you would think it was just one person"; other dances are "a lot more free-flowing" — the question wisdom asks is "what kind of dance are we engaging in here?"
- **The over-coached soccer formation (Reed).** As a volunteer coach he boxed kids into rigid 20-square-yard zones and "hampered" their play; the open field was "outside the box Reed told me." The point: good rules should "enable you to be creative," "doing it well, not just doing it by the book."
- **"To dolt or not to dolt" (Brent & Reed).** The Hamlet riff on the back-to-back fool verses (Prov 26:4-5), using Robert Alter's "dolt" for "fool."
- **"See also Ecclesiastes 7" in *Oh, the Places You'll Go* (Brent & Reed).** The running gag about the "classic high school graduation" gift inscribed with Proverbs 3:5-6 — someone should sign underneath, "See also Ecclesiastes 7": "Aunt Linda told you that God was going to make your path straight, but also maybe not."
- **The George Bailey effect, *It's a Wonderful Life* (Reed).** Why "the upright will live in the land" is generally true — do right by people and "there will be people who will be your friends."
- **Rob Bell's spray-bottle, *Introduction to Joy* (Reed).** To make *hevel* concrete, Bell "gets out this spray bottle and he spritzes... little mists into the air" — a picture of smoke/mist behind the abstract "meaninglessness."
- **The 85-year-old on the porch (Reed).** The thought experiment: if your consciousness were instantly "teleported" into your 85-year-old self surrounded by grandkids, it would mean nothing — "I wouldn't even know who these people are" (Brent). Meaning lives in "what it has cost," the costly journey wisdom helps you navigate.

## Discussion Questions

1. Reed says the wisdom literature zooms from the grand communal epic down to "the family's caravan along the way" — your work, money, speech, and the people who annoy you. Where have you treated the "quotidian" as *less* spiritual than prayer or worship, and what would change if you didn't?
2. "Wisdom is not knowing *what* is true, but *when* it's true." Take the back-to-back fool verses (Prov 26:4-5): describe a real situation where you had to decide whether to "answer the dolt" — what context made one response wise and the other foolish?
3. Reed reframes his students' "is it a sin?" into "is it *wise* or *foolish*?" Try it on a decision you're facing. What context "opens up" when you stop asking only about right and wrong?
4. The episode refuses to resolve Proverbs 3:5-6 ("he will make your paths straight") against Ecclesiastes 7:13-14 ("who can straighten what He has made crooked?"). Which text do you more naturally live by — and what does the *other* one have to teach you right now?
5. *Hevel* is smoke: obscuring, impermanent, un-graspable — "the only way through is through." Where are you currently tempted to "explain the meaning of everything" instead of passing through the mist?
6. The 85-year-old-on-the-porch thought experiment says meaning lives in what a life has *cost*, not in the pleasant endpoint. What costly, difficult stretch of your story turned out to carry the most meaning — and does "better the house of mourning" ring true to it?
7. Session 1 asked whether the partner would say *yes*; Session 2 sits with a partner who has largely failed, sliding toward "anti-story." What does it mean that God still bothers to hand that failing partner a wisdom tradition for living well in the ordinary day?

# BEMA 124 — Statement of Triumph — Study Guide

**Session 3 (The Gospels) · Episode 124**
**Speakers:** Marty Solomon, Brent Billings
**Primary texts:** Matthew 21:1–27; Zechariah 9:9–10; Zechariah 14:3–5, 16–21; Psalm 118:25–29; Luke 19:41–42; Proverbs 27:18

---

## Overview

This episode walks through the triumphal entry and Jesus's first day of confrontation in Jerusalem during Passover week: the donkey ride from the east, the palm fronds, the weeping over the city, the cleansing of the temple, the cursed fig tree, and the challenge to Jesus's authority. Marty frames the whole scene as deliberate political theater — Jesus staging a counter-procession to Pilate's — and as a sorrowful confrontation with corrupt spiritual leadership.

Within the Session 3 arc (the king/Messiah embodying the one story), this episode is the opening of Jesus's final week. Where Session 1 established God's invitation to partnership and shalom, and Session 2 traced the partner's unfaithfulness and God's refusal to quit, here the King himself rides in to embody what the people would not become. He comes offering the shalom of Zechariah 9:10 — warhorses removed, peace proclaimed — and the crowd answers with the palm fronds of revolution. The King embodies the story the people keep misreading, and he weeps because they still want the wrong kingdom.

> **Marty:** "He rides into Jerusalem, not to conquer, but to die. He rides in to bring true peace that might last for an eternity, not a political peace that will last only for now."

## East/West Lens

BEMA's recurring interpretive contrast between Hebraic (Eastern, concrete, relational, story-shaped) and Greek-Western (abstract, systematized) ways of reading. Only the categories actually engaged in this episode are tagged below.

- **Block logic / holding the tension (Hebraic).** The episode refuses to flatten the triumphal entry into a single pleasant image. The same crowd that honors Jesus provokes his tears; worship and misunderstanding sit side by side without resolution.
  > **Marty:** "I just love the irony of how we gather together in churches all around the country and wave palm fronds on Palm Sunday, the very thing that made Jesus cry... my goodness, the irony is thick."

- **Concrete image over abstract concept (Hebraic).** Meaning is carried by physical objects and geography — a donkey vs. a stallion, east vs. west, palm fronds, a fig tree, a visible mountain on the horizon — rather than by stated doctrine.
  > **Marty:** "From the west comes Pilate, entering Jerusalem with power and white stallions and soldiers... From the other side on the eastern side of Jerusalem comes this dirty, sweaty rabbi riding on a stinky old donkey."

- **Text in conversation with text (Hebraic / intertextuality).** The crowd reads Jesus's donkey through the whole scroll of Zechariah — not just 9:9 but the ending in chapter 14 — and the hosanna is a quotation of the Sukkot song, Psalm 118. Scripture is understood by its echoes.
  > **Marty:** "Zechariah ends like the last chapter, the closing scene of Zechariah ends with... his feet will stand on the Mount of Olives."

- **Reader invited into the story (Hebraic / midrashic device).** The "two of everything" pattern is named explicitly as a rabbinic technique that pulls the hearer into a decision rather than merely delivering information.
  > **Marty:** "A teacher will often insert an additional character into the story as a way of inviting you as the listener, as the hearer, as the reader, to a moment of decision."

## Episode Flow

The transcript's teaching, in order. Coverage verification follows.

1. **Setup: Passover-week tension in Roman Judea.** Jerusalem as the empire's hardest region; the only people who refused to worship the emperor; a million+ Jews gathering to remember deliverance from a superpower.
2. **The Herodian/Roman backdrop.** Herod the Great's genius for stability; his three sons (Philip in the north, Antipas in the center, Archelaus in the south — who lasted under two years); Archelaus replaced by Pilate, Rome's "bulldog."
3. **Pilate's procession from the west.** Pilate's home at Caesarea Maritima; his annual descent to Jerusalem via Joppa; entering from the west on a white stallion with soldiers, trumpets, banners — a show of force meaning "Don't even think about it."
4. **Jesus's counter-procession from the east.** The humble rabbi entering from the opposite side with his talmidim — "a very literal picture of two kingdoms colliding."
5. **The entry read (Matthew 21:1–11).** The two disciples, the donkey and colt, the cloaks and branches, the "Hosanna to the Son of David," the city thrown into an uproar.
6. **Zechariah 9:9 and the crowd's recognition.** Jesus mounting the donkey triggers the connection to Zechariah; the people "catch it."
7. **Why palm fronds / why Sukkot language at Passover.** Hoshana as the Sukkot cry of Psalm 118; the many Sukkot references in Matthew 21; Zechariah 14's vision of all nations keeping Tabernacles.
8. **The Mount of Olives connection.** Zechariah 14:3–5 — the Lord's feet standing on the Mount of Olives, the mountain split, the Lord fighting for his people.
9. **The palm frond as Zealot emblem.** Later a crucifiable offense; waving it signals readiness for armed revolution — the people "come to Passover packing."
10. **Jesus weeps (Luke 19:41–42).** The crowd's hunger for violence is exactly why Jesus laments; "the things that make for peace... now hidden from your eyes."
11. **Zechariah 9:9–10 in full context.** The king removes chariots and warhorses and proclaims peace — the opposite of what the crowd wants.
12. **Marty's July 4th application.** Which kingdom do I actually want? A warning about baptizing a thirst for power, influence, and security; much of American culture "standing at the west side of Jerusalem, not the east."
13. **The two donkeys / Matthew's "two of everything."** Two blind men, two demoniacs, two donkeys; the riderless donkey as an invitation to the hearer: which kingdom do you want?
14. **Cleansing of the temple (Matthew 21:12–13).** The Booths of Ananus (Annas); the priests changing money and selling/approving/pricing/raising sacrifices; corruption planted in the Court of the Gentiles — "a house of prayer... a den of robbers."
15. **The whip of cords and two cleansings.** The possibility that "a whip of cords" (John 2) is a rabbi shaking his tzitzit/tallit; John placing the cleansing at the start vs. the Synoptics at the end; the Leviticus 14 two-stage mold inspection as a frame — cleared once, then (mold spread) the house torn down.
16. **Children's praise and the leaders' indignation (Matthew 21:14–17).** Jesus answering with "out of the mouths of children" — calling out the priests from their own hymnal, the Psalms.
17. **The cursed fig tree (Matthew 21:18–22).** Not a temper tantrum; the fig tree as a symbol of God's *leaders* (via Proverbs 27:18 and the rav/rabbi link), not of Israel; "this mountain" as Herodium — a mountain Herod literally built — and faith like a mustard seed able to move it.
18. **The authority challenge (Matthew 21:23–27).** The priests ask about Jesus's s'micha; Jesus deflects to the source of John's baptism, implying John is his rabbi; the priests cornered, Jesus declines to answer.

**Coverage verification:** All major transcript segments are represented in the flow above. No significant teaching block was dropped. Minor asides (the pronunciation of "Pontius," the Caesarea underwater-concrete harbor, the closing "email Brent" banter) are not doctrinally load-bearing; the one substantive aside not folded into a Key Lesson is parked in *Other Talking Points* below.

## Key Lessons

Framed against the Session 3 arc — the King embodying the one story — and how this episode advances it.

### 1. The King stages the story the people keep refusing to read

The triumphal entry is a deliberate counter-procession. Jesus answers Pilate's western show of force with an eastern parody of it: a donkey against a stallion, talmidim against soldiers, shalom against empire. This is the King *embodying* the kingdom God has always offered — the shalom of Session 1 — directly in front of the empire that embodies the opposite.

> **Marty:** "To call back to Session 1, Brent, we might say Pilate with his empire, Jesus, with his shalom. A narrative of two empires."

**Arc advance:** Session 2 showed the partner choosing other kings and other kingdoms. Here the true King shows up in person and performs the alternative in the open — and the crowd still cheers for the wrong one.

### 2. The King weeps because the people want the right text and the wrong kingdom

The crowd is not ignorant; they read Zechariah accurately and respond with Scripture-shaped praise. But they stop at the Lord fighting the nations (Zechariah 14) and skip the warhorses being removed (Zechariah 9:10). Their hunger for violent revolution is precisely what makes Jesus weep.

> **Marty:** "He talks about peace. He talks about taking away the warhorses, taking away the chariots, taking away the violence — and they want violence. Jesus goes, 'Oh, you just don't get it.'"

**Arc advance:** The partner's recurring failure in Session 2 was wanting God's power on the partner's terms. The King now grieves that failure face-to-face, embodying a peace the people would rather not have.

### 3. The riderless donkey is addressed to the listener

Matthew's "two of everything" is a decision device. The extra donkey means there is a mount with no rider — left, deliberately, for you.

> **Marty:** "As you watch Jesus tearfully ride into His death, you see Him glance over His shoulder, at the rider-less donkey that you are faced with a question, which kingdom do you really want?"

**Arc advance:** The story is not a spectator sport. The King embodies it *so that* the hearer is pulled into the same choice Israel kept facing — covenant partnership or empire.

### 4. The King confronts corrupt leadership, not the people

The temple cleansing and the cursed fig tree aim at the shepherds, not the sheep. The fig tree, by the rabbinic reading of Proverbs 27:18, stands for leaders — those who guard the fig tree / serve the *rav* — not for Israel. Jesus's anger is on God's behalf about leadership that has failed his people "like sheep without a shepherd."

> **Marty:** "A fig tree symbolizes not the people of God, but the leaders of God... Jesus is very, very upset on God's behalf about the spiritual leadership that is failing his people like sheep without a shepherd."

**Arc advance:** God's refusal to quit (Session 2) now takes the form of the King coming after the corruption that stands between God and the Gentile outsider — in the very court reserved for them.

### 5. Authority comes from the margins, not the system

When challenged on his s'micha, Jesus points to John's baptism — authority that never came through the formal school system. The King's legitimacy is grounded in the prophetic margins, consistent with a Messiah who may himself have been treated as a *mamzer*.

> **Marty:** "John didn't have his authority through your fancy pants school system... You tell me."

**Arc advance:** The one who embodies the story carries an authority the institution cannot certify — the kingdom keeps breaking in from outside the approved channels.

## Narrative Tensions

Surface problems the episode raises, each with a classification tag, a cited quote, the relevant text, and either a Hebraic resolution or an honest "Open — dance with it."

**Tension 1 — Sukkot language at a Passover feast.** *(Classification: cultural / liturgical anomaly.)* The chapter is saturated with Sukkot imagery (palm fronds, the Psalm 118 hosanna) even though the feast is Passover.
> **Marty:** "There are so many Sukkot references in this chapter of Matthew. Some scholars have suggested He didn't come on Passover, He's actually here on Sukkot."
> **Resolution (Hebraic / intertextual):** The Sukkot cues are triggered by Jesus's quotation of Zechariah, whose closing vision (14:16–21) is about all nations keeping Tabernacles. The crowd reaches for the Sukkot script because Jesus invoked the prophet who ends there. Marty explicitly rejects relocating the event to Sukkot. See [Zechariah 14:16–21](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah+14%3A16-21&version=NIV).

**Tension 2 — Jesus weeps at his own welcome.** *(Classification: logic/emotional gap.)* The crowd honors Jesus with Scripture and praise, yet he responds with tears.
> **Marty:** "Their response makes Jesus weep."
> **Resolution (Hebraic):** Reading Zechariah 9 in full context resolves it — the crowd wants the warhorse-God of chapter 14 while skipping the peace-proclaiming King of 9:10. Their praise is aimed at the wrong kingdom. See [Luke 19:41–42](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+19%3A41-42&version=NIV) and [Zechariah 9:9–10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Zechariah+9%3A9-10&version=NIV).

**Tension 3 — The seemingly petty cursing of the fig tree.** *(Classification: apparent contradiction with Jesus's character.)* Jesus appears to throw "a really weird little temper tantrum," cursing a tree for having no fruit when it is not even fig season.
> **Marty:** "He has a little temper tantrum and kills a fig tree. It's just the weirdest story, right? But we know better."
> **Resolution (Hebraic / symbolic):** In the Jewish world a fig tree never represents Israel (the olive does); via Proverbs 27:18 it represents *leaders*. The cursing is an enacted parable against corrupt spiritual leadership, not a loss of temper. See [Proverbs 27:18](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Proverbs+27%3A18&version=NIV).

**Tension 4 — One cleansing or two?** *(Classification: translation/harmonization disagreement across Gospels.)* John places the temple cleansing at the start of Jesus's ministry; the Synoptics place it at the end.
> **Marty:** "John puts the cleansing of the temple at the very beginning of Jesus's ministry in John 2. Every other Gospel puts it here at the end... There could be two cleansings."
> **Open — dance with it:** Marty offers the Leviticus 14 two-stage mold inspection as a *juicy possibility* (cleared once, then torn down when the "mold" has spread), but holds it loosely: "I could be stretching it... I leave that with our listeners to wrestle with." The question of two cleansings is left genuinely open. See [Leviticus 14](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Leviticus+14&version=NIV).

**Tension 5 — Why two donkeys when Jesus rides one?** *(Classification: narrative detail / apparent redundancy.)* Matthew reports a donkey *and* a colt, though a rider needs only one.
> **Marty:** "To ride two donkeys at the same time would be quite the feat. Why do we have another donkey?"
> **Resolution (Hebraic / midrashic):** Matthew's "two of everything" is a deliberate rabbinic device inserting an extra character to invite the hearer into the story — the riderless donkey is for you. See [Matthew 21:1–11](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+21%3A1-11&version=NIV).

## Terminology

- **Hoshana / Hosanna** — "Lord, save us," the cry of Psalm 118, the Sukkot song. *(Marty: "The Hebrew for that is hoshana, hoshana.")*
- **Lulav / palm fronds** — the palm branch of Sukkot, also the emblem of the Zealots; later a crucifiable offense to wave publicly. Waving it at Passover signals readiness for revolution. *(Marty: "The palm frond was the symbol of which party... Zealots. It was a crucifiable offense to wave a palm frond in the Roman Empire.")*
- **Talmidim** — a rabbi's disciples. *(Marty: "Pilate with his soldiers, Jesus, with his talmidim.")*
- **Shalom** — God's wholeness/peace, set against empire. *(Marty: "Jesus, with his shalom.")*
- **Booths of Ananus (Annas)** — the priest-run temple market for money-changing and sacrifice sales. *(Marty: "The Booths of Ananus were run by the priesthood.")*
- **Tzitzit / tallit** — a rabbi's tassels / prayer shawl, possibly the "whip of cords." *(Marty: "A Rabbi using his tzitzit, his tassels... He shakes his tallit at them.")*
- **Rav** — master; the root behind *rabbi*. *(Marty: "The word for master is the word rav. It is the same root that rabbi is going to come from later.")*
- **S'micha** — rabbinic authority/ordination, conferred by the laying on of hands. *(Marty: "They want to know about His s'micha. Where'd you get your s'micha from?")*
- **Mamzer** — an outsider of questionable birth status; possibly how Jesus was regarded. *(Marty: "If he would've been seen as a true illegitimate birth, a mamzer, I don't think they would've let him in.")*
- **Gat shemanim** — Gethsemane, an olive press in the Kidron Valley. *(Marty: "He's in an olive press just off the eastern side of Jerusalem in the Kidron Valley.")*
- **Bet sefer / bet midrash / bet talmud** — the stages of the Jewish education system. *(Marty: "You didn't walk through bet sefer and bet midrash and bet talmud like everybody else did.")*

## Illustrations & Images

- **Two processions, two kingdoms.** Pilate from the west on a war stallion with soldiers, trumpets, and banners; Jesus from the east on a donkey with his ragtag talmidim. *(Marty: "A very literal picture of two kingdoms colliding together. Pilate on his stallion, Jesus on his donkey... Pilate instilling fear, Jesus pronouncing favor.")*
- **Coming to Passover 'packing.'** The crowd carrying palm fronds — Zealot emblems — into the feast. *(Marty: "They come to Passover packing. They're packing palm front heat, like they're ready to Sukkot this thing.")*
- **Palm Sunday irony.** Churches annually reenacting the very scene that made Jesus weep. *(Marty: "Every year we hand our children palm fronds and have them reenact the very scene that made our savior weep.")*
- **The riderless donkey.** Jesus glancing over his shoulder at the empty mount left for the hearer. *(Marty: "You see Him glance over His shoulder, at the rider-less donkey.")*
- **A fig tree as a backyard candy store.** Figs as the sweetest thing available, standing for the sweet "fruit" of a rabbi's teaching. *(Marty: "A fig tree is like a candy store in your backyard... A fig is one of the sweetest things that you can get your hands on.")*
- **Herod moving a mountain.** Herodium built on a man-made mountain, pointed at as "this mountain" that faith can throw into the sea. *(Brent: "On a mountain that didn't used to be there." / Marty: "Herod literally built a mountain, he moved a mountain.")*
- **Rabbinical judo.** Jesus's counter-question about John's baptism cornering the priests. *(Marty: "Just brilliant rabbinical judo if you ask me.")*
- **Mold in the house.** The Leviticus 14 two-stage inspection as a frame for two temple cleansings. *(Marty: "In Leviticus, we are given instructions about what to do when your house gets mold.")*

## Discussion Questions

1. Marty argues the triumphal entry is a political maneuver — a counter-procession to Pilate's. If Pilate enters from the west with power and Jesus from the east with shalom, where do you find yourself standing on a given day, and why? *(Marty: "I think far too much of our American culture has us standing at the west side of Jerusalem, not the east.")*
2. The crowd read Zechariah correctly but wanted the wrong kingdom — the warhorse-God of chapter 14 without the peace-proclaiming King of 9:10. Where might we be reading Scripture accurately yet still cheering for "a revolution that comes with my kind of power and my kind of might"?
3. If the fig tree represents corrupt *leaders* rather than the people (Proverbs 27:18), what does Jesus's cursing of it say about how the King deals with the shepherds who have failed the sheep — and about leadership today?
4. There is a riderless donkey in the story, inserted (Marty says) to invite you in. If you had to answer Jesus's over-the-shoulder question — "which kingdom do you really want?" — honestly, what would you say, and what would it cost you?
5. Jesus grounds his authority in John's baptism rather than the formal system. What does it mean that the King's legitimacy comes from the margins, and where do we overvalue "letters after the name" over genuine prophetic authority?

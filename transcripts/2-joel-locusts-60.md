### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Locusts.** Joel (a Babylonian-period prophet, three chapters, read in full) opens immediately with his governing metaphor: a devastating **locust plague** — "What the locust swarm has left, the great locusts have eaten..." In the Middle East, shifting winds can blow in massive locust plagues of staggering magnitude; Joel uses this to picture Babylon's invasion. The sheer number of Babylonians is the swarm; and after they pass, like locusts they leave nothing — fields, vines, fig trees, grain, oil all consumed.

**Structure — image, plea, image, plea, hope:**
1. **The image (ch. 1):** the locust devastation establishes the plot of God's people.
2. **First call to repent:** "Declare a holy fast, call a sacred assembly... cry out to the LORD," for "the day of the LORD is near."
3. **Return to the image (ch. 2):** the locust metaphor resumes, now so blended with the "large and mighty army" that you cannot tell whether Joel means literal locusts or the Babylonian army — almost certainly on purpose ("they scale walls like soldiers... like thieves they enter through the windows").
4. **Second, deeper plea to repent:** climaxing in the famous call.
5. **A decisive turn to hope (chs. 2–3).**

**"Rend your heart, not your garments" (2:13).** Tearing one's garment from collar to hem (top to bottom) was the ancient Eastern expression of grief. Marty relays Ray's story of a guide who died of a heart attack on a trip: at 2 a.m. Ray brought the news, the wife fainted at the door, and the son — a doctor — after reviving her, grabbed his gown and ripped it top to bottom; Ray never forgot the sound. (As a PS: when the temple curtain — said to be four to six inches thick — is torn at Jesus' death in the Gospels, a Jewish reader hears *God the Father rending his garments* at the death of his Son.) God's point: don't merely rend your garments — *tear your heart in two*, be genuinely changed and moved. God is "gracious and compassionate, slow to anger and abounding in love" (echoing Exodus 34).

**"I will pour out my Spirit on all people" (2:28-32).** The great hope-vision: not only on men who lead, but "your sons and daughters will prophesy, your old men will dream dreams, your young men will see visions — even on my servants, both men and women." "Everyone who calls on the name of the LORD will be saved." This is the passage Peter quotes at Pentecost (Acts 2) — a forward-pointing image that lands in the New Testament in a big way.

**"I will repay you for the years the locusts have eaten" (2:25).** The core promise of restoration: grain, new wine, and oil restored; the threshing floors filled; "never again will my people be shamed."

**Chapter 3 — judgment of the nations in the Valley of Jehoshaphat.** The nations are gathered and put on trial for what they did to God's inheritance (scattering his people, trading boys for prostitutes, selling girls for wine, selling Judah to the Greeks). The familiar prophetic reversal: "Beat your plowshares into swords and your pruning hooks into spears" (an inversion of Isaiah's peace vision). "Multitudes, multitudes in the valley of decision." The LORD will be a refuge and stronghold for his people.

**The bookend of new wine, and the acacias.** Joel opens with new wine *snatched from the lips* of a people about to enjoy it (Marty's wine-tasting image: the glass raised to the mouth, then grabbed away — their stolen future). It closes with new wine *dripping from the mountains*, milk flowing from the hills, and a fountain flowing from the LORD's house watering even the desert **acacias** (the acacia from Session 1 — the wood of the tabernacle and the desert tree of life). We are confronted again with images that began the journey out of Egypt: the prophets' woes and discipline ask us to *remember our own story*.

**The arc of the prophets: warning, woes, hope.** Joel marks the hinge. The Pre-Assyrian prophets were *warning*; the Assyrian/Babylonian prophets described the *woes* befalling everyone. Now, heading into the Exilic period, the tone shifts decisively toward *hope* — like a parent who, after ending the chaos and doling out the punishment, now walks the child through to the end of being grounded: "you've been disciplined; hang in there, because God is going to put it back together."

### Hebrew Words Explained

- **miqsat ma'asay haTorah** (“some of the works of the law”) — the specific portion of Torah that functioned as identity markers making a person distinctly Jewish: circumcision, kosher dietary law, Sabbath, tassels, prohibition on blended fabric, and cleanliness/purity laws. A Dead Sea Scroll from Qumran is literally titled *Miqsat Ma'asay Ha-Torah* (4QMMT), listing such boundary-marking regulations. The debate at the Jerusalem Council is whether Gentiles must take on these works of the law to belong.
- **Three divisions of the law** (framework from the “New Perspective on Paul”) — scholars distinguish *cultic law* (temple/sacrificial system), *ethical law* (how to treat others), and *miqsat ma'asay haTorah* (the identity-marker works). When Paul argues against “works of the law,” he is not rejecting ethics; he is rejecting the demand that Gentiles adopt the Jewish identity markers to be justified.
- **theosabes** (Greek, “God-fearer”) — Gentiles who worshiped the God of Israel and attended synagogue but had not fully converted. They are the exact population at the center of the council's question.
- **b'nei Avraham** / proselyte — “children of Abraham,” a full convert who took on circumcision and the identity markers. The Shammai position effectively required Gentiles to become *b'nei Avraham* first.
- **Hillel vs. Shammai** — the two great rabbinic schools frame the dispute: the Shammai-type position says a Gentile is justified by obedience/full conversion (take on the works of the law first); the Hillel-type position (echoed in James's ruling) says a Gentile is justified by faith and only the Noahic essentials are required.
- **Noahic covenant** — the rabbinic “Noahide” expectations for all humanity. James's four prohibitions (abstain from food polluted by idols / idolatry, from sexual immorality, from the meat of strangled animals, and from blood — the sanctity of life) track these universal, pre-Sinai requirements rather than the full Jewish identity markers.
- **binding and loosing** — the rabbinic authority to declare a practice forbidden (binding) or permitted (loosing). “It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us” (Acts 15:28) is the council exercising exactly this Matthew 16:19 authority communally.

### Hebrew Words Explained

**The image: Purim.** Esther is the last book of the Tanakh covered in Session 2 — a *writing* (Ketuvim), not a prophet. It is the story behind the festival of **Purim**.

**Purim.** A later, non-Levitical festival (added, like Hanukkah, after the biblical festivals ordained at Sinai — Passover, Unleavened Bread/First Fruits, Weeks; and the fall feasts Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, Sukkot). *Purim* means **"lots."** At first glance the name seems to come from the lots Haman cast to pick a date to destroy the Jews — but the episode argues it actually refers to **Esther's** lot: when she cast her lot before God ("if I die, I die; if I live, I live"). It's a two-day celebration of the book of Esther — the Jewish "Halloween," with costumes, because **God is never named in the book yet is present throughout — God in disguise/costume.** (Esther is also the only Old Testament book not found anywhere in the Dead Sea Scrolls, which fuels discussion about its late canonization.)

**Who the book is for.** A remnant-period book under **Persia**, but written not for those who *returned* to rebuild (Ezra, Nehemiah, Haggai, Zechariah) but for the large contingent of Judaism that *stayed* in Persia. (Only ~a quarter-million of perhaps 5–6 million Jews returned.) Its lesson: how to persevere, influence, and be obedient while living inside empire.

**The story (flyby).** King **Xerxes** of Persia, at a drunken feast, demands Queen **Vashti** present herself (the Hebrew insinuates a striptease — crown and nothing else); she refuses and is deposed (the officials fear their own wives will follow suit). A "Bachelor/American Idol" contest gathers the unwed virgins into the harem — in the Hebrew context, sexual conquest, each given one night to impress the king. A Jewish girl, **Hadassah** (Hebrew name), enters under the Persian name **Esther**. She is the adopted daughter ("redeemed to sit at his table," the Genesis sense) of her cousin **Mordecai**, who tells her to hide her Jewish identity.

**The name Esther / Ishtar.** *Esther* derives from **Ishtar** (the Persian form; the Canaanite/Phoenician equivalent is Asherah/Ashtoret — the goddess of sexual fertility whose cult involved prostitution). (Aside: "Easter" is a Roman mishearing of Ishtar.)

**Two readings of how Esther wins:**
- **Take A (textual-critical):** she wins by "sketchy means" — entering and winning a sex contest. Marty's sermon on this angered many but gave hope to those who got where they are through broken, sinful roads: she is *perfectly placed* at a critical moment, and what matters is not how she got there but whether she's willing to be used "for such a time as this."
- **Take B (the dominant Jewish/midrashic reading):** she does *not* win sexually but impresses the king with her **intellect and knowledge of Torah** — one midrash says she tells him the stories of Torah, and he chooses her for her wit.

**Joseph parallels.** Esther 2 is laced with echoes of Joseph in Egypt: brought to the palace/harem, winning the favor of the overseer (Hegai), entrusted with charge, then given robes and a crown and raised to power — exactly as Pharaoh exalted Joseph. Mordecai, like a faithful teacher, walks the courtyard daily (the midrash: teaching her the Text). This makes deep sense for an audience — like Joseph — *staying* in a foreign empire, learning to persevere, influence, and obey.

**The plot and Esther's shrewdness.** Mordecai uncovers an assassination plot and saves the king; the arrogant **Haman**, enraged that Mordecai won't bow, engineers an edict to destroy the Jews. Mordecai presses Esther: doing nothing is the same as condoning it (Fohrman links the phrasing to a passage in Numbers). Esther risks death to approach the unsummoned king — but rather than simply asking him to reverse the edict (which would make the king lose face), she plays a shrewd game: she requests a *banquet*, and insists **Haman** attend, planting suspicion in the king's mind (a rival? an affair?). A sleepless king hears the chronicle of Mordecai saving his life (God in disguise again); Haman, asked how to honor "the man the king delights to honor," assumes it's himself and ends up parading Mordecai through the streets. At the banquet Esther deflects attention from her own Jewishness onto Haman, who is then hanged on the 70-foot gallows he built. The king does *not* reverse the edict (he must save face) but gives his signet authority to Esther and Mordecai, who shrewdly issue a *counter*-edict (the Jews may defend themselves), forcing every subject to ask "what does the king really want?" — and the darkness falls apart in the light of their wisdom. Marty hears Jesus' "be innocent as doves but shrewd as serpents."

**The lesson.** To stand before injustice and say nothing is to condone it. To engage the injustice of empire — to live in Persia — requires more than obedience and moral rightness; it requires a *shrewd wisdom* that forces empire to crumble in the light of goodness.

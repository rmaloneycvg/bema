### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Diyn.** Nahum is the counterpart to Jonah. Where Jonah (image: *potential*) shows God's unbelievable patience toward Assyria — staying his hand because he sees the potential in even our worst enemies — Nahum shows the other side: God does, finally, act against entrenched evil.

- **diyn** (דין) — a Hebrew word for justice, not the dominant one. It denotes *retributive* justice: God's pronouncement that discipline is on its way. Distinct from (but not strictly the opposite of) *mishpat*; Nahum is "the other side of the mishpat coin." The whole book proclaims God's *diyn* against Nineveh.
- **mishpat** (משפט) — restorative justice: putting things back together, making them into what they should be. God's justice is driven by grace, mercy, and forgiveness — but a "weighted coin" (Brent): it won't come up fifty-fifty. Sometimes, to restore, God must stop the madness, set boundaries, and *discipline* — which is larger than mere punishment because its aim is restoration and order out of destructive chaos.

**The 1000:3 ratio.** From the Name proclaimed in Exodus 34: God shows *chesed* (loyal love) to a thousand generations of those who love him, and repays the sin of those who hate him only to the third and fourth generation. Marty (who wrote the Moses chapter in *The Gospel of Being Human*) stresses this is deliberate hyperbole and juxtaposition — 1000:3, not 1000:1000 and not 500:500. But it is also *not* 1000:0. That "three" is real, and the book of Nahum is about the three. God is slow to anger, but not *never* to anger.

**Reading from the right vantage point.** Nahum is a prophecy *concerning* Nineveh but written *to* Israel — Israel now crushed under Assyrian rule. To the oppressed, this "alarming picture of God" is actually a message of *hope and liberation*: God sees, God cares, God will act. The danger is reading the Bible "from the top of the power pyramid" and weaponizing a prophecy of hope-for-the-oppressed against one's own enemies. The Assyrians were historically brutal — known for "staking" (impaling enemies on poles) and flaying — so Israel's cry "God, do you not care?" is answered: yes.

**"I am against you."** Twice God declares "I am against you" (2:13; 3:5). Marty, who insists God is *for* all humanity flourishing, reads this as prophetic hyperbole: God is not against the Assyrians as *people/children of God* but against them *as oppressors* — the same thing God said about his own people when they "became the anti-story," the anti-shalom to his shalom. The 1000:3 "three" shows up precisely when a people becomes the anti-story.

**Source A / Source B.** Chapter 1 scores for the idolatry/morality column (Source A — explicit idols, esp. v.14). But chapters 2–3 are saturated with *injustice* language (Source B): a "city of blood," piles of dead, bodies without number, the crack of whips, endless cruelty. The book ends on injustice — "who has not felt your endless cruelty."

**Closing synthesis (Marty's notes):** Jonah explains God's patient inaction but doesn't resolve the ongoing problem of evil; our souls still cry out at injustice for deliverance. "God is patient and patient and patient... and then God acts." That is Nahum. As one more daughter is violated and one more child impaled, the people cry out, and God — who still sees the potential in each Assyrian — finally announces "Enough is enough." We hold Jonah in one hand and Nahum in the other: God hears the cry of the hurting, is in the business of *mishpat*, and sometimes proclaims *diyn*. The invitation remains: trust his goodness, his timing, his perspective — trust the story.

**Bonus coinage:** "verocity" — Marty's accidental blend of *veracity* + *ferocity* for the prophet making his point with teeth (he meant ferocity).

## Other Book References and Recommendations

- **Jonah** (previous episode) — the paired prophet; God's patience/potential toward Assyria, the other side of the coin from Nahum's *diyn*.
- **Exodus 34:6-7** — the source of the 1000:3 ratio (loyal love to a thousand generations; sin repaid to the third/fourth generation).
- **Joshua** — the earlier Session 2 conversation framing God's commitment to protect the people he loves and to confront oppression.
- **Isaiah / 2 Kings (Hezekiah, Sennacherib)** — Hezekiah's reforms stay God's hand so Judah survives Assyria while the northern kingdom falls.
- **Marty Solomon & Reed Dent, *The Gospel of Being Human*** — Marty's Moses chapter develops the 1000:3 ratio; Reed's chapter covers Jonah.
- **Abraham Joshua Heschel** — the *pathos* of the prophet; God has feelings, and the prophet feels them, which is where the ferocious language comes from.
- **Impact Campus Ministries / BEMA** — equipping college students to live between Jonah and Nahum: a world full of both injustice and potential, learning to be on the right side of the mishpat coin.

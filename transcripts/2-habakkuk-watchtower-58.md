### Habakkuk 1:1-17 (NET)

**1** The following is the message which God revealed to Habakkuk the prophet: **2** How long, LORD, must I cry for help? But you do not listen! I call out to you, “Violence!” But you do not intervene! **3** Why do you force me to witness injustice? Why do you put up with wrongdoing? Destruction and violence confront me; conflict is present and one must endure strife. **4** For this reason the law lacks power, and justice is never carried out. Indeed, the wicked intimidate the innocent. For this reason justice is perverted. **5** “Look at the nations and pay attention! You will be shocked and amazed! For I will do something in your lifetime that you will not believe even though you are forewarned. **6** Look, I am about to empower the Babylonians, that ruthless and greedy nation. They sweep across the surface of the earth, seizing dwelling places that do not belong to them. **7** They are frightening and terrifying; they decide for themselves what is right. **8** Their horses are faster than leopards and more alert than wolves in the desert. Their horses gallop, their horses come a great distance; like a vulture they swoop down quickly to devour their prey. **9** All of them intend to do violence; every face is determined. They take prisoners as easily as one scoops up sand. **10** They mock kings and laugh at rulers. They laugh at every fortified city; they build siege ramps and capture them. **11** They sweep by like the wind and pass on. But the one who considers himself a god will be held guilty.” **12** LORD, you have been active from ancient times; my sovereign God, you are immortal. LORD, you have made them your instrument of judgment. Protector, you have appointed them as your instrument of punishment. **13** You are too just to tolerate evil; you are unable to condone wrongdoing. **14** You made people like fish in the sea, like animals in the sea that have no ruler. **15** The Babylonian tyrant pulls them all up with a fishhook; he hauls them in with his throw net. When he catches them in his dragnet, he is very happy. **16** Because of his success he offers sacrifices to his throw net and burns incense to his dragnet; for because of them he has plenty of food, and more than enough to eat. **17** Will he then continue to fill and empty his throw net? Will he always destroy nations and spare none?

### Habakkuk 3:1-19 (NET)

**1** This is a prayer of Habakkuk the prophet: **2** LORD, I have heard the report of what you did; I am awed, LORD, by what you accomplished. In our time repeat those deeds; in our time reveal them again. But when you cause turmoil, remember to show us mercy! **3** God comes from Teman, the sovereign one from Mount Paran. Selah. His splendor covers the skies, his glory fills the earth. **4** He is as bright as lightning; a two-pronged lightning bolt flashes from his hand. This is the outward display of his power. **5** Plague goes before him; **6** He takes his battle position and shakes the earth; with a mere look he frightens the nations. The ancient mountains disintegrate; the primeval hills are flattened. He travels on the ancient roads. **7** I see the tents of Cushan overwhelmed by trouble; the tent curtains of the land of Midian are shaking. **8** Is the LORD mad at the rivers? Are you angry with the rivers? Are you enraged at the sea? Is this why you climb into your horse-drawn chariots, your victorious chariots? **9** Your bow is ready for action; you commission your arrows. Selah. You cause flash floods on the earth’s surface. **10** When the mountains see you, they shake. The torrential downpour sweeps through. The great deep shouts out; it lifts its hands high. **11** The sun and moon stand still in their courses; the flash of your arrows drives them away, the bright light of your lightning-quick spear. **12** You furiously stomp on the earth, you angrily trample down the nations. **13** You march out to deliver your people, to deliver your special servant. You strike the leader of the wicked nation, laying him open from the lower body to the neck. Selah. **14** You pierce the heads of his warriors with a spear. They storm forward to scatter us; they shout with joy as if they were plundering the poor with no opposition. **15** But you trample on the sea with your horses, on the surging, raging waters. **16** I listened and my stomach churned; the sound made my lips quiver. My frame went limp, as if my bones were decaying, and I shook as I tried to walk. I long for the day of distress to come upon the people who attack us. **17** When the fig tree does not bud, and there are no grapes on the vines; when the olive trees do not produce, and the fields yield no crops; when the sheep disappear from the pen, and there are no cattle in the stalls, **18** I will rejoice because of the LORD; I will be happy because of the God who delivers me! **19** The sovereign LORD is my source of strength.

### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Watchtower.** Habakkuk — one of Marty's favorite prophets — is a Babylonian-period prophet structured as a *dialogue* between the prophet and God. The whole short book (three chapters) is read.

**The structure — complaint, dodge, watchtower, answer, worship:**
1. **Habakkuk's first complaint (ch. 1):** "How long, LORD, must I call for help, but you do not listen?" The wicked hem in the righteous; justice is perverted. Habakkuk, like Abraham bargaining over Sodom, digs in his heels — unusual for a prophet, who normally just receives and delivers a word. He is perhaps the closest rival to Jonah in arguing with God.
2. **God's answer:** "I am raising up the Babylonians" — described as ruthless, dreaded, a law to themselves. From Habakkuk's seat this only sharpens the problem: *that* is exactly what I'm objecting to.
3. **Habakkuk's second complaint:** God, your eyes are too pure to look on evil — why then tolerate the treacherous and let the wicked swallow those more righteous than themselves?
4. **The watchtower (2:1):** "I will stand at my watch and station myself on the ramparts; I will look to see what he will say to me." A gutsy *chutzpah* posture: that first answer didn't satisfy me, so I'll wait right here until you give one that does.
5. **God's fuller answer (ch. 2):** "Write down the revelation and make it plain on tablets... for the revelation awaits an appointed time... though it lingers, wait for it." The famous line — **"the righteous person will live by his faithfulness"** (2:4) — followed by a series of *woes* fusing Source A and Source B: woe to him who piles up stolen goods, who builds his house by unjust gain, who builds a city with bloodshed, who makes idols that cannot speak. The "divine mic drop": "The LORD is in his holy temple; let all the earth be silent before him."

**Whose condemnation is it, really?** Chapter 2 ostensibly describes *Babylon* — but the indictment echoes word-for-word the charges God leveled against *his own people* through every prior prophet. Habakkuk seems to realize he cannot point the finger at Babylon's empire-building when his own people devoted themselves to the same thing — and this humbling gets "calculated into" his response in chapter 3.

**Genesis and Exodus callbacks.** The hymn of chapter 3 is laced with Israel's own story:
- "The earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD *as the waters cover the sea*" (2:14) and the following "woe" about drunkenness and nakedness evoke **the Flood and Noah in the vineyard** (Brent's catch) — God restoring/remaking creation as he did in the flood ("waters over seas" only makes full sense against Genesis 1's waters over the deep).
- "You came out to deliver your people... you trampled the sea with your horses" — **Exodus** language.
- "With his own spear you pierced his head" — echoes **David and Goliath**.
Habakkuk walks through his people's history, grounding his trust: God has always taken care of us.

**shigionoth** (3:1) — an obscure term (probably a literary or musical direction) that even scholars can't define.

**Marty's life verse (3:17-19).** Habakkuk's closing worship is uttered while *staring at a dismal future* — Babylon is coming and his world will be laid waste — which is what makes it so far from prosperity-gospel optimism: "Though the fig tree does not bud and there are no grapes on the vines... yet I will rejoice in the LORD... The Sovereign LORD is my strength; he makes my feet like the feet of a deer and enables me to tread on the heights." (Note the musical tag "for the director of music, on my stringed instruments" — this is *art*, a song to get the people through hard times: "this sucks, but we're going to make it.")

**Why God sometimes shows the future (and sometimes doesn't):** Habakkuk, unlike Job, is granted a glimpse of God's plan — and his knees tremble. Marty's reflection: sometimes we think we want answers but don't realize what we're asking; God may keep the future from us precisely to enable us to walk into it. Habakkuk trusts the story enough to face opposition and still affirm that God's story gets the last word.

## Other Book References and Recommendations

- **Genesis 1 / the Flood / Noah in the vineyard (Genesis 6-9)** — the "waters cover the sea" and drunkenness/nakedness allusions in Habakkuk 2.
- **Exodus** — "you came out to deliver your people" and "you trampled the sea with your horses" in Habakkuk 3.
- **1 Samuel 17 (David and Goliath)** — "with his own spear you pierced his head."
- **Genesis 18 (Abraham bargaining for Sodom)** and **Jonah** — the other biblical figures who argue/contend with God, paralleling Habakkuk's chutzpah.
- **Job** — contrasted as a figure God does *not* grant a glimpse of the plan.
- Review of prophetic images: Amos = plumb line / ripe fruit, Hosea = prostitute, Micah = judge, 1 Isaiah = vineyard, Jonah = potential, Nahum = diyn, Zephaniah = t'shuvah (return), 2 Isaiah = woe, Jeremiah = weeping, Lamentations = lament and hope (ACA), and now Habakkuk = watchtower.

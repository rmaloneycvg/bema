### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Hope.** 4 Isaiah (chapters **55-66**) carries the fourth and final Isaiah voice and opens the **remnant** period of prophetic history. (Marty didn't always teach a fourth voice; he's increasingly convinced of a distinct shift here.) After 1 Isaiah's vineyard/warning, 2 Isaiah's woes, and 3 Isaiah's servant/comfort, the tone shifts again to open invitation: "Come, all you who are thirsty, come to the waters... come, buy wine and milk without money and without cost" (55:1). The suffering is over; God is providing.

**Remnant.** The *remnant* is the group left over when it seems everyone has fallen away and everything is destroyed — "a small remaining quantity... a surviving trace." There is always a remnant (cf. Elijah's 7,000 who never bowed to Baal); you are never utterly obliterated — there is always a portion set aside to rebuild with. 4 Isaiah speaks to this remnant — the people who *persevered* (the suffering servant of 3 Isaiah) — and says: come back home, come thirsty, come hungry, let me restore you.

**"My ways are not your ways" is about scandalous grace (55:8-9).** This famous verse is badly misquoted — we use it to mean "God works in mysterious ways." In context (right after "Let the wicked forsake their ways... let them turn to the LORD, and he will have mercy on them... for he will freely pardon"), it is about God's *scandalous grace and forgiveness*: "I know it doesn't make sense, but my ways are not your ways — all you have to do is come back home." (Marty credits Rachel Held Evans's *Evolving in Monkey Town* / later retitled *Faith Unraveled* for this correction.)

**Hope rooted in warning (Isaiah 58).** The hope is not "Care Bear hope" but "Noah-rainbow hope" — it comes with a charge. Isaiah 58 confronts a people who *fast* and perform piety while exploiting their workers and quarreling. God asks: "Is this the fast I have chosen?" No — "to loose the chains of injustice... to set the oppressed free... to share your food with the hungry... to provide the poor wanderer with shelter... when you see the naked, to clothe them." This sounds exactly like 1 Isaiah — so much so that some argue 1 and 4 Isaiah were originally a single voice with 2–3 dropped in the middle. God is calling back to the original condemnation: in the vineyard I came looking for *tzedakah* (righteousness) and found only *tze'akah* (the cry of the oppressed). True obedience truly cares for people, especially those on the underbelly of society.

**Full circle to the vineyard — "Repairer of Broken Walls."** 58 continues: "You will be like a well-watered garden... your people will rebuild the ancient ruins... you will be called Repairer of Broken Walls, Restorer of Streets with Dwellings." This returns to 1 Isaiah's vineyard image — where God threatened to break down the walls and let briars grow — and promises that if the remnant learns from its past, it can rebuild the mission it lost and repair the broken walls. (Marty considered calling the image "restoration" but settled on *hope* — hope *for* restoration, through hard work and becoming the people God called them to be.)

**The Sabbath bookend (58:13-14).** Strikingly, Isaiah 58 ends not with abstract poetry but with a specific call to keep the **Sabbath** — "if you call the Sabbath a delight... then you will find your joy in the LORD... and feast on the inheritance of your father Jacob." This mirrors how the Tanakh itself is framed: it *begins* in Genesis 1 with the goodness of creation and the invitation to rest/trust (Sabbath), and in the Jewish ordering it *ends* with 2 Chronicles, which closes on Sabbath (the exile explained as forgetting the invitation to rest). Isaiah does the same — the whole thing comes back to Sabbath. ("The inheritance of your father Jacob" also echoes 3 Isaiah's servant — Israel/Jacob — so the author seems to say: all that suffering led to *this* inheritance.) Marty, following Brueggemann's *Sabbath as Resistance*, suggests that for 21st-century Americans, truly observing a Sabbath — not formulaically, but the heart behind it — may be one of the most countercultural, resistive things we can do.

### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Son of Man.** Daniel is placed in the exilic period for its *content* (the characters and setting are unmistakably Babylonian exile), but Marty argues it was actually *written* much later — as late as the 2nd century BC — using the exile as a backdrop/stage to make a different point. (Jewish tradition does not count Daniel among the *Nevi'im* / Prophets but among the *Ketuvim* / Writings, one of the last books of the Hebrew scriptures composed.) He flags that he'll return to this at the end of Session 2.

**The two names.** The three friends' real Hebrew names are **Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah** — the Babylonians renamed them **Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego** (names praising Babylonian gods) to humiliate and "bully" them in exile. Marty laments that we default to the Babylonian names.

**Two languages.** Daniel is the only biblical book received in two languages. It opens in **Hebrew** (ch. 1), switches to **Aramaic** (the common, "secular" Semitic language — sometimes called Chaldean, not to be confused with Arabic) for chapters 2–7, then returns to Hebrew for the back half. The language itself tells the story: in exile the reader feels "what happened to our Jewishness?" — and the "downhill slide" back into Hebrew signals restoration and hope before a word of content is read.

**The legendary double chiasm.** Each half is a chiasm, and together they form a greater one:
- **Chiasm A** (the Aramaic half): ch. 2 ↔ ch. 7 (kingdoms — a four-part statue; four beasts); ch. 3 ↔ ch. 6 (refusal to worship / furnace ↔ lions' den); ch. 4 ↔ ch. 5 (fall of Nebuchadnezzar ↔ fall of Belshazzar). Its **center is Daniel 4:37** — the evil, prideful Nebuchadnezzar's own lips confessing "everything he does is right, and all his ways are just... those who walk in pride he is able to humble" — the very justice-of-God question the exile is wrestling with.
- **Chiasm B** (the Hebrew half): ch. 8 ↔ ch. 11 (prophecies of beasts / kings); ch. 9 ↔ ch. 10 (trials and forgiveness / trials and mourning). Its **center is Daniel 9:25-27** — the famous "sevens" (seven sevens, sixty-two sevens, the anointed one "put to death" and the sanctuary destroyed).
- **Chiasm C** (the whole book): ch. 1 ↔ ch. 12 (prologue/epilogue), outward pairs mirroring kingdoms, suffering, and the fall of a king, with **chapters 7 and 8 at the dead center** (the two beast-prophecies, one Aramaic, one Hebrew).

**The big idea: Daniel 7:13-14, "one like a son of man."** The center of the whole double chiasm — held almost without exception in rabbinic thought — is the vision of "one like a son of man coming with the clouds of heaven," approaching the Ancient of Days and receiving everlasting, indestructible dominion. The book's message to a suffering people (Babylonian, or later Greco-Roman): *persevere* — your righteousness and faithfulness will not be overlooked, because a day is coming when one like a son of man will stand before God, render judgment, and make everything right. From this swirl Jewish ideas about the **resurrection of the dead** (someone standing before the Ancient of Days to set the world right), and Jesus repeatedly draws on Daniel — which may have been a very *recent* publication in his day (fuller treatment in Session 3).

**On the "sevens" and prophecy.** The 70/7/62 sevens of Daniel 9 have gotten enormous mileage in end-times eschatology (used to "pinpoint" Jesus' birth and death). Marty doesn't deny a Jesus connection on this side of history ("the anointed one will be put to death and will have nothing"), but insists on the reading principle: prophecy must have meant *something* to its original audience, not leave them scratching their heads — which is itself a clue that Daniel was written later, using the exile as its stage, rather than being a straightforward exilic prophecy.

**Apocalyptic, again.** Like Ezekiel, Daniel is apocalyptic — using images and symbols (statues, beasts, sevens) to convey hope to the present. Pride (Nebuchadnezzar exalting himself) is the thing that brings the king down; the kingdom that lasts forever belongs to the one who comes humbly. God's people plod forward — standing strong under suffering, refusing to bow to gods not their own — until the day they might see one like a son of man coming in the clouds.

### Hebrew Words Explained

**The image: Q&A.** Malachi is the last of the prophetic voices (only Esther remains in Session 2, and it is not counted as prophecy). **Malachi** literally means "my messenger" — possibly a title rather than a proper name.

**The context: disillusionment in the remnant.** The returnees' frustrations (seen in Haggai and Zechariah) have boiled over in the decades after the restoration. They came back from Persia dreaming of restoring a glorious kingdom — but God didn't hand back their land and sovereignty. The "turning of the tables" with Persia was really just a *reshuffling* of items on the table: Persia let them go home but they remained under empire. (Brueggemann's *Out of Babylon*: Persia's rule was actually longer, wider, and deeper than Babylon's — less brutal in method but more subtle.) The outright oppression gave way to a *subtler* oppression — cultural servitude that enticed and controlled them from the inside out, robbing them not of possessions but of their *identity as partners of God*. (We should relate: it is harder to fight the subtle, hidden idolatry of Western culture than outright idolatry.)

**Nostalgia and false memory.** The people are losing hope and longing for "the good old days." Marty's reflection (via Rabbi David Fohrman's question): *Do we imagine new tomorrows, or do we lie to ourselves about a better past?* We can't go backward, only forward — and the past usually wasn't as great as we remember. When we believe the lie of a better past, we live more poorly in the present: cynical, jaded, bitter, falling back into old sins. This, Marty suspects, is the spiritual condition Malachi addresses.

**The Q&A structure.** Malachi is written as a *dialogue* between God and his people: God makes an accusation → the people respond with an incredulous question ("How have we...?") → God answers. Examples read:
- **1:6-8** — "A son honors his father... if I am a father, where is the honor due me?" / "How have we shown contempt?" / "By offering defiled food... blind, lame, diseased animals. Try offering them to your governor — would he accept you?" (The irony, Brent notes, given how scrupulous later Jewish history was about spotless sacrifice; here they shrug, "a goat's a goat" — an apathetic worship, like Nadab and Abihu's "common fire.")
- **2:13-14** — "You flood the LORD's altar with tears... why? Because the LORD is the witness between you and the wife of your youth, to whom you have been unfaithful" (echoing Hosea).
- **3:6-15** — "Return to me and I will return to you" / "How are we to return?" / "Will a mere mortal rob God?... in tithes and offerings... Bring the whole tithe into the storehouse... test me in this... and see if I will not throw open the floodgates of heaven."

**The tithe passage in context.** 3:10 is routinely yanked into a *formula* (give → God does X) or prosperity gospel. In context it is the opposite: the people have "spoken arrogantly" — "It is futile to serve God; what do we gain?... the arrogant are blessed, evildoers prosper." That apathetic unbelief is *why* they withhold tithes and obedience. The point is not a transaction but that disbelief in God's promises and an apathetic walk carry real consequences — it doesn't honor God, isn't worship, isn't wholeness. (Brent: for them giving meant hauling goods and animals over distances, so "what good does it do?" bit hard — unlike our frictionless electronic giving.)

**The scroll of remembrance and hope (3:16-18).** Unlike the hopeless prophets whose audiences refuse to get it, in Malachi a **faithful remnant** hears the message: "Then those who feared the LORD talked with each other, and the LORD listened... A scroll of remembrance was written... concerning those who feared the LORD. 'On the day when I act,' says the LORD, 'they will be my treasured possession'" (hearkening back to Exodus and the marriage). God once again finds a *partner* — sometimes just the 7,000 who never bowed to Baal, or the handful who could have saved Sodom, or a few people who put their names on a scroll and set out to become people of the promise.

**Malachi for today.** In a world dominated by debate, bifurcated worldviews, and seeming hopelessness, Malachi shows that a few people who put their names on a scroll and take a stand have the power to change everything.

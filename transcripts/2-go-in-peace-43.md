### Hebrew Words Explained

- **ish gadol** (Hebrew, “great/large man”) — Naaman is Aram's *ish gadol* (2 Kgs 5:1): a weighty greatness you *feel* when he enters a room (Marty's “Ken Griffey Jr. walked behind me” story). *Gadol* = large/great. Related in idea (not etymology) to **kavod** (heaviness → glory).
- **the nameless captive girl** — a traumatized Israelite slave girl *sees* Naaman not as her oppressor but as a man who suffers, and points him to “the prophet in Samaria” — who turns out to be **Elisha** (the disciple Elijah made last episode).
- **good eye / bad eye** — the king of Israel tears his robes, reading a genuine request for help as a threat/quarrel — insecurity's bad eye. Setup for Jesus's teaching (Session 3): “if your eye is good, your body is full of light; if bad, how great the darkness.”
- **the Jordan = a dirty crick** — Elisha doesn't even come to the door (sends a servant) and tells the *ish gadol* to wash *seven times* in the unimpressive Jordan. Naaman rages (his Damascus rivers — Abana, Pharpar — are grander); his servants' *kal va'chomer* (“if he'd asked a *great* thing … how much more ‘wash and be clean'”) persuades him. His flesh is restored “like a young boy.”
- **yada** (Hebrew, “to know” by *experience*) — “Now I **know** there is no god in all the world except in Israel” (5:15): an *enormous* confession from a polytheist — experiential *yada*, not just intellectual assent.
- **gods were geographical** — Naaman asks for two mules' load of *dirt*: to worship Adonai back home he thinks he must stand on Israel's ground (gods ruled by territory). He “gets it” (Adonai alone) yet “doesn't get it” (primitive theology) — and even asks pardon for bowing in the temple of **Rimmon** when his king leans on his arm.
- **“Go in peace” (lech l'shalom)** — Elisha's response to all the bad theology and latent idolatry is not “turn or burn” but *“Go in peace”*: you'll figure it out; relationship with the God who goes *with* you (in Aram, in the temple of Rimmon) is enough. Marty: this lesson gives huge freedom to pastors/parents — when someone is running hard after God in a gray, complex world, “Go in peace.”

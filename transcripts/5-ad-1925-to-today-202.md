### Hebrew Words Explained

**Two streams split (c. 1925).** Fundamentalism and secular humanism were locked in a battle for truth. What happened to the parts of Christendom that *weren't* fundamentalist? They drifted toward **theological liberalism** — which to many felt like "a weak take on humanism with Jesus slapped on the label." The 20th century offered Christians two seemingly bad options — Conservative or Liberal — a bifurcation the church would only later learn to move beyond. Meanwhile academia and textual criticism were learning to ask critical questions of Scripture (the very world BEMA is grounded in), even as Marty's own fundamentalist upbringing instilled a deep, abiding love of Jesus and the place of Scripture for which he's grateful. **BEMA is the eventual re-collision, 100 years later, of these two streams** (critical scholarship + a fundamentalist love of Jesus and the Bible) that had taken separate paths.

**World War II ends the humanist dream.** Humanism was "winning the game of life" and Christianity seemed to lack staying power — until **World War II**. Any hope that humanity could usher in a utopia (fascist, socialist, communist, or democratic) crashed in the face of genocide, communist oppression, and nuclear war: "humanity isn't as great as we thought." This gave Christianity a sudden turnaround and ushered in the **modern evangelical era**: fundamentalism gave way to a broader, softer evangelicalism.

**The culture wars.** Modern evangelicalism planted its flag and set up defenses; we're still in that tension. The culture wars (Brent: "I've boycotted things"; "how dare they take Merry Christmas off my coffee cup") are neither the promised land nor the final Armageddon we often want them to be — at best the awkward phase of an evangelicalism going through "sociological puberty," at worst the "final gasping breaths of a movement coming to an unflattering end."

**The great evangelists.** **Billy Graham** and **Bill Bright** (founder of Campus Crusade for Christ, now **Cru**) gave the church a new language for communicating the Gospel — cultural engagement like never before, with evangelism in the hands of ordinary parishioners, not just clergy. The **Four Spiritual Laws** (whatever one's view of their theological accuracy) let college students, coworkers, and soccer moms articulate the movement of Jesus simply. Marty thinks history may view this era like the printing press or the Reformation. It fed into the **Jesus Movement** of the 1970s — a crashing wave of "welcoming Jesus into my heart" fervor (Brent's mom got saved in 1975).

**The academic stream — Jacob Neusner and the Jewish-Christian reconvergence.** Simultaneously, academia had its own new frontier. **Jacob Neusner** — a Jewish literary scholar (not a believer; academic Jew with Orthodox roots), astonishingly prolific (~950 books written/edited) — *changed the world of hermeneutics*, and **BEMA would not exist without his work.** Studying how modern Christian thought had influenced Jewish thought, he brought (mostly Catholic) Christian scholars to the table, where they relearned lessons from Judaism that had gone missing ~1,800 years earlier — since the Jewish-Gentile schism of ep 193, never repaired. After 1,800 years down a very Gentile stream (councils, reformations, movements), the Jewish and Gentile worlds *collide again* on a scholarly level, and Christians would never again do serious scholarship or archaeology without their Jewish colleagues. (Brent asks about **Vatican II** — 1962-65 — noting most of this work happened after it, and wondering what a new council today, in light of this Jewish interaction, might change.)

**"Why didn't we know this for 1,800 years?"** The simplistic answer: *we hadn't asked* — Christendom had been too worried about doctrinal purity and theological rightness to ask basic questions about the Bible's long-lost Jewish context. Since the turn of the century this scholarship has been reaching the church — e.g. **N.T. Wright** and the **Kingdom theology** movement stand on the shoulders of that conversation. Marty's professors told him new discoveries take 20-30 years to travel from scholarship → seminaries → pastors → pulpits/podcasts; the internet is now compressing that curve dramatically (for good and bad information alike). The **Dead Sea Scrolls** and Neusner are part of why things now change so fast.

**Quantum science and the limits of knowing.** The modern/secular-humanist confidence that we could "figure everything out" was upended by **quantum science** — much of Newtonian physics no longer applied at that level. Combined with the century's sociological realizations, this produced a major worldview shift: humanism took a hit, but secularism is far from dead — and the **post-Christian culture** is perhaps the most shocking shift Christendom has yet to accept (we're still in denial, still unsure how to respond well).

**Postmodernity and the Emerging Church.** In the late 1990s the cultural cry became "What do we truly know anyway?" — absolutes felt less absolute across society, politics, and science. The church railed against perceived **moral relativism**, but the world moved on. Some progressive evangelicals tried to move *with* the change via the **Emerging Church**, which evangelicalism largely rejected ("the spawn of Satan himself," in the rhetoric of the day) but which started conversations that set the stage for needed growth; it has since morphed into something new.

**Today.** With decentralized consumerism (Uber, Airbnb) and social networking reshaping interaction everywhere, it's hard to know what lies around the bend. Is there hope for Christianity's future amid the mess? Marty's answer (the setup for the finale): we find ourselves — as every generation has — *perfectly placed* for this future.

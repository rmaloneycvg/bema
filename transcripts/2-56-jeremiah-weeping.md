### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Weeping.** Jeremiah opens the *Babylonian* prophets (the third of five prophetic periods: Pre-Assyrian, Assyrian, Babylonian, Exilic, Remnant). It is impossible to pick one image for so vast a book, so the review word is **weeping** — Jeremiah is traditionally "the weeping prophet." He laments that God "seduced/wooed" him into a brutal calling ("You deceived me, LORD, and I was deceived"): he must bring a message no one wants to hear.

**Jeremiah's message.** The time for repentance has passed. There will be no second "Hezekiah moment." God's discipline (Babylon) is here and cannot be turned back; the wisest move is to lay down weapons and go peacefully into captivity — far less bloodshed that way. Jeremiah is so "pro-Babylon" in this sense that when Babylon arrives they *leave him behind* rather than deport him. His prophecy spans a huge breadth of time (before, during, and after Babylon's arrival), compiled as many small pieces — and is itself a *re-collection*, since the original scroll was burned and re-dictated to his scribe **Baruch**.

**Covenant relationship is the lens.** Jeremiah sees everything through *broken covenant relationship*, not abstract theology. God is the husband/lover; Israel is the bride ("I remember the devotion of your youth, how as a bride you loved me and followed me through the desert" — the Exodus/Numbers honeymoon imagery of Session 1). The core sin: "They have forsaken me, the spring of living water, and have dug their own cisterns — broken cisterns that cannot hold water" (2:13). The call persists: "Return, faithless people... for I am your husband" (3:14).

**Source A and Source B fused.** Marty retires the running "Source A (idolatry) vs. Source B (injustice)" trick question here, because Jeremiah refuses to let them be separated. Jeremiah 2–3 leans heavily on idolatry ("to wood, 'You are my father,' and to stone, 'You gave me birth'"); Jeremiah 7 (the temple-gate sermon) hammers *false religion* — chanting "the temple of the LORD, the temple of the LORD" while oppressing the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow and shedding innocent blood. Verse 9 deliberately welds the two: idolatry *drives* the injustice. The gods they serve — **Baal** (fertility, commerce, profit) and **Asherah** — care nothing for other people. (Modern parallels: the gods of consumerism, security, comfort — "you buy a bag of chips and nobody else gets chips"; our idolatry comes at others' expense.) Idolatry feeds injustice and injustice feeds idolatry — a catch-22 Deuteronomy tried to reverse by commanding care for the alien, orphan, and widow (what James calls "pure and undefiled religion").

**"A den of robbers" (7:11)** — flagged as extra credit for a later Jesus connection (the temple cleansing).

**Gehenna / the Valley of Hinnom.** Jeremiah 7:30–8:3 and chapter 19 introduce **Tophet** in the **Valley of Ben Hinnom** (Hebrew *Gei Hinnom*), where the people built high places to burn their sons and daughters to Molech — "something I did not command, nor did it enter my mind." God vows to make it the "Valley of Slaughter." This actual valley outside Jerusalem later became the city's trash dump, and in the first century the Greek **gehenna** (from *Gei Hinnom*) is Jesus' driving image for "hell" — not an eternal fiery destination but the utter *desolation* of refusing to be part of what God is doing (a present-tense image; fuller treatment comes in Session 3). The lack of archaeological "proof" of the dump (per Francis Chan) is unsurprising — the valley is now full of houses — but the *biblical* references to exactly what historians describe are right here in Jeremiah.

**The broken jar (ch. 19).** God sends Jeremiah to perform street/guerrilla theater: shatter a potter's jar before the people as a sign — "I will smash this nation and this city just as this potter's jar is smashed and cannot be repaired."

**Seventy years and the land's Sabbath rest (25:1-14 → 2 Chronicles 36:20-21).** Jeremiah announces 70 years of Babylonian captivity, then judgment on Babylon itself — and the ever-present "little bit of hope." Reading very source-A on its own, but 2 Chronicles (written later, "with perspective," at the very end of the Tanakh) reinterprets the exile: the land "enjoyed its Sabbath rest... until the seventy years were completed in fulfillment of the word... by Jeremiah." The deeper issue was *injustice* — failing to keep the Sabbath years, the *shmitah*, and the Jubilee that enforced economic justice and cared for those at the bottom. Chronicles again fuses idolatry and injustice.

## Other Book References and Recommendations

- **2 Chronicles 36:20-21** — the exile reframed as the land receiving its Sabbath rest, fulfilling Jeremiah's 70 years (Chronicles' "later perspective" lens).
- **Deuteronomy** — the command to care for the alien, orphan, and widow, meant to reverse the idolatry–injustice catch-22.
- **Exodus / Numbers** — the bride/desert-honeymoon imagery Jeremiah reuses for God and Israel (Session 1).
- **Leviticus** — the Sabbath years, *shmitah*, and Jubilee behind Chronicles' "Sabbath rest" reading.
- **The Gospels** — "den of robbers" (temple cleansing) and Jesus' *gehenna* teaching, both rooted in Jeremiah (Session 3 preview).
- **Alan Rabinowitz, *The Disciple Scroll*** — a ~300-page historical-fiction (ebook, ~$4) that Marty recommends for grasping Jeremiah's period.
- **Rob Bell, *Love Wins*** and **Francis Chan, *Erasing Hell*** — the two sides of the modern hell debate, both linked for the full conversation.
- **Habakkuk** — noted as another nature-image prophet ("the feet of a deer"); Jeremiah is the prophet most devoted to images from nature.
- Review of images: Amos = plumb line / ripe fruit, Hosea = prostitute, Micah = judge, 1 Isaiah = vineyard, Jonah = potential, Nahum = diyn, Zephaniah = t'shuvah (return/repent), 2 Isaiah = woe, and now Jeremiah = weeping.

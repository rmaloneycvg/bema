### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Judge.** Micah's governing metaphor is God as *judge* — but not a figure in a black robe and powdered wig making abstract pronouncements. The biblical judge is like Deborah or Gideon: one who both *pronounces* the right decision and *enacts* it, bringing the restoration to pass. The focus of judgment is restoration, not abstract indictment.

- **shaphat** (שפט) — the verb "to judge." The three-consonant root (shin–pei–tav) underlies the whole word family. Judging in the biblical world is active: making right what has been wronged.
- **shofet** (שופט) — the noun "judge," the person who does the *shaphat*. The book of Judges is *Shofetim*.
- **mishpat** (משפט) — "justice." Same root with the *mem* prefix meaning "the place of." For Marty, *mishpat* is not merely the pronouncement but the *place/act of restoration* — restorative justice. (Marty notes a friendly disagreement with Elle Grover Fricks over how to translate *tzedakah* and *mishpat*.)
- **tzedakah** (צדקה) — "righteousness," the state of being in right relationship. Together with *mishpat*, it produces *shalom*.
- **diyn** (דין) — the Hebrew word for retributive/punitive justice: the pronouncement of condemnation and guilt. Sometimes a measure of *diyn* is needed to arrive at *mishpat*, but *mishpat* (restoration of shalom) is what God is ultimately after.
- **shalom** (שלום) — the byproduct of righteousness (*tzedakah*) and justice (*mishpat*); the world put back in its proper place.

**Retributive vs. restorative justice:** In an American context, "justice" usually connotes retribution — consequences and comeuppance for the wrongdoer (a punitive system that deters crime by punishment). The biblical/ancient (and many non-Western, e.g. Nez Perce) frameworks lean restorative — making the world right again, putting everything back in its proper place. It is not strictly either/or; the question is which one drives the day. In the world of the Bible, *mishpat* (restoration) drives it.

**Micah 5 and Bethlehem:** The "ruler from Bethlehem Ephrathah" verse is read every Christmas as a prophecy of Jesus. But Micah's first-order meaning is a *Davidic* ruler — David also came out of Bethlehem, was a shepherd (the passage speaks of shepherding the flock), and was the eighth son of seven brothers ("seven shepherds, even eight commanders"). The deliverance in view is from *Assyria*, not a distant Christmas prophecy — though it can be read secondarily in light of Jesus. The point: real strength in God's kingdom comes not from empire and might but from smallness — out of lastness comes firstness.

**Source A / Source B:** Continuing the series' running question — is the prophet focused on *immorality and idolatry* (Source A, the Samuel-Kings lens) or on *empire and injustice* (Source B, the Chronicles lens)? Micah reads as a near-tie: strong Source A notes (idolatry, Samaria's incurable "plague" spreading to Judah) alongside Source B (coveting and seizing fields and houses, defrauding people of their homes). Micah 6:8 — "act justly, love mercy, walk humbly" — is the synthesis: God wants not empty obedience nor correct worship void of justice, but correct worship *full* of justice. The answer to "Source A or Source B?" is **yes**.

**A note of hope:** Unlike the common caricature that "the people never listen," Judah actually *does* heed Micah and is spared from Assyria for a time (though not later from Babylon). Sometimes God's people do listen.

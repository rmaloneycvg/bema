### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Petra.** Obadiah is the shortest prophet in the Tanakh — a single chapter — and a Babylonian-period prophet. Uniquely, it is *not* addressed to Israel or Judah but to **Edom** itself.

**Edom = the brother.** Edom are the descendants of **Esau**, twin brother of **Jacob** — so Edom is the "long-lost brother" of Judah, both children of Abraham. That closer, quasi-covenantal kinship (unlike Philistia, Moab, Ammon, or Phoenicia) is why God holds Edom accountable: "Even from you I expect decent treatment of people, respect for your brother."

**Chutzpah lineage.** Jacob is the biblical figure who introduced the idea of a life built around *chutzpah* (gutsy audacity) — tying back to Habakkuk's chutzpah in the previous episode. God tends to use people with chutzpah.

**Edom → Idumeans → Nabataeans → Petra.** Esau's descendants become Edom; Edom becomes known to history as the **Idumeans**; and the Idumeans are, debatably, synonymous with the **Nabataeans** (often written "Idumean-Nabataeans"). They grew fabulously wealthy working the southern **spice-trade route**. Nomadic for roughly a thousand years, they mysteriously transitioned to building astonishing cities — most famously **Petra** (modern Jordan; the facade from *Indiana Jones: The Last Crusade*, though in reality just a shallow tomb-front). Their rock-cleft fortresses were natural strongholds — approachable only single-file through narrow passages, thought impenetrable, which breeds the *pride* God confronts: "you who live in the clefts of the rocks... who say in your heart, 'Who can bring me down to the ground?'" Even Rome struggled to besiege Idumea (couldn't get water to its armies).

**Future relevance (Session 3 preview):** **Herod the Great** has his roots in the Idumean-Nabataeans — which later explains his wealth and his character.

**The charge: standing aloof and then preying on your brother.** The sin God is furious about is how Edom treated Judah when Babylon came. Marty's Bible-college image: Judah comes fleeing into the rock fortresses, and Edom says "Sorry, we can't help you — keep moving," then points Babylon the way Judah went ("he went that way"). Obadiah's indictment escalates (vv. 10-14): first merely *standing aloof* while strangers carried off Jacob's wealth (itself convicting — it is not okay to do nothing while others suffer); then *gloating* and rejoicing over his misfortune; then *looting* his wealth; and finally *cutting down the fugitives* and handing over the survivors. God cares about people — it upsets him when we fail to treat people well, and it makes him *furious* when we take advantage of people in need.

**Hope (for Jacob, not Esau).** "On Mount Zion will be deliverance... Jacob will possess his inheritance." Jacob will be restored; Esau becomes stubble. The book ends: "Deliverers will go up on Mount Zion to govern the mountains of Esau, and the kingdom will be the LORD's."

**The enduring challenge.** Marty presses that the study of the prophets is not just an academic exercise: whatever our political or regional defaults, the prophets' condemnation challenges each of us to examine how we treat people. (Brent connects it to Jesus' later point that even pagans care for their own — Edom isn't even being a "good pagan," failing to care for its own brother.)

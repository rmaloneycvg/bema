### Jonah 1:1-6 (NET)

**1** The Lord’s message came to Jonah son of Amittai, **2** “Go immediately to Nineveh, that large capital city, and announce judgment against its people because their wickedness has come to my attention.” **3** Instead, Jonah immediately headed off to Tarshish to escape from the commission of the Lord. He traveled to Joppa and found a merchant ship heading to Tarshish. So he paid the fare and went aboard it to go with them to Tarshish, far away from the Lord. **4** But the Lord hurled a powerful wind on the sea. Such a violent tempest arose on the sea that the ship threatened to break up! **5** The sailors were so afraid that each cried out to his own god and they flung the ship’s cargo overboard to make the ship lighter. Jonah, meanwhile, had gone down into the hold below deck, had lain down, and was sound asleep. **6** The ship’s captain approached him and said, “What are you doing asleep? Get up! Cry out to your god! Perhaps your god might take notice of us so that we might not die!”

### Jonah 3:3-9 (NET)

**3** So Jonah went immediately to Nineveh, in keeping with the Lord’s message. Now Nineveh was an enormous city—it required three days to walk through it! **4** Jonah began to enter the city by going one day’s walk, announcing, “At the end of 40 days, Nineveh will be overthrown!” **5** The people of Nineveh believed in God, and they declared a fast and put on sackcloth, from the greatest to the least of them. **6** When the news reached the king of Nineveh, he got up from his throne, took off his royal robe, put on sackcloth, and sat on ashes. **7** He issued a proclamation and said, “In Nineveh, by the decree of the king and his nobles: No human or animal, cattle or sheep, is to taste anything; they must not eat and they must not drink water. **8** Every person and animal must put on sackcloth and must cry earnestly to God, and everyone must turn from their evil way of living and from the violence that they do. **9** Who knows? Perhaps God might be willing to change his mind and relent and turn from his fierce anger so that we might not die.”

### Jonah 4:1-3 (NET)

**1** This displeased Jonah terribly and he became very angry. **2** He prayed to the LORD and said, “Oh, LORD, this is just what I thought would happen when I was in my own country. This is what I tried to prevent by attempting to escape to Tarshish! – because I knew that you are gracious and compassionate, slow to anger and abounding in mercy, and one who relents concerning threatened judgment. **3** So now, LORD, kill me instead, because I would rather die than live!”

### Jonah 4:9-11 (NET)

**9** God said to Jonah, “Are you really so very angry about the little plant?” And he said, “I am as angry as I could possibly be!” **10** The LORD said, “You were upset about this little plant, something for which you have not worked nor did you do anything to make it grow. It grew up overnight and died the next day. **11** Should I not be even more concerned about Nineveh, this enormous city? There are more than one hundred twenty thousand people in it who do not know right from wrong, as well as many animals!”

### Exodus 34:6-7 (NET)

**6** The LORD passed by before him and proclaimed: “The LORD, the LORD, the compassionate and gracious God, slow to anger, and abounding in loyal love and faithfulness, **7** keeping loyal love for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin. But he by no means leaves the guilty unpunished, responding to the transgression of fathers by dealing with children and children’s children, to the third and fourth generation.”

### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Potential → Patient Compassion.** Marty's original image for Jonah was *potential* (Jonah's inability to see the potential God sees in the Ninevites/pagans). Guest Reed Dent reframes it to **patient compassion** — God patiently bearing with both Jonah and Nineveh; waiting patiently for the wicked to learn repentance and for the righteous to learn compassion.

- **hineni** (הנני) — "Here I am." The classic prophetic response to God's call (Samuel, 1 Samuel 3): not merely "I'm present" but "I'm fully attentive to whatever you have to say." The prophetic pattern is "the word of the LORD came to [name]" → the prophet presents himself. Jonah breaks the pattern entirely: God calls, and Jonah *flees* toward Tarshish — the text says "from the LORD" twice in two verses.
- **emet** (אמת) — "truth / faithfulness / loyalty": being true and loyal to someone. It is the root behind **Amittai** (אמתי), Jonah's father's name — so Jonah is literally "son of truth / son of faithfulness." The irony is biting: the son of faithfulness refuses to grant God the attribute of faithfulness.

**The misquoted Name (Jonah 4:2 vs. Exodus 34:6-7).** In his rage Jonah hurls God's own self-revelation back at him as an insult: "I knew that you are a gracious and compassionate God, slow to anger and abounding in love." This quotes the Name God proclaimed to Moses in the cleft of the rock (Exodus 34). But Jonah *omits* "faithfulness" (*emet*) and substitutes "a God who relents from sending calamity." The son of *emet* strips *emet* from God — saying, in effect: "I know what it takes to be true and loyal, and you don't have it, because if you are for *them* (my worst enemy), you cannot be for me too." The unstoppable force of God's love meets the immovable object of Jonah's "truth."

**Five "problems" in the text (reading it as the Easterners would, not resolving them away):**
1. **The call is refused.** Every other prophet presents himself (even if he contends with God); Jonah alone just runs. Why? Nineveh is the capital of Assyria — brutal conquerors who impaled/"staked" the heads of those they conquered. Jonah doesn't merely fear them; he hates them.
2. **The message is pathetic.** In Hebrew Jonah's entire sermon is five words ("Forty more days and Nineveh will be overthrown"), delivered one day into a three-day city, with no call to repent and no hint of hope — compared to the thousands of words of Amos, Isaiah, Hosea. Jonah is not bringing his best self to work.
3. **The wrong people believe.** The pagan sailors ("Maybe he will take notice of us") and the Assyrian king ("Who knows? God may yet relent... with compassion") trust God — the king even puts animals in sackcloth. Animals don't repent, yet the most brutal empire on earth earnestly repents, while God's own spokesman works against God.
4. **The misquotation of Exodus 34** (above).
5. **No ending.** The book stops mid-scene on a question. God grows a plant for shade, sends a worm to kill it, and when Jonah grieves the plant more than the city, God asks: "Should I not have concern for the great city of Nineveh, in which there are more than 120,000 people who cannot tell their right hand from their left, and also many animals?" Curtain. The question hangs in the air, turned from Jonah to face the audience — to you.

**The real point.** The showpiece is not the fish (familiarity breeds Fohrman's "lullaby effect"). It is that God longs to be merciful to our worst enemies. "They cannot tell their right hand from their left" — evil somewhere lives in ignorance (Brent links this to Jesus on the cross: "Father, forgive them, they do not know what they are doing"). The unanswered closing question: *What do you hope for your worst enemy — and does God have your permission to be merciful to them?* "His anger is for a moment, but his steadfast love endures forever"; Jonah would rather his anger be forever.

**Jonah sits to the east** of the city — the direction judgment comes from — still hoping, like Elijah watching for the small cloud, that fire might rain down after all (Brent: "stoking the flames").

## Other Book References and Recommendations

- **Exodus 34:6-7** — the LORD's self-revelation to Moses in the cleft of the rock ("compassionate and gracious... slow to anger, abounding in love and *faithfulness*"), which Jonah quotes but truncates in 4:2.
- **1 Samuel 3** — the calling of Samuel and *hineni*, the model prophetic response Jonah refuses.
- **Amos, Isaiah, Hosea** — their soaring prophetic oratory ("let justice roll down like waters"; "on this mountain God will swallow up death forever"; "I will allure her... and speak tenderly") contrasted with Jonah's five-word sermon.
- **1 Kings 18 (Elijah)** — watching the horizon for the small cloud; Brent's parallel for Jonah waiting on the east to see judgment.
- **Luke 23:34** — "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing"; linked to "cannot tell their right hand from their left."
- **Marty Solomon & Reed Dent, *The Gospel of Being Human*** — contains a chapter on Jonah (penned mostly by Reed).
- **Original BEMA Jonah episode (v1.0)** — Marty's teaching on the book as a *chiasm*, drawing on David Fohrman (worth the extra listen; presentation and Fohrman links in the show notes).
- **VeggieTales / Phil Vischer** — the famous *Jonah* adaptation ("tales of the veggie variety").
- **Quartet of the Vulnerable** — orphan, widow, foreigner, and the needy; the recurring object of the pre-Assyrian prophets' warnings.
- Review framing: Torah's one word is *partnership* (Genesis = choosing a partner, Exodus = rescuing/marrying, Leviticus = defining, Numbers = shaping, Deuteronomy = remembering); then the redemption cycle; then wisdom ("not what is true but when it's true"); then the prophets as "warnings, woes, and hope."

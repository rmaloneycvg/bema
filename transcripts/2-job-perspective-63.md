### Hebrew Words Explained

**The image: Perspective.** Placed (debatably) as an exilic-period book, Job is read here for its center — chapter 28 — and its word for review is **perspective**.

**Observation 1 — Job is a drama.** Read it like a play, with assigned parts — not necessarily denying a true tale beneath it. The *midrash* even names a real Job, and (as a tangent) says his wife was **Dinah** of Genesis (whose own trauma after Shechem would explain her bitter "evil eye" / glass-half-empty outlook), and that Job was one of the three magicians in Pharaoh's court alongside **Jethro** (Moses' father-in-law) and **Balaam**. In Jewish tradition Job is a *pagan* — nowhere does the text say he is Jewish — which unsettles many.

**Observation 2 — sitting shiva.** Job's friends initially do the right thing: they *sit shiva* (שבעה), the Jewish practice of being present with a griever in silence, not speaking unless spoken to — not consoling, not explaining, just being there for the suffering. (The whole rest of the book then becomes a drama of doing it *wrong*.)

**Observation 3 — dating / genre (Marty holds all three at once).**
- Many conservatives call Job one of the *oldest* stories in the Bible (echoes of very old Hebrew prose, roughly Abraham's era) — fitting, since humanity has wrestled with suffering since Cain and Abel, even Adam and Eve.
- Yet some content is *too late* for that: the zodiac references (Pleiades, Orion), the beast-language, and especially **Satan** as a personified character wandering heaven and wagering with God — which doesn't appear until roughly the Hellenistic period, almost a millennium later.
- Many Jewish thinkers class Job as *wisdom literature* (Marty agrees, which is why he skipped it earlier). His personal hunch: an ancient tale, perhaps reshaped by someone like Solomon into wisdom literature, then *re-told again* in the Babylonian exile (the natural "go-to" story when suffering) with an added Hellenistic-flavored intro/outro about Satan — much as our culture endlessly remakes stories (Robin Hood, Spider-Man) and updates them for a new audience. Early or late, Marty receives it as inspired, authoritative scripture either way.

**Observation 4 — Job is a chiasm of chiasms.** Not merely a chiasm, nor merely a double chiasm like Daniel, but chiasms within chiasms down to each **pericope** (an extract/section of text — pronounced per-ICK-o-pee). A Japanese Bible scholar ran the scriptures through a computer algorithm detecting parallelisms and inverted parallelisms; the Job breakdown (explore the 1-1, and the 3-1/3-2/3-3) is mind-blowing — the sub-chiasms seem to be in question-and-answer conversation with one another, all building toward the center.

**The center: Job 28.** The whole chapter is a poem (in Job's voice): humans have mined incredible things from the earth — silver, gold, iron, copper, lapis lazuli, sapphires — cutting shafts into untouched places, turning rock inside out. "But where can **wisdom** be found? Where does understanding dwell?" It cannot be mined, bought with the gold of Ophir, or weighed in silver. "God understands the way to it... for he views the ends of the earth and sees everything under the heavens... And he said to the human race, 'The fear of the LORD — that is wisdom, and to shun evil is understanding.'"

**Why it matters.** Like Lamentations (lament with a nugget of hope at its chiastic center), Job is a whole book about *suffering* with this chapter at its center: in the midst of suffering, what we really lack is **wisdom — perspective**. True wisdom belongs to God alone; only he can unearth it. God can mine wisdom from the hills of suffering as easily as humans mine minerals from the mountains.

**No explanation at the end.** After 40+ chapters, Job gets no answer — God shows up but never gives the backstory. That is the point: suffering often comes with no explanation. The *reader* alone is let in on the ridiculous heavenly "wager" between God and Satan that set it all off (a setup so strange we forget how absurd it is). The author invites us to consider the infinite, even foolish-seeming, possibilities that could lie behind our suffering — that God sees what we never could. Even in the midst of suffering, we are invited to **trust the story**.

### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Strength.** Ezekiel opens the **exilic** period (after Pre-Assyrian, Assyrian, and Babylonian). The people now sit in exile in Babylon — temple destroyed, homes gutted — and ask new questions: Will we be here forever? What did we do to deserve this? Has God forsaken us? Is God even real? The exilic prophets shift from a voice of *woe* to a voice of *hope*. The time for warnings and preventive repentance is gone; to return to God's path the people will need to *overcome* their circumstances — they will need **strength**.

- **chazak** (חזק) — "to be strong / to strengthen." The name **Ezekiel** is *Chazak-iel* — "God strengthens (you)." (Marty named his son Ezekiel.) The root appeared in the Exodus "hardening" of Pharaoh's heart (alongside *kavod*, "to make heavy") and in Joshua's *chazak va'amatz* — "be strong and courageous." The name has a dual sense: Ezekiel will *need* strength for a hard calling, and his *message* is strength — he is sent to strengthen a people tempted to despair.

**Apocalyptic literature.** Ezekiel introduces a new prophetic form. Marty's careful definition (repeated for emphasis): *apocalyptic literature uses images and symbols to convey hope to their present day.* It is **not** a crystal ball about the end times. The word "apocalyptic" means "to uncover / to reveal" — pulling back the curtain to show what is *actually* happening *now*, a present (not future) implication. (Enormous damage has been done to our understanding of prophecy and eschatology by misreading how apocalyptic works in the ancient Eastern world.) Four Old Testament books are apocalyptic, including Ezekiel; the fuller treatment comes in Zechariah, and it returns in Daniel and Revelation.

**Guerrilla theater (Ezekiel 4).** Ezekiel is less an author than a *thespian*: he performs prophetic street theater, enacting a message and leaving the audience gaping. In chapter 4 God tells him to build a model of Jerusalem (Marty: like Legos), set his cut hair on it and burn it, and hack the model with a sword (Jerusalem will be destroyed) — and to cook his food over human excrement; Ezekiel bargains God down to *animal* dung. Being a prophet is rarely fun work.

**Ezekiel 1 — the chariot/throne vision.** The famous opening: four living creatures, each with four faces (human, lion, ox, eagle) and wings, moving straight ahead without turning; wheels intersecting wheels, rims "high and awesome" and full of eyes; and above a vault of crystal a throne of lapis lazuli with a figure like glowing metal and fire — "the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD." Reading it with wide eyes for the *big ideas* rather than every detail:
- **Four** — the four directions/corners of the earth: the most expansive, inclusive language. The creatures see in every direction.
- **Eyes all over** — nothing goes unseen; God sees everything.
- **Wheels / movement** — an entire paragraph on *motion*. Unlike other throne-room visions (e.g. Isaiah), movement dominates. The glory of God is *mobile* — "this God has wheels... rims high and awesome" — and can go anywhere.

**The glory of the LORD moves east — with the exiles.** The people believed God was located in the tabernacle/temple; now the temple is destroyed. But in Ezekiel 10 the glory of the LORD *departs the temple and heads east* — the direction of Babylon. The implication: **God went with you into exile.** He was not trapped or destroyed when the temple fell. And in the closing chapters, as the people return, God's presence comes back *from the east* with them — an apocalyptic vision (like Revelation's new Jerusalem) of a new home and new temple, not a literal architectural blueprint. This is Ezekiel's message of strength: God has not deserted or forsaken you; God has wheels, and his glory goes where his people go.

**Chapters 16–17** offer a poetic (not exhaustive) *explanation* of why the people were disciplined — their unfaithfulness — giving just enough understanding to strengthen them.

**The takeaway.** Ezekiel strengthens through encouragement: press forward, learn from mistakes, hold the promises, believe things are bigger than present circumstances, and help put the world back together. He uses "the mind of an innovator, the creativity of an artist, and the imagination of a storyteller." The very stubbornness that made Israel a "stiff-necked people" in the desert is what can get them through — endure, overcome, be strong.

**Brent's parallel:** the eastward/westward movement echoes the Tower of Babel — Babel/Babylon, a tower built vs. a temple rebuilt; God consistently calling his people back from the east. God knows where he wants his people — and goes with them.

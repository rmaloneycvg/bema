### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Servant.** 3 Isaiah — the *exilic* voice, chapters **40-54** — is the **servant discourse**. (Marty, following Walter Brueggemann's *Out of Babylon*, now distinguishes a fourth Isaiah too; 4 Isaiah comes next episode.) After the vineyard of 1 Isaiah and the woes of 2 Isaiah, the tone turns to *comfort*: "Comfort, comfort my people... speak tenderly to Jerusalem... A voice of one crying in the wilderness, 'Prepare the way of the LORD'" (40:1-5).

**The servant is Israel/Jacob.** The famous "suffering servant" of Isaiah 53 does not stand in a vacuum — it is the climax of a long discourse that repeatedly, explicitly names the servant:
- **41:8-10** — "But you, Israel, my *servant*, Jacob, whom I have chosen, you descendants of Abraham my friend... you are my servant; I have chosen you and have not rejected you."
- **43:10** — "You are my witnesses, declares the LORD, and my *servant* whom I have chosen."
- **44:1-2** — "Now listen, Jacob, my *servant*, Israel, whom I have chosen... Do not be afraid, Jacob my servant, Yeshurun whom I have chosen."
The singular "he/servant" is the metaphor for God's whole people. God reaches back to Torah — whose one-word summary is **partner** — and says: I was looking for a partner, and I found you; you are my servant.

**Reading Isaiah 53 in context.** The common Christian claim — "no Jew could read Isaiah 53 and not see Jesus" — fails because Jews read it *in context*, as the peak of the 40-54 servant discourse. Its first-order meaning: *this* is why you sit in exile. God was seeking a partner; 1 and 2 Isaiah already named the sin. Now the servant (Israel) is "despised and rejected... a man of suffering," "pierced for our transgressions... crushed for our iniquities" — taken to Babylon and crushed — but *because the servant perseveres and suffers and comes out the other side, "by his wounds we are healed."* The exiles must endure, so that their children and the generations after them will be healed. (Brent notes the Jewish communal reading of scripture: "*we* were in Egypt," "*we* did it" — the servant and the "we" are the same people across generations. The grammar — "he" pierced for "our" transgressions — reads like an out-of-body observer looking back, which some use to argue a later date for this voice.)

**The crucial correction: Jesus points to the Tanakh, not the reverse.** Christians usually say "everything in the Old Testament points to Jesus," which (to a Jewish ear, offensively) implies the whole story was pointless without him. Brent reframes it, and Marty affirms: *Jesus lived his life to portray perfectly what God had been doing all along.* So it is not that the Tanakh points to Jesus; it is that **Jesus points back to the Tanakh** — "pick any passage, any book, and I'll live out its word (logos) perfectly." Jesus is indeed the fulfillment of Isaiah 53, and on this side of history we rightly see him there — but not because Isaiah was foreseeing a rabbi named Jesus; rather because Jesus brought beautiful clarity to what the servant was always called to be.

**The sharpest point: Isaiah 53 is an invitation, not an exemption.** We often read it as "Jesus did it so I don't have to." That is the exact *opposite* of its meaning. Jesus suffered not so we wouldn't have to, but to *show us how* to suffer on behalf of others so that others might find healing (Marty gestures ahead to Hebrews in Session 4). Isaiah 53 is therefore not merely about one rabbi 2,000 years ago — it is a passage God's people before and after Jesus are invited to *partake in*: sometimes you are called to be a servant, even a suffering servant, so that others can find hope (which is where 4 Isaiah goes next).

**Pastoral note.** Our individualistic, Greek-shaped thinking makes it hard to enter the story as a participant rather than hold it at arm's length as an abstract observer; and it is genuinely hard, after years of seeing only Jesus in Isaiah 53, to also see its first meaning. Don't beat yourself up — it takes years to change how you think.

### Hebrew Words Explained

**From imperial political state to imperial religious state.** After the councils and the fall of Rome, the empire shifts from the political state of the Caesars to the "Holy Roman Empire" — which Marty insists on calling an *imperial religious state* (Christendom): emperors give way to popes, bishops become civic leaders. (A caveat: the ancient world knew no separation of church and state — Rome was always deeply religious/pagan — so this isn't wholly new; the point is the shift from Rome to Christendom.)

**The Byzantine Empire — bright moments and dark ones.** The new kingdom (the **Byzantine Empire**) had genuine high points, but also dark problems. **Justinian** expanded its rule to its largest extent. This era pairs Christian faith with the imperial *sword*: people forced to affirm creeds and doctrines, offered conversion, and — if they refused — persecuted, sanctioned, or executed. Christianity's anti-Semitic streak continued; in large corners of the empire Jews who wouldn't affirm the Trinity were beaten or killed. "How things change when you find yourself at the handle end of the sword" — the story has flipped from being at the *point* of the sword to holding the *handle*.

**Orthodoxy vs. orthopraxy.** Having cut itself off from its Jewish heritage — and a *practice* that had defined God's people for centuries — and having spent over a century perfecting **orthodoxy** (right *belief*), the movement now, out of necessity, had to ask about **orthopraxy** (right *practice*). But Gentile Christians didn't follow Torah — set free from the *miqsat ma'ase haTorah* (the "works of the law"): no kosher, no tassels, not the 613 commandments — so the movement was "missing the playbook" on how to walk the path of faith.

**Augustine's anti-Semitic edict.** Augustine — "father of the Christian faith" — penned a seven-point document forbidding African Christians to even entertain Jewish relationships: no doing business with Jews, no lighting Sabbath candles, and a command to eat **ham on Easter** (an anti-Semitic statement against Judaism — that's the origin of the Easter-ham custom). The opposite of Session 1's hospitable Abram and the Gospel of the *mamzer*.

**The monastic movements shine.** Though monasticism began much earlier (strong already in Constantine's day), it found its golden moment here. Often flippantly dismissed as secluded/isolated — the same critique leveled at the **Essenes**, and with real similarities — we owe much of what is good in Christian faith to the monastics' faithfulness: preserving the physical texts (like the Essenes), works of service, sustaining hospitals/clinics now in Jesus' name, and devotion. Crucially, *they are the source of what we know about creating space for God* — the spiritual disciplines and practices of the Session 1 "creating space" episode come from the monks. Thinkers like **Gregory of Nyssa** and **Basil the Great** corrected the isolationist tendency with teaching on community.

**Gregory the Great and liturgy (7th c.).** **Gregory the Great**, pope and "father of medieval spirituality," gave Christendom its first large-scale taste of **liturgy** — church *order*, the shape of worship and practice (orthopraxy made concrete), a very Western/Greek, Roman-flavored structuring of worship — including **Gregorian chant**. Much of what endures in the Catholic mass (and Brent's own Latin-heavy Catholic upbringing) traces here.

**The double edge.** The same church order that preserved good also *systematized* the chronic power struggle and corruption that had been ruining the Christian trajectory — giving infrastructure and efficiency to chronic abuses. (Marty is emphatic: none of this is shade at Catholicism — at this point Christianity and Catholicism are simply the same thing, and he has deep appreciation for what his Catholic brothers and sisters have taught him.)

**Hope: create space and God fills it.** Session 1's principle — *if we create space, God will fill it* — came with no prerequisites: no need to understand God perfectly first. So even a period that may have *largely misunderstood* God still created space, and Marty believes God filled it in spite of itself. As the Tanakh repeats, there is always a **remnant** — always some who follow the Creator with all their heart, soul, and mind — and God is always looking for **partners**, be they Jews, Christians, or pagans. Still, Christians keep finding new things to argue about, which leads into the next chapter.

**A balanced read.** History can be held under overly rosy or overly critical lenses. This era brought real gifts (note the positives) and real failures (it did little to undo the imperial abuses, and systematized some). "What you look for, you will find" — and there are good stories happening everywhere; the world is bigger than "how we jacked up Christianity with the councils."

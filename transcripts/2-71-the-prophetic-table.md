### Hebrew Words Explained

**A capstone, not a book study.** This near-final Session 2 episode presents no single passage; it is a synthesis drawn from Walter Brueggemann's *Out of Babylon*. The core move: the Bible — and the prophets especially — is not one monolithic voice but an *ongoing dialogue* of differing perspectives that readers must wrestle with.

**The Babylonian table.** Imagine an ancient table set during the Babylonian exile; the prophets we studied enter, bless the meal (*Baruch atah Adonai Eloheinu melech ha'olam ha-motzi lechem min ha-aretz*), and debate: "What do we do about Babylon?" They answer differently:
- **Isaiah** (esp. 3 Isaiah / the suffering-servant discourse): *stand and suffer.* Stand publicly for God's way and justice against empire; if we suffer well in Babylon, we come out on the winning side.
- **Ezekiel, Habakkuk, Job:** *exile is home now.* Don't fight an uphill battle; the Babylonians aren't right, but we're here for a while — learn to live here well.
- **Jeremiah, Zephaniah:** *restore the relationship with God.* Zephaniah's **t'shuva** (repent/return); Jeremiah's broken-and-restored covenant. Don't fixate on staying/leaving/fighting — just get right with God and let him handle the rest.

**Source A / Source B, revisited.** This mirrors the earlier Session 2 point about **Samuel–Kings (Source A: agenda-driven headlines)** vs. **Chronicles (Source B: the documentary, looking-back perspective)** — *neither is "right"*; both are accurate, differing perspectives, and the text holds the tension in ongoing dialogue. The prophets do the same.

**A Jewish way of reading.** Not "God said it, I believe it, that settles it" — but "God said it, and now we all have to wrestle with it." (Peter Rollins's parable: two rabbis argue daily; God descends to settle it, and both rabbis unite to tell God to get out and let them keep arguing — because the power is in the ongoing wrestling match.) Come to the Text with open hands every time; the Bible is not a single road to decipher but the story of God's people learning to walk the *many* roads that different circumstances provide.

**The Persian table (a century-plus later).** Clear the table; a new era. Enter **Ezra, Nehemiah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi, Esther, and Daniel.** The question shifts — Persia isn't a brutal captor like Babylon (it's "the new Persian order," not even called exile): *What do we do about Persia?*
- **Ezra, Nehemiah, Haggai, Zechariah** (the largest group) — **return and rebuild/restore**: go home, rebuild God's house, the walls, Jerusalem. Hard, but worth it. (Marty's running gag: Nehemiah slamming the table while pastor-Ezra tries to calm him down.)
- **Esther and Daniel** — **stay and shrewdly subvert.** Don't abandon Persia by going home; stay to *engage, influence,* and bring shalom-order to imperial chaos — not assimilation but "honor the king with a shrewd subversion" (Daniel still prays and refuses to bow; Esther's shrewd wisdom). (Marty notes Daniel, placed earlier in the exilic period, was likely written much later — "God is my judge" — about the Greco-Roman world after Hanukkah/the Hasmonean revolt, a close parallel to Persia, so he seats him at the Persian table.)
- **Malachi** — (like Jeremiah before, but sharper on *obedience*): it doesn't matter if you stay or go — just **obey God**: don't rob him, dishonor him, or give blemished offerings.

**Brueggemann's thesis for today.** We 21st-century Americans don't sit in Babylonian *exile*; we live under a Persian-style order / a *Pax Romana* of our own. So the table we must wrestle with is the **Persian** one — and the voice to embrace in our context is the **Esther–Daniel voice of "accommodation and resistance"**: accommodate the surrounding world while, "shrewd as serpents and innocent as doves," subverting and resisting its imperial narrative. (Brueggemann wrote this long before any contemporary "resistance" politics — it is not a partisan agenda.)

**But no single voice is universally "right."** Context decides which narrative to embrace. Marty imagines persecuted believers in Egypt or Libya (churches burned, violence) rightly embracing the **Ezra–Nehemiah return-and-rebuild** narrative or Zechariah's **apocalyptic hope**; and believers elsewhere embracing Malachi's **obedience** narrative. In *our* unique context, accommodation-and-resistance fits.

**The takeaway.** We are *not* called to read the Bible merely to extract the one right propositional answer. Scripture is a living, God-breathed (inspired) dialogue in which God's people continually wrestle — to this day — with what faithful response the mission of God demands in *their* context. Since Torah's one word is **partner**, the question is always: as partners in God's mission, what does that mission demand of us here? Engaging the Text as ongoing dialogue (rather than only exegeting a propositional answer) makes scripture a compelling, empowering guide for how to live, decide, and join God's mission. A fitting way to close Session 2.

- **The whole Session 2 prophetic cast** — Isaiah (1–4), Jeremiah, Zephaniah, Ezekiel, Habakkuk, Job (Babylonian table); Ezra, Nehemiah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi, Esther, Daniel (Persian table).

- Review theme: the images/words of every prophet, and the arc of warning → woe → hope across the pre-Assyrian, Assyrian, Babylonian, exilic, and remnant periods.

### Hebrew Words Explained

**The prophet's image: Woe.** This episode wraps up the Assyrian period, flying through the second voice of Isaiah — chapters 13–39 (with ch. 12 as a possible hinge). The word for 2 Isaiah is **woe** (not "whoa," but W-O-E — the prophetic cry of lament/judgment). 2 Isaiah is a relentless slog of woes against nation after nation.

**The prophets are compiled, not continuous.** A key reading principle: do *not* read Isaiah as one long essay Isaiah sat down and wrote. The "major prophets" are not single discourses meant to be read straight through — they are little chunks, poems, and mini-sermons (Marty's image: "prophetic tweets") uttered over years in different settings, later compiled and arranged. This explains the jarring shifts in tone (woe → praise → woe) and apparent redundancies (Assyria condemned, then Damascus; Babylon condemned twice).

**The long litany of woes.** Chapters 13–23 pronounce judgment on Babylon (13–14), Assyria, Philistia, Moab (15–16), Damascus/Syria (17), Cush (18), Egypt (19–20), Babylon again (21), Edom and Arabia — and, crucially, God's own people (22). Chapter 24 is a "capstone" of the LORD's devastation of the whole earth. Marty's mental image: God looking out over the earth, frustrated that *nobody cares about other people* — "you don't care about others, and you don't care about others, even my people don't care about others" — so "woe, and woe, and woe." God wants a partner to help put the world back together, and no one — pagan or covenant people — is doing it.

**"Lucifer" is a translation artifact (Isaiah 14).** The whole taunt of chapter 14 is explicitly directed (v.3) at *the king of Babylon*. The famous "How you have fallen from heaven, O Morning Star, son of the dawn" (14:12) is not originally about Satan. **Lucifer** is simply the *Latin* word for "Morning Star" (light-bringer) used to render the Hebrew. The grandiose boasts ("I will ascend to heaven... make myself like the Most High") were standard ancient royal self-exaltation — every nation had its sacred mountain where the gods dwelt (Greeks: Olympus; also Tyre, Babylon, Persia). The lesson: be careful not to build an elaborate backstory for Satan out of passages (Isaiah 14, Ezekiel 28) that are stated to be about human kings. Translate and contextualize honestly.

**Ariel (29:1).** "Woe to you, Ariel" — *Ariel* means "lion," and the Hebrew for "altar" sounds like Ariel, so it alludes to Jerusalem (the city of David).

**Islands of hope.** Amid the woe, hope erupts — abruptly, because the pieces are compiled. Isaiah 25 is a sudden burst of praise: "a refuge for the poor... a shelter from the storm and a shade from the heat... On this mountain the LORD will prepare a feast of rich food for all peoples... he will swallow up death forever... wipe away the tears from all faces" — followed immediately (and jarringly) by Moab trampled in the manure. Chapters 26–27 continue in praise and deliverance; chapter 32 promises "a king will reign in righteousness" (shelter from wind, refuge from storm — Session 1 desert imagery) but warns the complacent that it is short-lived; chapter 35 ends the section with "the joy of the redeemed."

**The redactor's bridge (chs. 36–39).** The clearest evidence of multiple voices/compilation: chapters 36–39 abruptly shift from poetry to *prose history* (much of it reading like it was lifted from 2 Kings), narrating Sennacherib and the Assyrian crisis. Most scholars see a redactor bridging the gap between the first section and the exilic "3 Isaiah" that resumes in poetry at chapter 40. (This could still be Isaiah shifting into "historian mode" for those who prefer a single author — the point is simply to read honestly what the text is doing.)

**Source A / Source B.** The litany is driven by how people treat one another — if it were only about idolatry, the condemnation would simply be "you chose the wrong god." Instead it is woe for pride, oppression, exploitation of the poor and needy, and failure to care for others. God's own people are not exempt ("woe to Ephraim and Judah," "woe to David's city," "woe to the obstinate children... who go down to Egypt" — don't put your trust in Empire).

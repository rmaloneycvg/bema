# Where We Actually Disagree

**A shared map for a conversation at Discovery Church, Broomfield**

---

## Why I wrote this

I've spent a long time studying this question, mostly through the BEMA Discipleship material and the texts it sent me back to. I could have written you a document designed to win. I've read enough of those to know they don't work, and I don't think winning is what I want.

So this is the other kind of document. It lays out where I think **you are strongest**, where I think **I am strongest**, and where I think **neither of us can settle it from the text**. Then it tells you exactly what I'm asking for, and why I think the ask is smaller than it sounds.

I'd rather be understood than agreed with, and I'd rather be corrected than deferred to.

## Where I stand, with no ball hidden

I hold an affirming view. I'm telling you that at the top rather than letting you discover it later, because I've seen what happens to trust when someone leads with process and reveals a conviction three meetings in.

**I am not asking Discovery to adopt my view.** I don't think it would be honest for me to ask that, and I don't think a church should change a conviction because one member pushed. What I'm asking for is in §6, and it doesn't require you to move an inch theologically.

I also want to say this plainly: **I think your position is held in good faith by people who love Scripture and love people.** I don't think it's bigotry with a proof text. I've watched this church care for people at real cost. That's why I'm bringing this here instead of leaving quietly, which was the other option available to me.

## 1. What I think you have right

These are the places where I think the traditional reading is genuinely strong. I'm not listing them as setup. I'm listing them because if I can't state your case at its best, I have no business asking you to consider mine.

### 1.1 The distinction between ethical law and identity markers

This is, I think, your strongest argument — and I only understood it properly by studying the BEMA material I'm citing.

Second-Temple Judaism divided Torah three ways: **cultic law** (needs a temple; fulfilled in Jesus), **ethical law** (universally true — "the way God made the universe to function"), and *miqsat ma'asay haTorah*, the identity markers that made Israel distinct — circumcision, kosher, tassels, mixed fabrics.

Acts 15 loosed the **identity markers**. It did not loose ethics. And the textual proof is right there: James dropped circumcision and kosher while **explicitly keeping** *porneia*, sexual immorality (Acts 15:20, 29).

So when I point to Acts 15 as a precedent for loosening a boundary, you can fairly answer: *that council loosened the category your argument doesn't apply to, and kept the category it does.* That's a good answer. I've revised my own writing because of it (see §7).

### 1.2 Paul's warrant in Romans 1 is creational, not just cultural

I argue that Romans 1 is describing Hellenistic idolatry, pederasty, and a Roman dominance ethic — which I do think is true, and which I think matters. But you can grant all of that and still say: **Paul's reasoning appeals to creation, not to local custom.** "Since the creation of the world" (1:20). "Created things rather than the Creator" (1:25). *Para physin* (1:26).

Practices are local. Warrants generalize. If Paul grounds his argument in how things were made rather than in what Rome happened to be doing, then the argument reaches past Rome. I think that's a fair reading of the structure of the passage, and I don't have a knockdown answer to it.

### 1.3 Jesus grounds marriage in Genesis

Matthew 19:4–6. Jesus, asked about divorce, goes to Genesis 1 and 2. I don't get to say "Jesus never addressed this." He addressed marriage, and he addressed it by reaching for the creation account.

### 1.4 Two thousand years of near-unanimous reading

This is the argument I've given the least weight to and probably should give the most. The church has read these texts one way for almost its entire history, across cultures with radically different sexual mores, including cultures far more permissive than ours. That's not nothing. A reading that emerges in the last few decades, in one corner of the world, in the direction that corner's culture happens to be moving, bears a real burden of proof.

I think there are answers. I also think anyone who waves this away isn't being serious.

### 1.5 Harm does not establish error

John 6:66 — "many of his disciples turned back and no longer followed him." People left Jesus. That people have left Discovery over this does not, by itself, show the position is wrong. If the gospel never cost anyone anything, it wouldn't be the gospel.

I won't argue that departures prove you wrong. I'll argue something narrower in §2.5, but I want to concede this cleanly first, because I think the "look how many people left" argument is lazy and I don't want to make it.

### 1.6 Costly discipleship isn't a unique burden

Christianity asks hard things of everyone. It asks singleness of some, fidelity in difficult marriages of others, the surrender of money, ambition, autonomy. Celibacy is not a punishment invented for one group; it's a form of life the church has honored for two millennia. The claim "this is too hard to be what God asks" is not, on its own, a Christian argument.

## 2. What I think I have right

These are claims, not conclusions. I hold them, I think they're well-grounded, and I expect pushback on each.

### 2.1 Romans 1 is not describing what our position statement describes

The sexual world Paul's readers inhabited was organized around **dominance and penetration** — who penetrates whom — not around orientation or mutuality. Pederasty was close to status quo among middle-class Romans: a boy's sexual use by an older man was part of how he was formed into a man. Roman law reportedly forbade a woman from being on top. Sex was about status.

The concept of **sexual orientation does not exist in the text.** There's no word for it and no category for it. A durable, largely unchosen disposition toward the same sex is a nineteenth-century idea. Whatever Paul is condemning, he is not condemning something he had a concept of.

So my claim is narrow but I think it holds: Romans 1 is addressing idolatry, exploitation, and domination. Our position statement addresses covenanted, mutual partnership between consenting adults. **Those are different subjects.** I'm not saying Paul would have approved of the second. I'm saying he isn't discussing it, and a formal institutional boundary is a lot of weight to rest on a passage that isn't discussing it.

And then Romans 2:1 — "You, therefore, have no excuse, **you who pass judgment on someone else**." Whatever else chapter 1 is doing, Paul's next move is to turn it on the reader who reached for it. I don't think that's incidental.

### 2.2 Scripture's stated indictment of Sodom is not sexual

When God summarizes Sodom's sin himself, this is what he says:

> "Now this was the sin of your sister Sodom: **She and her daughters were arrogant, overfed and unconcerned. They did not help the poor and needy.** They were haughty and did detestable things before me." — Ezekiel 16:49–50

Jude 7 and 2 Peter 2 do touch sexuality, and Genesis 19 is its own text. I'm not claiming sexuality is absent from the tradition. I'm claiming that the **summary indictment God gives is economic and hospitality-based, and it comes first** — the general phrase follows the named specifics rather than replacing them.

And the part that stops me every time: the whole oracle is addressed to Jerusalem. "Son of man, confront Jerusalem" (16:2). God is telling his own devout people that their sins make Sodom look righteous by comparison. If we use Ezekiel 16 in this conversation, I think we have to use it pointed the direction Ezekiel pointed it.

### 2.3 Scripture revised an anatomy-based exclusion of a sexual minority — inside its own pages

This is the thing I most want you to sit with, because it isn't an argument from culture. It's an argument from the canon.

| | Text | Basis for belonging |
|---|---|---|
| **Excluded** | Deuteronomy 23:1 — no emasculated man in the assembly | anatomy |
| **Reversed** | Isaiah 56:3–5 — "let no eunuch say, 'I am only a dry tree'... I will give them, **within my temple and its walls**, a memorial and a name better than sons and daughters" | Sabbath-keeping, covenant fidelity |
| **Enacted** | Acts 8:36 — "Look, here is water. **What can stand in the way of my being baptized?**" | nothing recorded |

Philip knew Deuteronomy 23:1. He was a man who knew his text well enough to find Isaiah on a scroll in a moving chariot. He quoted **Isaiah** instead. Luke records no obstacle, no condition, no examination.

I'm not claiming this settles same-sex marriage. I'm claiming it's the closest thing Scripture gives us to a **worked example**: what happens when a written, anatomy-keyed exclusion of a sexual minority meets a person in whom the Spirit is evidently at work. And the example runs in one direction.

### 2.4 "Homosexuality is sin," predicated of a condition, is a recent sentence

Before the late 1800s, the category was **acts**. "Homosexual" is coined in 1869. The shift from *act* to *kind of person* is a modern move, and our position statement is framed in the grammar of that move — it predicates sin of an orientation, not only of a behavior.

I'm not saying "the culture has advanced past the Bible." I'm saying the specific *sentence* we've published isn't ancient. It's about 120 years old, and it's built on a category the biblical authors didn't have. That's worth noticing about a statement we treat as the historic position.

### 2.5 Right interpretation plus wrong walk is not faithfulness

This is where I'd push back on the harm question — not "people left, therefore you're wrong," but something else.

In rabbinic usage, to **fulfill** Torah means to interpret it correctly *and* walk it out correctly. Fail at either and you're **abolishing** it, not fulfilling it. That's the frame behind Matthew 5:17–20, and it means the walk is a test of the interpretation, not a separate department.

Which is why I can't fully accept the position/posture distinction, even though I understand why Posture Shift draws it. If our position is right and our walk is producing exiles, the framework doesn't let us call that "good position, imperfect posture." It asks us to re-examine the interpretation.

And I'd add the passage that Posture Shift itself quotes. "Come to me, all you who are weary and burdened" (Matthew 11:28) sits four verses after Jesus pronounces woes on Chorazin, Bethsaida, and Capernaum — the **most doctrinally rigorous, most sincerely devout** communities in Galilee. They weren't hypocrites. They believed, they knew the text, they walked what they believed. The charge was that their devotion didn't make them care for the people on the outside. The weary in Matthew 11 are weary *from religious burden*. I don't think that verse can be a warrant for softening a boundary marker, because in context it's relief from one.

### 2.6 We've formalized the contested thing and left the clear thing informal

Leviticus 19:9–10, 23:22 — don't harvest the corners of your field; leave them for the alien, the orphan, the widow. Repeated, unambiguous, never qualified. Acts 2 ends with the church selling property to meet need.

We have a published position on the contested, culturally-embedded, lexically-disputed question. We have none on the one Scripture states flatly and repeatedly. I don't think that asymmetry is a conspiracy. I think it's what happens when a question becomes a cultural identity marker. But I do think it's worth naming.

## 3. What I don't think either of us can settle from the text

I want to be honest about the limits, including mine.

**Whether *para physin* generalizes.** You read Paul's creational warrant as reaching past Rome. I read the "exchange" language in 1:23 and 1:25 as making idolatry the subject and the sexual material the symptom. Both are readings of the same structure. I don't think either of us can prove it.

**Whether celibacy is a "reachable condition."** My argument from Isaiah 56 is that God set conditions the eunuch could actually meet *while remaining a eunuch* — so conditions a person can't meet while remaining who they are reproduce Deuteronomy 23:1 with extra steps. Your answer is that celibacy *is* reachable, and has been honored by the church for two thousand years. That's a serious answer. I find it insufficient; I can't demonstrate that it's wrong.

**Whether the trajectory is complete or continuing.** I read Deuteronomy → Isaiah → Acts 8 as a finished movement I'm pointing at. You can fairly ask what stops the same logic from going anywhere it likes. My answer is that the brake is the canon — I'm not extrapolating, I'm reading to the end. You may not find that brake sufficient.

**What *arsenokoitēs* means.** I've heard the claim that "homosexual" only enters English Bibles in 1946. I've since concluded that's overstated — the KJV's 1611 "abusers of themselves with mankind" shows the referent was read into it long before the word existed. What I think is true and narrower: the term is rare, possibly coined by Paul, and its precise referent is genuinely disputed among people who read Greek professionally.

**Where I might simply be wrong.** I'm a person with a stake in this, formed by a particular moment, reading texts I love in a way that happens to relieve a tension I feel. I don't think that invalidates my reading. I do think it should make me slower than I'm naturally inclined to be.

## 4. The place I'd ask us to look at ourselves

I offer this as an observation, not a gotcha, and I might have the facts wrong about Discovery specifically.

Jesus speaks about **divorce and remarriage** directly, repeatedly, and in stronger terms than anything in Scripture addresses same-sex relationships (Matthew 5:31–32, 19:3–9; Mark 10:2–12; Luke 16:18). Most evangelical churches — including, I believe, this one — have quietly arrived at a pastoral practice considerably gentler than those words read plainly. We remarry divorced people. We let them lead. We don't publish a position statement on it.

I don't think that's hypocrisy. I think it's the church doing something appropriate: weighing plain words against pastoral reality, considering the exploitation the original teaching was protecting people from, and landing somewhere the text doesn't spell out.

**That's the thing I'm asking for.** Not a novel method. The method we already use on the question Jesus addressed most directly.

## 5. The one thing I think the text does settle

Not the sexual ethics question — I've said I don't think it settles that. What I think it settles is **how a community handles a question like this.**

Acts 15 is a recorded instance of a church with two irreconcilable factions, both convinced they were defending Scripture, both with real arguments. They convened. They argued. Peter spoke. Barnabas and Paul testified. James — leader of the *more conservative* wing — stood up and spoke for the outcome, while still binding what he thought had to be bound.

The letter they wrote says something I find remarkable: "**It seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us**" (15:28). Not "the Spirit revealed." *It seemed good.* The Spirit as a party in a discussion.

The framework behind that is binding and loosing (Matthew 16:19). Some things God has said and they're settled. A great many things are gray, and God left the application to the community. Whatever the community binds or looses, God goes with.

Two features of it matter here, and one of them cuts against me:

**It cuts against me:** the tradition expects the dissenter to submit and **stay**. Not leave, not write angry letters, not go find another church. Keep wrestling inside. I accept that, and §8 is my commitment to it.

**It cuts toward my ask:** binding presumes the community actually **deliberated**, and that the minority kept its standing. Acts 15's losing side remained full members. When Paul and Barnabas split over Mark right afterward — which they did, badly — the story ends with Paul restoring him (2 Timothy 4:11).

So I'd ask, genuinely, not rhetorically: **when was this deliberated here? Who was in the room? Were any gay members of this church present?** If the statement was adopted from a network or drafted by staff, then whatever it is, it isn't a binding in the sense the tradition means — and the deliberation is still available to us.
